## 1. Skin Cancer in Skin of Color

Skin cancer in patients with skin of color is diagnosed at **later stages** and carries **higher mortality rates** than in White patients.

- 52% of Black patients and 26% of Hispanic patients present with **advanced-stage melanoma**, vs. 16% of White patients
- Mortality from **squamous cell carcinoma** is disproportionately high in people of color (18-29% estimates)
- 5-year melanoma survival rate: **65% in Black patients** vs. **91% in White patients**
- SCC occurs **8.5x more often in non-sun-exposed areas** (lower extremity, anogenital) in Black patients
- **Acral lentiginous melanoma** is rare overall but represents a disproportionate percentage of melanomas in darker-skinned individuals; often detected late

**Contributing factors to disparities:**
- Inadequate medical education (47% of dermatologists reported insufficient training for skin conditions in Black patients)
- Only 4-18% of images in major dermatologic textbooks show diseases in skin of color
- People with darker skin are significantly underrepresented in clinical trials
- Dermatology is the **second least diverse specialty** in medicine

## 2. Nonmelanoma Skin Cancer (NMSC)

**NMSC** includes **squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)** and **basal cell carcinoma (BCC)**.

- Most common form of cancer in the US: ~5 million cases diagnosed annually
- 1 in 2 men and 1 in 3 women will develop NMSC in their lifetime
- Diagnosis and treatment of NMSC increased **77%** between 1994 and 2014
- ~90% are associated with exposure to **UV radiation**

## 3. Actinic Keratosis (AK)

Also called **"precancer"** or **solar keratosis**. A premalignant growth representing chronic UV damage to the skin.

### Clinical Features
- Presents in the **4th and 5th decade**; frequency increases with age and cumulative sun exposure
- More common in fair-skinned individuals
- **Small (<1 cm) erythematous, scaly, rough papule or plaque**; may have a **cutaneous horn** (hard, cone-shaped growth)
- Clue on exam: **gritty texture** (better felt than seen)
- Distribution: **sun-exposed areas** (face, neck, scalp, upper chest, arms, dorsal hands)

### Natural History
- ~30% involute spontaneously
- ~1-5% undergo malignant transformation
- ~60% of SCCs arise from AKs

### Histopathology

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Atypical keratinocytes** | Limited to the **lower third of the epidermis** (basal layer) |
| **Acanthosis** | Variable thickening of the epidermis; "budding" of atypical cells |
| **Alternating orthokeratosis and parakeratosis** | Orthokeratosis = normal basket-weave stratum corneum (no nuclei); parakeratosis = retained nuclei in stratum corneum |
| **Solar elastosis** | In the underlying dermis; marker of chronic sun damage |
| **Basement membrane** | Intact (no invasion) |

> Key distinction: AK has atypical keratinocytes in the **lower 1/3** of the epidermis only.

## 4. Squamous Cell Carcinoma In Situ (SCCIS) and Invasive SCC

### SCCIS (Bowen's Disease)

Also called **Bowen's disease**; when on genital mucosa, called **erythroplasia of Queyrat**.

**Clinical:** Erythematous, scaly, crusted plaque; few mm to many cm. May mimic eczema or psoriasis but does **not** improve with topical steroids.

**In skin of color:** Presents as scaly, **hyperkeratotic pigmented or black plaques**, papules, or nodules (**pigmented SCCIS**); more common in Black and Asian patients.

**Pathogenesis:** Chronic sun exposure (most common), psoralens, mustard gas, chronic arsenic toxicity, **HPV**

**Transformation:** 3-8% rate of progression to invasive SCC; higher metastatic rate once invasion occurs.

**Histopathology:**
- **Atypical keratinocytes involving ALL levels of the epidermis** ("full-thickness atypia") — key distinction from AK
- Overlying parakeratosis
- No sparing of skin appendages
- No breach of the basement membrane

---

### Invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

**Epidemiology:**
- **Second most common** skin cancer overall; **most common skin cancer in Black patients**
- ~20% of all skin cancers; more common in males; middle-aged to elderly

**Risk factors:** Chronic sun exposure > immunosuppression (organ transplant) > chronic inflammation (chronic wounds) > tanning bed use > arsenic exposure > HPV (penile/anal SCC)

**Clinical:** Hyperkeratotic, skin-colored papule, nodule, or plaque with **variable erythema**; locally destructive with metastatic potential

**Histopathology:**
- **Nests of atypical squamous cells arising from the epidermis and invading the dermis** — key distinction from SCCIS
- Evidence of **squamous differentiation**: keratinization and intercellular bridges
- **Keratin pearls** (keratinized concentric layers of abnormal squamous cells) — highly characteristic
- Nuclear pleomorphism, cytologic atypia
- Vascular and perineural invasion (in aggressive tumors)

**Prognosis:**
- Metastasis rate: 0.5-5.2% overall; **10x increased risk in Black patients**
- Risk factors for metastasis: location (ear, lip, scar, anogenital), size, depth, perineural/lymphovascular invasion, desmoplastic features

## 5. Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

**Most common malignancy in humans** (>4.3 million/year in US; 4x more common than SCC).

- Most common skin cancer in **White, Asian, and Hispanic** patients (SCC is most common in Black patients)
- Cumulative lifetime risk: 1 in 6
- Men > women; typically >40 years
- 40% of patients with BCC will have another within 10 years
- Predisposing factors: **intermittent** intense UV exposure (sunburns, tanning) > chronic UV; radiation; genetic syndromes (Basal Cell Nevus Syndrome, xeroderma pigmentosum, albinism)

### BCC Subtypes

| Subtype | Clinical Appearance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nodular** | Pearly, translucent papule with **telangiectasias** and rolled border; may ulcerate | Most common subtype |
| **Morpheaform/Infiltrative** | Shiny, scar-like depressed plaque; ill-defined | Aggressive; associated with larger surgical defects |
| **Superficial** | Dry, scaly, flat, erythematous papule or plaque | Most common on trunk/extremities |
| **Pigmented** | Any subtype with melanin pigment | More common in skin of color |

> "Rodent ulcer" is an old term for BCC due to its tendency to form ulcers with ragged edges.

### Histopathology

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Basaloid cells** | Increased nuclear:cytoplasmic ratio = "blue tumor nodules" |
| **Peripheral palisading** | Cells at the edge of nests align in a row — key diagnostic feature |
| **Cleft formation** | Between tumor nodules and surrounding stroma (retraction artifact) |
| **Mucinous stroma** | Tumor stroma often contains mucin |
| **Attachment to epidermis** | At least some tumor nodules are attached to the undersurface of the epidermis |
| **Mitoses** | Present; individual cell necrosis |

### Prognosis
- Locally aggressive and destructive behavior
- **Very low metastatic potential (<0.1%)** — contrast with SCC
- Recurrence rate depends on subtype

## 6. Treatment of Nonmelanoma Skin Cancer

### Actinic Keratoses and SCCIS

| Treatment | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen)** | #1 treatment for AK; typically two rounds |
| **Topical 5-fluorouracil** | Topical chemotherapy |
| **Imiquimod** | Topical immunomodulator |
| **Photodynamic therapy** | Light-activated treatment |
| **Shave excision** | Surgical option |
| **Electrodesiccation and curettage** | Scraping and burning |
| Chemical peels, laser resurfacing | Less common |

Patients with several AKs may need skin checks **every 6 months to 1 year** (marker of significant sun damage).

### SCC and BCC

Treatment depends on **size, location, and depth of invasion**.

| Treatment | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Electrodesiccation and curettage** | For smaller, low-risk lesions |
| **Surgical excision** | Standard; margins drawn circumferentially around the cancer |
| **Mohs micrographic surgery** | Skin-preserving; used for cosmetically sensitive areas and high-risk cancers; tissue removed in thin layers until all margins are clear |
| **Radiation** | Adjunct for non-surgical candidates |

> **Keratoacanthomas** are a variant of SCC that grow rapidly and may spontaneously regress, but surgical removal is still highly recommended.

## 7. Benign Melanocytic Nevi

**Common acquired melanocytic nevi** are benign "moles." Prevalence is related to age, race, genetics, and early sun exposure. Average number: ~20 nevi by age 20.

### Clinical Subtypes (Stages of Maturation)

| Type | Clinical Appearance | Location of Melanocytes |
|---|---|---|
| **Junctional nevus** | Round, flat, dark brown/black macule (<1 cm) | At the dermal-epidermal junction |
| **Compound nevus** | Raised, light tan to dark brown papule | Junction AND dermis |
| **Intradermal nevus** | Papillomatous, skin-colored to brown papule | Dermis only (loses pigment) |

Melanocytes are derived from the **neural crest**.

### Histopathology
- **Nests of melanocytes** along the dermal-epidermal junction and/or dermis
- **Well-circumscribed and symmetrical**
- **Architectural and cytologic maturation** with increasing depth: melanocytes get **smaller** as they travel deeper into the dermis (key feature distinguishing benign from malignant)

---

## 8. Dysplastic Melanocytic Nevi

Also called **"atypical nevi."** Intermediate lesion between common acquired nevi and malignant melanoma.

**Clinical features:**
- 0.5-1.0 cm or larger; prevalence 7-20%
- Usually on back, chest, abdomen, arms
- **Larger, more variegated in color, asymmetric, irregular borders** — distinguished from common nevi
- May be sporadic or familial

**Significance:**
- Marker of **increased melanoma risk**
- May represent precursor lesions to melanoma
- **Familial atypical multiple mole melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome**: complex inheritance pattern with patient and relatives having increased atypical nevi and melanomas; risk of melanoma development approaches 100% in some high-risk patients

**Histopathology:** Cytologic and architectural atypia (unlike the orderly appearance of benign nevi)

## 9. Malignant Melanoma

The most common **fatal cutaneous disorder**; accounts for <5% of skin cancer cases but >75% of skin cancer deaths.

### Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
- ~112,000 new invasive cases estimated in the US in 2026; ~8,510 deaths
- Incidence has doubled in the US over the last four decades
- Equal incidence in males and females; females have better prognosis
- Up to **85% of cases result from UV exposure** (sunlight); risk doubles with >5 blistering sunburns
- **60% have activating mutations in BRAF** (most commonly **V600E**), a gene that activates RAS signaling

### Risk Factors for Cutaneous Melanoma

| Risk Factor | Relative Risk |
|---|---|
| Changing pigmented lesion | Very high |
| **FAMMM + prior melanoma** | **500** |
| FAMMM, no prior melanoma | 150 |
| Numerous common nevi | 5-65 |
| Atypical nevi | 7-20 |
| Previous melanoma | 9 |
| Fair skin, red/blonde hair | 2-7 |
| **Blistering sunburns** | 2-4 |
| Tendency to freckle | 2-4 |
| Asian, Hispanic, Black | 0.08-0.15 |

> Key: **Changing pigmented lesion** and **FAMMM with prior melanoma** are the top risk factors to know.

### ABCDE Criteria for Clinical Recognition

| Letter | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **A** | Asymmetry |
| **B** | Border irregularity |
| **C** | Color variegation (dark black) |
| **D** | Diameter >0.6 cm |
| **E** | Evolution (changing over time) |

### Subtypes

| Subtype | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Superficial spreading** | Most common; thin, irregularly pigmented plaque with multiple colors |
| **Nodular** | Ulcerated, exophytic dark nodule |
| **Lentigo maligna** | On sun-damaged skin; irregular pigmented plaque |
| **Acral lentiginous** | Least common overall; occurs on **palms, soles, around nails**; same incidence across races but disproportionate in darker-skinned individuals; often detected late |

### Histopathology
- **Haphazardly placed** intraepidermal melanocytic nests (contrast with orderly nests in benign nevi)
- **Pagetoid upward spread** (melanocytes spreading upward through epidermis) or **lentiginous spread** (along dermal-epidermal junction)
- **Cytologic atypia**
- **Lack of dermal maturation**: melanocytes remain large at the base (do not get smaller with depth — opposite of benign nevi)
- **Dermal mitotic figures** — key indicator of malignancy
- Poor lateral circumscription

### Staging and Prognosis (AJCC 2018)

The most important prognostic factors:
1. **Tumor thickness (mm)** — most important factor; deeper = worse survival
2. **Ulceration** — presence worsens prognosis
3. **Sentinel lymph node metastasis** — lymph node involvement and visceral spread worsen prognosis

- 5-year survival for regional/distant stage: 63% / 20%, respectively

### Treatment

| Stage | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Melanoma in situ | Surgical excision with **0.5 cm margins** |
| Tumors 1-2 mm depth | Surgical excision with **1 cm margins** |
| Tumors >2 mm depth | Surgical excision with **2 cm margins** |
| Stage T1b and above | **Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)** |
| SLNB positive | Lymph node dissection |
| Metastatic / Stage III unresectable | Adjuvant targeted therapy or **immunotherapy** |

> Tumor depth is the most important factor determining surgical margins.

## 10. Mycosis Fungoides

**Mycosis fungoides (MF)** is a **cutaneous T-cell lymphoma** — a neoplastic proliferation of **CD4+ T-cells** that home to the skin.

### Clinical Features
- Middle-aged to older individuals
- **Predilection for buttocks and breasts** ("bathing suit" distribution)
- Slowly progressive; patch stage may last months to years
- Progression: **patches → plaques → tumors**
- Often evolves from long-standing therapy-resistant chronic dermatitis
- Rapid downhill course once lymph node, hematogenous, or visceral involvement occurs

**Presentation by stage:**
- **Patch stage:** Slightly scaly, erythematous patches; may appear hypopigmented (especially in darker skin), hyperpigmented, or red
- **Plaque stage:** Thickened, indurated plaques
- **Tumor stage:** Exophytic nodules; may ulcerate

**Sezary syndrome:** The leukemic form of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma — malignant cells circulating in the bloodstream with erythroderma and lymphadenopathy.

### Histopathology

| Stage | Histologic Features |
|---|---|
| Patch and plaque | **Epidermotropism**: atypical lymphocytes infiltrating the epidermis **without causing spongiosis**; band-like dermal infiltrate of atypical lymphocytes in the superficial dermis |
| Tumor stage | Diffuse dermal infiltrate of monomorphous atypical cells |

**Sezary cells:** Hyperchromatic lymphoid cells with **hyperconvoluted ("cerebriform") nuclear membranes** — seen in Sezary syndrome.

> Key: **Epidermotropism** (atypical lymphocytes in the epidermis without spongiosis) is the defining histologic feature of mycosis fungoides.

## Summary Table: Key Histopathologic Distinctions

| Lesion | Key Histologic Feature |
|---|---|
| Actinic keratosis | Atypical keratinocytes in **lower 1/3** of epidermis; basement membrane intact |
| SCCIS | Atypical keratinocytes at **all levels** of epidermis ("full-thickness atypia"); no invasion |
| Invasive SCC | Atypical keratinocytes **invading the dermis**; keratin pearls |
| BCC | Basaloid nests with **peripheral palisading** and clefting from stroma |
| Benign nevus | Small, well-circumscribed nests; melanocytes **mature (shrink)** with depth |
| Melanoma | Haphazard nests; **lack of maturation**; pagetoid spread; dermal mitoses |
| Mycosis fungoides | **Epidermotropism** (atypical lymphocytes in epidermis without spongiosis) |
