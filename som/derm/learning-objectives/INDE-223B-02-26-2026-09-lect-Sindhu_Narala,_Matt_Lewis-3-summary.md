## Objective 1: Basic Skin Histopathology — Desmosome and Hemidesmosome

The skin is organized in layers: epidermis (including the stratum corneum at the top), dermis (papillary and reticular), and subcutaneous tissue. The **basement membrane** (also called the **dermal-epidermal junction**, or DEJ) separates the epidermis from the dermis.

Keratinocytes in the epidermis are held together by two types of adhesion structures:

| Structure | Function | Disease when targeted |
|---|---|---|
| **Desmosome** | Adheres keratinocytes to each other | Pemphigus group |
| **Hemidesmosome** | Adheres keratinocytes to the basement membrane | Pemphigoid group |

- Desmosomes are composed of **desmoglein**, desmocollin, desmoplakin, and plakoglobin — think of them as mortar holding bricks together
- Hemidesmosomes anchor the bottom of the epidermis to the DEJ

### Classification of Bullous Diseases by Level of Split

| Intraepidermal (split within epidermis) | Subepidermal (split below epidermis, at DEJ) |
|---|---|
| Pemphigus vulgaris | Bullous pemphigoid |
| Pemphigus foliaceus | Mucous membrane pemphigoid |
| Paraneoplastic pemphigus | Pemphigoid gestationis |
| IgA pemphigus | Lichen planus pemphigoides |
| | Linear IgA bullous dermatosis |
| | Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita |
| | Dermatitis herpetiformis |
| | Bullous systemic lupus |

## Objective 2: Clinical Morphologies of Bullous Dermatoses

Blisters are classified by how they look:

- **Flaccid blisters** — thin-roofed, easily rupture, leaving erosions; seen in pemphigus (intraepidermal split = thin roof)
- **Tense blisters** — thick-roofed, dome-shaped, harder to rupture; seen in bullous pemphigoid (subepidermal split = full epidermis as roof)

## Objective 3: Diagnostic Tests for Autoimmune Blistering Conditions

### Biopsy Sites
- **Routine H&E histology**: biopsy of the lesion itself (the blister edge)
- **Direct immunofluorescence (DIF)**: biopsy of **intact perilesional skin** (next to but not in the lesion) — this is the **gold standard** for all autoimmune bullous diseases
  - For dermatitis herpetiformis, biopsy further from the lesion (uninvolved skin nearby)

### Direct Immunofluorescence (DIF)
Fluorophore-labeled antibodies are applied to the biopsy tissue. Where abnormal antibody/complement deposits exist, fluorescence is visible under microscopy. The **pattern** distinguishes diseases:

| Disease | DIF Pattern |
|---|---|
| Pemphigus vulgaris/foliaceus | Intercellular IgG and C3 (honeycomb/fishnet pattern within epidermis) |
| Paraneoplastic pemphigus | Intercellular IgG + linear IgG at the BMZ |
| Bullous pemphigoid | Linear IgG and C3 at the basement membrane zone (BMZ) |
| Pemphigoid gestationis | Linear C3 at the DEJ |
| Mucous membrane pemphigoid | Linear IgG and C3 at the BMZ |
| Dermatitis herpetiformis | Granular IgA in the dermal papillae |

### Supportive Tests
- **H&E biopsy of lesional skin**: shows the level of split and inflammatory infiltrate
- **ELISA**: blood test for circulating autoantibodies to specific target proteins (e.g., DSG3, DSG1, BP180, BP230, TG3)
- **Indirect immunofluorescence (IIF)**: detects circulating autoantibodies in serum

## Objective 4: Pemphigus Vulgaris

### Pathogenesis
- Antibodies (IgG) target **desmosomal cadherins**: **Desmoglein 3 (DSG3)** (present in mucosa) and **Desmoglein 1 (DSG1)** (present in skin)
- Loss of desmosomal adhesion causes keratinocytes to separate from each other (**acantholysis**), producing an intraepidermal split

### Clinical Presentation
- Age 40-60, M=F; enriched in Jewish, Mediterranean, Indian descent
- **Mucosal type**: antibodies only to DSG3 → oral erosions only
- **Mucocutaneous type**: antibodies to DSG3 + DSG1 → oral erosions + flaccid skin blisters and widespread erosions
- 50-90% have oral mucosal involvement; oral lesions often precede skin lesions
- **Nikolsky sign**: lateral friction on perilesional skin induces a new blister (also seen in SJS, Staph Scalded Skin)
- **Asboe-Hansen sign**: gentle pressure on an intact blister causes fluid to spread laterally under the skin
- Natural history: 25% resolve by 2 years, 50% by 5 years, 75% by 10 years

### Diagnosis
- **Gold standard**: DIF of intact perilesional skin — shows **intercellular IgG and C3** (honeycomb pattern within the epidermis)
- Supportive: H&E biopsy (suprabasal acantholysis), ELISA for DSG3 and DSG1 antibodies

### Treatment
- **Prednisone** 1 mg/kg with slow taper
- **Rituximab** (Rituxan): FDA-approved 2018; given to essentially everyone who needs prednisone; superior to Cellcept for inducing remission
- Steroid-sparing agents: Cellcept (mycophenolate mofetil), azathioprine (preferred in pregnancy), MTX, dapsone, IVIG
- Plasma exchange if severe/refractory

## Objective 5: Pemphigus Foliaceus

### Pathogenesis
- Antibodies to **DSG1 only** (DSG1 is expressed superficially in skin and throughout the epidermis, but is absent in mucosa — so no mucosal disease)
- Very superficial split (subcorneal level), so blisters rupture immediately and are rarely seen intact

### Clinical Presentation
- Age 40-60, M=F
- **Seborrheic distribution**: scalp, face, chest, upper back — no mucosal involvement
- Characteristic **"cornflake-like scale"** — usually only crusts and scales are seen, rarely intact blisters
- Can look like impetiginized dermatitis
- Less likely to be systemically ill than pemphigus vulgaris
- **Pemphigus erythematosus**: localized subtype involving seborrheic areas of the face only
- Can mimic bullous impetigo or Staph Scalded Skin (both cleave DSG1 via bacterial exfoliative toxin)

### Fogo Selvagem (Endemic Pemphigus Foliaceus)
- Meaning "Wild Fire" in Portuguese; found in rural Brazil and other regions (Colombia, El Salvador, Paraguay, Peru, Tunisia)
- Caused by **molecular mimicry**: antibodies made against black fly (*Simulium* species) saliva cross-react with DSG1
- Higher prevalence near rivers (where black flies breed); up to 3% prevalence in indigenous Brazilian areas vs. 1/100,000 in the US
- Younger age of onset (10-30 years), generally milder; HLA-DRB1 alleles confer risk
- Treatment: prednisone (similar to sporadic pemphigus foliaceus)

### Diagnosis and Treatment
- DIF: same intercellular IgG and C3 pattern as PV (but more superficial)
- ELISA for DSG1
- Treatment: rituximab, prednisone (same approach as PV)

## Objective 6 & 7: Paraneoplastic Pemphigus (PNP) and Its Link to Malignancy

### Link to Malignancy
PNP is triggered by underlying tumors. The tumor expresses antigens that stimulate an immune response; this response cross-reacts with skin and mucosal proteins (**epitope spreading**).

**Associated malignancies (in order of frequency):**
1. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (most common)
2. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)
3. Castleman's disease
4. Thymoma (benign or malignant)
5. Sarcomas, Waldenstrom's

### Pathogenesis
- Both **humoral** and **cellular** immune responses drive disease
- Lichenoid inflammation → antigen exposure → humoral immune response → epitope spreading
- Autoantibodies target: **DSG3, DSG1**, the **"-plakins"** (desmoplakin, envoplakin, periplakin, plectin), **BP230**, **alpha-2-macroglobulin-like-1 (A2ML1)**
- 1/3 can develop myasthenia gravis (especially with thymoma)
- **Bronchiolitis obliterans (BO)**: CD8 T-cell rich bronchiolitis infiltrates; more common in lichenoid PNP variant — this is the main cause of death

### Clinical Presentation
- **Horrific stomatitis** crossing the vermilion lip (skin and mucosa both involved) — distinguishes from PV
- Polymorphic skin findings: palms and soles commonly involved (unlike PV)
- 5 clinical variants: PV-like, BP-like, EM-like, LP-like, morbilliform/lichenoid (GvHD-like)
- Also: severe pseudomembranous conjunctivitis, esophageal/nasopharyngeal/genital mucosal lesions

### Diagnosis
- DIF: **intercellular IgG + linear IgG at the BMZ** (combined pattern)
- Serologic workup for underlying malignancy is mandatory

### Prognosis
- Mortality 50-80%; most die within 1 year of diagnosis
- Risk factors for death: envoplakin antibodies, TEN-like or EM-like or BP-like morphology, older age, BSA involvement >18%
- Causes of death: **bronchiolitis obliterans (#1)**, infection (#2), malignancy (#3)

### Treatment
- **Treat the tumor ASAP** (coordinate with oncology)
- **Treat bronchiolitis obliterans** (coordinate with pulmonology): add T-cell agent (Cytoxan, cyclosporine, Cellcept) if BO present — BO is distinct from GvHD
- Prednisone 1-2 mg/kg
- Rituximab (lymphoma dosing: 375 mg/m2 weekly x 4 weeks)
- IVIG 1-2 g/kg/month
- PLEX (plasmapheresis) if high autoantibody titers
- Prophylaxis required: PJP, Zoster, Candida

## Objective 8: Bullous Pemphigoid

### Pathogenesis
- Most common autoimmune blistering disease (~1/10,000 in US)
- Antibodies target **BP180 (BPAG2)** and **BP230 (BPAG1)**, components of **hemidesmosomes**
- Antibodies disrupt dermal-epidermal cohesion → subepidermal split → tense blisters

### Clinical Presentation
- Older adults, typically 70s-80s
- **Very itchy** — often presents with urticarial plaques before blisters appear
  - 20% present in urticarial (pre-bullous) phase, 80% in bullous phase
- **Tense blisters** on erythematous or normal skin
- Mucosal involvement in ~30% (much less than pemphigus)
- Palmoplantar involvement suggests a **drug-induced cause**
- **Drug triggers to ask about**: DPP4 inhibitors (gliptins), furosemide, ACE inhibitors, TNF inhibitors, amoxicillin, COVID vaccine, immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab)
- Natural history: resolves in 5-7 years on average

### Diagnosis
- **Lesional H&E**: subepidermal split with eosinophils and neutrophils
- **Perilesional DIF**: **linear IgG and C3 at the basement membrane zone** — key distinguishing feature from pemphigus
- ELISA for BP180 and BP230 antibodies
- Mimickers to be aware of:
  - Low positive ELISA results can occur in eczema and lichen planus
  - Paraneoplastic BP does not respond well to Dupixent
  - Scabies can mimic BP with positive DIF

### Treatment
- **Topical steroids**: clobetasol 0.05% or betamethasone diproprionate (class 1 steroid)
- **Oral prednisone** 0.5-1 mg/kg/day to clear blisters
- Steroid-sparing agents:
  - **Dupilumab (Dupixent)**: FDA-approved June 2025; given to almost all patients with BP
  - Omalizumab (Xolair)
  - Doxycycline and niacinamide
  - Mycophenolate mofetil
  - IVIG
  - Rituximab (if needed)

## Objective 9: Pemphigoid Variants — Pemphigoid Gestationis and Mucous Membrane Pemphigoid

### Pemphigoid Gestationis
- Bullous pemphigoid occurring **in pregnancy**; rare, 1:50,000 pregnancies
- Usually presents in the **3rd trimester** or post-partum
- 75% flare at delivery
- Genetic risk: **HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4**
- Fetal outcomes: prematurity, small for gestational age
- **DIF: linear C3 at the DEJ**
- Treatment: topical steroids, prednisone, Dupixent
- Recurrence: very high risk with subsequent pregnancies; only 5% skip a pregnancy; 50% will recur with oral contraceptive pills (OCPs)

### Mucous Membrane Pemphigoid (MMP)
- Rare: 1-10 per million; age 60-80
- Predominantly mucosal disease with some skin involvement
- **Site involvement**: 90% oral, 40% conjunctival, 30% nasopharyngeal, 20-30% skin only (**Brunsting-Perry** variant)
- 3 autoantibody patterns: **Laminin 332**, **alpha-6-beta-4 integrin** (ocular MMP), **BP180**
- **DIF: linear IgG and C3 at the BMZ**
- IIF: only positive 20-30% of the time
- Runs a more **chronic course** than bullous pemphigoid
- Treatment: Rituximab, IVIG, Cellcept, Cytoxan

## Objective 10 & 11: Dermatitis Herpetiformis and Its Link to Diet

### Link to Diet (Gluten)
Dermatitis herpetiformis (DH) is a **gluten hypersensitivity** skin condition. Gluten ingestion drives the immune response; eliminating gluten improves both the gut and skin disease.

- >90% have **gluten-sensitive enteropathy**; **20% have frank Celiac disease**
- Pathophysiology: antibodies form against **tissue transglutaminase (TG2)** in the lamina propria of the gut → **epitope spreading** to **epidermal transglutaminase (TG3)** in skin → DIF shows **granular IgA** in dermal papillae
- **HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8** are predisposing genetic factors
- Lifelong disease — waxes and wanes with gluten exposure
- Strict gluten-free diet normalizes small bowel changes and controls skin in ~80% of patients
- **Avoid iodides** (seaweed, salt, eggs, certain medications/supplements) — can worsen DH

### Clinical Presentation
- Prevalence ~1/10,000; mean onset age in 30s (range 2-90); M>W (1.5-2:1)
- **Very itchy papules and vesicles on extensor surfaces**: elbows, knees, buttocks, back of neck
- Blisters are very thin and fragile → usually only see excoriations and erosions, rarely intact vesicles

### Diagnosis
- **Gold standard DIF**: biopsy of **uninvolved skin away from the lesion** — shows **granular IgA deposits in the dermal papillae**
- ELISA for TG3 antibodies (available through ARUP)

### Treatment
- **Eliminate gluten** (primary treatment)
- **Dapsone** PRN (rapid symptom relief while waiting for gluten-free diet to take effect)
- Sulfasalazine, colchicine as alternatives

## Objective 12: Important Differential Diagnoses for Autoimmune Bullous Disease

Always consider non-autoimmune causes of blistering before diagnosing an autoimmune disease.

### Dyshidrotic Eczema
- Chronic, relapsing course on **hands and feet**
- Symmetric, firm, deep-seated itchy vesicles and bullae on the palms and lateral/medial aspects of fingers
- Characteristic **"tapioca-like"** appearance

### Bullous Allergic Contact Dermatitis
- Acute, itchy blisters from severe inflammation
- Distribution follows allergen exposure pattern ("outside job")
- Treatment: allergen avoidance, topical/oral steroids

### Bullous Arthropod Bite Reaction
- Grouped erythematous pruritic papules that occasionally become bullous
- Children react more severely than adults; exaggerated responses seen in CLL and hematologic malignancies
- Treatment: topical/oral steroids

### Bullous Tinea
- Caused by **Trichophyton mentagrophytes**
- Burning, itching, discomfort; acutely inflammatory multilocular bullae, typically on feet
- Treatment: topical antifungal creams

### Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)
- Drug reaction (antibiotics and anticonvulsants most common), typically 1-3 weeks after new drug
- Prodrome of fever, sore throat, headache, malaise 1-14 days prior
- Widespread **painful erythema** progressing to skin sloughing
- SJS: ≤10% BSA; SJS/TEN overlap: 10-30% BSA; TEN: >30% BSA
- Biopsy: **full-thickness epidermal necrosis**
- High mortality; treatment: stop offending drug, supportive care, consider Enbrel + IVIG; send to ER

### Bullous Impetigo
- **Staphylococcus aureus** exfoliative toxin cleaves **DSG1** (same target as pemphigus foliaceus)
- Common in children, especially with atopic dermatitis
- Face, perineum, exposed skin; arcuate lesions with collarette of scale
- Highly contagious
- Non-bullous impetigo can also be caused by Group A beta-hemolytic strep

### Staph Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS)
- Staph aureus produces exfoliative toxin targeting **DSG1**
- Affects neonates and adults with renal failure (cannot clear the toxin)
- Localized infection (nose, umbilicus, perineum) with systemic spread of toxin
- Clinical: fever, malaise, irritability; radial fissures around the mouth; superficial fragile blisters rupturing to leave tender, denuding skin

### When to Refer to Dermatology
- Any time there are blisters
- Any time an autoimmune bullous disease is suspected
