## Objective 1: Recognize Common Cutaneous Drug Reaction Patterns

### What is an Adverse Cutaneous Drug Eruption?
- Any unintended or undesired skin effect of a drug
- Most common adverse event in hospitalized patients (2-3% of all inpatients)
- May be **immunologic (allergic)** or **non-immunologic** — the vast majority (75-80%) are non-immunologic
- Most are **self-limited**; if eruption is mild and the drug is critical, can "treat through"
- Some represent **complex drug eruptions**: adverse drug reactions with systemic manifestations (SJS/TEN, DIHS/DRESS)
- In darker skin tones, clinical findings may be subtle and more difficult to recognize

### Urticaria (Hives)
- **Morphology:** well-defined, *transient*, blanchable, pink, edematous plaques with pale center — called **"wheals"**
- Individual lesions last **<24 hours** (often seem to move around, lasting <1 hour)
- +/- pruritus
- **Pathogenesis:** release of histamine or other vasoactive substances from **mast cells**; **Type I hypersensitivity reaction** (IgE-mediated)
- 9% associated with drugs (also caused by foods, infections, post-vaccines, or idiopathic)
- **Common culprits:** NSAIDs, aspirin, opioids, antibiotics (penicillins and sulfas), blood transfusion/products, CT contrast, ACE inhibitors, diuretics
- **Timing:** 1-2 weeks after first exposure; faster on re-challenge

#### Warning Sign: Angioedema and Anaphylaxis
- **Angioedema** is a variant of urticaria with edema at a deeper level (dermis/subcutaneous fat)
  - Diffuse erythematous swelling of eyelids, lips, tongue, genitalia
  - Can last **>24 hours** (unlike hives which last <24 hours)
  - Systemic symptoms: fatigue, fever, arthralgias, respiratory symptoms, abdominal pain, diarrhea
  - Can be caused by medications, foods, or be idiopathic
- **Anaphylaxis:** laryngeal edema, nausea/vomiting, hypotension, shock
- **CAUTION:** Angioedema of the larynx, upper airway, or tongue can progress to airway obstruction and asphyxiation
- **Immediate treatment with epinephrine is required**

### Drug-Induced Photosensitivity Reaction

There are two distinct types:

| Feature | Drug-Induced Phototoxicity | Drug-Induced Photoallergy |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Non-allergic | Allergic |
| Occurs in | All exposed patients (dose-dependent) | Only some patients |
| Predominant symptom | Burning > pruritus | Pruritus > burning |
| UV wavelength | Usually **UVA** (not filtered by windows) | UVA |
| Distribution | Photo-distributed; may blister | Initially photo-distributed; may spread to photo-protected areas |

#### Drug-Induced Phototoxic Reactions
- Systemic drug in blood vessels absorbs ultraviolet light, causing a sunburn-like reaction
- **Morphology:** bright red, confluent edematous patches and plaques in photo-distribution (face, V of chest, dorsal hands/extensor arms); sharp cutoff at shirt line; spares under chin/neck; may blister
- **Common culprits:** NSAIDs, quinolones, **tetracyclines (esp. doxycycline)**, voriconazole, vemurafenib (BRAF inhibitors), diuretics, St. John's wort

#### Drug-Induced Photoallergic Reactions
- **Morphology:** erythematous papules and plaques, initially in photo-distribution, but can spread to photo-protected areas
- **Common culprits:** sunscreen, NSAIDs, griseofulvin, antimalarials, quinolones, sulfonamides

### Exanthematous (Morbilliform) Drug Eruption
- **Most common** medication-induced eruption — **75% of all drug eruptions**
- "Morbilliform" means measles-like
- **Morphology:** symmetric, blanching, coalescing erythematous macules and papules; itchy; **centrifugal spread** (arises on trunk, spreads to proximal extremities)
- In darker skin, may appear hyperpigmented or have a purplish hue
- **Timing:** typically 4-14 days after first exposure; faster on re-challenge (1-3 days); persists 1-2 weeks after drug withdrawn
- **Mechanism:** Type IV hypersensitivity reaction
- If medication is essential and reaction is mild, can "treat through"
- **Ddx:** viral exanthem (tends to be asymptomatic), graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)
- Very rare to have mucosal involvement — if present, evaluate for more serious drug eruption

## Objective 2: Initial Approach to a Patient with Suspected Drug Eruption

### Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Exclude alternative causes** — viral exanthems, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), infection, autoimmune or rheumatologic disease; keep DDx broad
2. **Look for warning signs** to differentiate non-life-threatening from life-threatening reactions
3. **Determine the type of drug eruption pattern** if possible
4. **Determine possible culprits** — create a drug chart:
   - Names of all medicines (include OTC medications such as NSAIDs)
   - Start date of all medications (go back up to **3 months**)
   - Timing of symptoms/rash
   - Patient history (has this occurred before?)
5. **Familiarize yourself with previous drug adverse event data** — use LITT's dermatology database (drugeruptiondata.com) to look up the most common timeline for a given reaction pattern

## Objective 3: Expected Timelines of Common Cutaneous Drug Eruptions

| Drug Eruption | Onset After Starting Drug |
|---|---|
| Simple morbilliform eruption | 4 days – 2 weeks |
| SJS/TEN | 1 – 3 weeks |
| DIHS/DRESS | 2 – 6 weeks (up to 8 weeks) |
| Angioedema/urticaria | Can occur within hours to 1-2 weeks |

- These are approximations — **there will always be exceptions**
- Patients previously sensitized to a drug can develop rash faster on re-exposure
- For DIHS/DRESS, ask about medications started up to **3 months** prior

## Objective 4: Erythema Multiforme — Clinical Presentation and Triggers

### Clinical Features
- Acute, self-limited skin reaction with few complications
- **Morphology:** symmetrically distributed erythematous papules and **targetoid plaques** in **acral distribution and face** (palms, soles, dorsal and extensor hands/arms/legs/feet)
- **Classic Target Lesion:** 3 distinct circular color zones:
  1. Central dusky area (may have central bulla/crust)
  2. Middle pink or edematous zone
  3. Outer red ring
- **+/- limited mucous membrane involvement** (limited, in contrast to SJS/TEN)
- Often crusting on lips secondary to HSV

### Triggers
- Usually **infectious**, not drug-related
  - **HSV is most common (~50%)**
  - Mycoplasma, salmonella, histoplasma
- **Mechanism:** cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction (**Type IV**)

### Key Distinction from SJS/TEN
- Erythema multiforme does **not progress to SJS/TEN**
- However, both can present with skin lesions + mucosal membrane involvement
- If systemic symptoms or **extensive skin sloughing** is present (with or without targetoid lesions or mucosal involvement), consider **SJS/TEN**

## Objective 5: Warning Signs of Life-Threatening Drug Eruptions

The following signs should raise suspicion for a severe, potentially life-threatening drug reaction:

- **Fever**
- **Facial edema**
- **Lymphadenopathy**
- Swelling of tongue, eyes, lips
- **Skin pain**, blistering, purple/dusky color, **+Nikolsky sign**
- Palpable purpura, retiform purpura
- SOB, nausea/vomiting, hypotension, voice change
- **Mucosal involvement** (ocular, oral, genital)
- **Laboratory abnormalities:** eosinophilia, transaminitis, elevated creatinine, atypical lymphocytes

> A morbilliform rash + fever + facial swelling + lymphadenopathy should prompt evaluation for DIHS/DRESS.
> A rash + skin pain + blistering/peeling + mucositis should prompt evaluation for SJS/TEN.

## Objective 6: Clinical Presentation of DIHS/DRESS and SJS/TEN, and Systemic Complications

---

### DIHS (Drug-Induced Hypersensitivity Syndrome) / DRESS Syndrome

**DRESS** = Drug Reaction with Eosinophilic and Systemic Symptoms
**DIHS** is the preferred term because **>30% of patients do not have eosinophilia**

- **Mortality up to 10%**

#### Pathogenesis
- Defect in drug metabolism — inability to eliminate toxic reactive intermediates
- May be related to reactivation of **HHV-6** (human herpesvirus 6)

#### Timing
- Long latency period: **2-6 weeks** (or longer) after initiation of drug

#### Common Culprits
- **Aromatic anticonvulsants:** carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital (70-80% cross-reactivity between them)
- Allopurinol
- Sulfonamides
- Minocycline
- Dapsone
- HIV medications (nevirapine, abacavir)
- Vancomycin

#### Key Clinical Features
- **Fever, malaise** (often early symptoms)
- **Marked facial edema** (pronounced periorbital; +/- swelling of ear lobes)
- **Lymphadenopathy** (may be tender)
- **Morbilliform, itchy, edematous, sometimes pustular eruption** with perifollicular accentuation; on face, extremities, upper trunk
- Can also see vesicles, urticaria, bullae, erythroderma, purpura

#### Systemic Evaluation (check if rash + fever + facial edema + lymphadenopathy)
- **Hematologic:** eosinophilia >1.5, atypical lymphocytes
- **Internal organ involvement** (may be asymptomatic; may occur months after onset):
  - **Hepatitis (transaminases)** — most common and most severe
  - Interstitial nephritis (BUN/creatinine) — more common with allopurinol and vancomycin
  - Interstitial pneumonia (CXR)
  - Myocarditis/pericarditis (EKG, ECHO, cardiac enzymes)
  - Encephalitis/aseptic meningitis
  - Colitis/esophagitis
  - **Delayed autoimmune sequelae:** autoimmune diabetes, thyroiditis (check TSH, re-check in 2-3 months)
- **Check HHV-6 DNA PCR** to assess for viral reactivation

#### Diagnosis: RegiSCAR Criteria
The **RegiSCAR** scoring system is used clinically to determine how likely a diagnosis of DIHS is. It includes fever, enlarged lymph nodes, atypical lymphocytes, eosinophilia, skin rash extent and quality, internal organ involvement, resolution time, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses. A RegiSCAR calculator is available online — the specific point values do not need to be memorized.

### Stevens Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)

- Rare but potentially **life-threatening** drug eruption
- Rapid progression is possible
- **SJS and TEN are the same disease on a spectrum**, classified by % total body surface area (**BSA**) with epidermal detachment:

| Classification | BSA Epidermal Detachment | Mortality |
|---|---|---|
| SJS | <10% | 1-5% |
| SJS/TEN overlap | 10-30% | ~19% |
| TEN | >30% | 15-35% |

#### Pathogenesis
- Almost always **drug-induced**
- Host inability to detoxify drug metabolite → **cell-mediated cytotoxic T cell response** → **apoptosis of keratinocytes** → full-thickness necrosis of the epidermis
- **Common culprits:** NSAIDs, antibiotics, anticonvulsants (sulfonamides, allopurinol, lamotrigine)

#### Timing
- **1-3 weeks** after drug ingestion

#### Clinical Features
- **Prodrome (days before rash):** fever, malaise, headache, vomiting, diarrhea, cough, coryza, pharyngitis, myalgias, arthralgias
- Starts abruptly, spreads rapidly
- **Hallmark:** **mucocutaneous tenderness and erythema with extensive exfoliation**
  - Lips and mouth: 100%
  - Eyes: ~91% (conjunctival involvement — can be subtle; ask about gritty/painful sensation)
  - Nose, genitals, anal mucous membranes
- **Morphology:** dusky red macules on trunk and extremities, atypical targets, bullae, erosions and ulcerations, **sheets of desquamation**
- **+Nikolsky sign:** exerting tangential mechanical pressure with a finger on erythematous skin leads to dermal-epidermal cleavage

#### Early vs. Late Findings
- **Early SJS/TEN** may be subtle — look for mucosal erosions and dusky tender macules/patches
- **Later stages** show full-thickness epidermal necrosis, sheets of desquamation, extensive erosions

#### Systemic Complications of SJS/TEN
- Ocular scarring
- Genital problems (urethritis, phimosis, vaginal synechiae)
- Renal involvement (glomerulonephritis)
- Hepatitis
- Pulmonary involvement (cough, dyspnea, pneumonia, hypoxemia)
- Esophagitis
- **Sepsis**
- **Fluid loss and electrolyte imbalance**
- **Temperature dysregulation**

> Loss of skin integrity leads to inability to regulate temperature and fluid/electrolyte balance — similar to a severe burn patient.

#### Mortality Prediction Scores
Two scoring tools are used (do not need to be memorized; available online):

| Score | Key Variables |
|---|---|
| **SCORTEN** | Age ≥40, malignancy, BSA ≥10%, tachycardia ≥120, serum urea >10, serum glucose >14, serum bicarbonate <20 |
| **ABCD-10** | Age >50, bicarbonate <20, cancer, dialysis (most heavily weighted), BSA >10% |

- ABCD-10 places more emphasis on **renal dysfunction** (dialysis = 3 points vs. 1 for others)

## Lecture Summary

1. **Exanthematous (morbilliform) drug eruption** is the most common type of adverse cutaneous drug reaction — usually arises **7-14 days** after initiation of the medication.
2. **Erythema multiforme** is a relatively benign, acute, self-limited reaction, typically triggered by infections such as **HSV** or mycoplasma rather than drugs. The classic feature is the **target lesion**.
3. **Drug-induced photosensitivity** reactions are categorized as phototoxic (burning, confluent erythema, mediated by **UVA light**) or photoallergic (pruritic, allergic).
4. **DIHS/DRESS** is a severe, potentially life-threatening reaction characterized by fever, facial edema, lymphadenopathy, transaminitis, and elevated creatinine. It usually arises **2-6 weeks** after initiation of the offending drug.
5. **SJS/TEN** is a rare but possibly life-threatening adverse drug eruption. **Mucosal involvement is universal.** Classified by %BSA of epidermal detachment.
