## Objective 1: Epidemiology, Pathophysiology, Clinical Features, and Diagnosis of Gout

### Definition

**Gout** is an inflammatory arthritis caused by **deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals** within joints, tendons, and surrounding tissues.

### Epidemiology

- Affects ~5% of the US population
- More common in **males**
- Gout is **rare** in men <25 years old and pre-menopausal women
- Women at increased risk if:
  - **Postmenopausal** (estrogen is uricosuric/protective)
  - On **thiazide diuretics**
  - Have **CKD**

### Risk Factors

| Risk Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Obesity (BMI >30) | Increases uric acid production |
| Insulin resistance / DM | Reduces renal uric acid excretion |
| Purine-rich diet (meat, seafood) | Increases uric acid production |
| Alcohol / fructose intake | Inhibits renal clearance of urate |
| CKD, HTN | Reduces renal excretion |
| OSA | Associated with elevated SUA |
| Medications | See below |

**Drugs causing hyperuricemia:** Aspirin (low dose), Lasix/Thiazides/Chlorthalidone, ETOH, Tacrolimus/Cyclosporine (transplant patients), Niacin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol

**Mechanism of thiazides:** OAT1 transporter brings thiazide in, keeping urate in blood; OAT4 pulls urate back in; volume depletion causes even more urate reabsorption — net result is elevated serum uric acid.

### Pathophysiology: Uric Acid

- Uric acid is the end product of **purine metabolism** in humans (humans lack uricase)
- **Hyperuricemia** = serum uric acid (SUA) >/= 6.8 mg/dL (insoluble at this level at 37°C)
- Leads to **monosodium urate crystal** formation

**Two mechanisms of hyperuricemia:**

| Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Overproduction** | Inborn error of purine metabolism; malignancies (myelo/lymphoproliferative); excess alcohol, purine, or fructose intake; psoriasis; hemolytic disorders; obesity; heavy exercise |
| **Underexcretion** | Chronic kidney disease; drugs (thiazides, cyclosporine, aspirin, etc.) |

### Disease Stages

**Stage 1: Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia**
- Can persist for **decades**
- Elevated SUA in serum; MSU crystals may be forming silently

**Stage 2: Acute Intermittent Gout**
- Acute onset pain and inflammatory arthritis
- Peak symptoms within **<12-24 hours**
- Typically **monoarticular** (can be polyarticular)
- Flares with increasing intensity and frequency over time
- **Commonly involved joints:**
  - **1st MTP joint** (podagra) — 80-90% of patients at some point
  - Midfoot, ankles, heels, knees
  - Less often: wrists, hands (DIPs/PIPs)
- Men typically begin symptoms at age **40-60**
- Average **10 years** between Stage 2 and Stage 3
- DDx: **Septic Arthritis** — always send synovial fluid for gram stain and culture; WBC >50k raises concern for septic arthritis

**Inflammatory mechanism of gout flare:**
- MSU crystals activate **TLR2/TLR4** on monocytes/macrophages
- Triggers **NLRP3 inflammasome** → releases **IL-1β**
- Causes endothelial activation, neutrophil recruitment
- Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) form → release anti-inflammatory molecules (IL-10, IL-1ra, TGF-β, IL-37) → **resolution of flare**

**Stage 3: Chronic/Advanced (Tophaceous Gout)**
- **Chronic tophaceous gout**: polyarticular, can involve small joints
- SUA often significantly elevated
- **Tophi** can form at:
  - Pressure points (elbows, heels)
  - Connective tissue (heart valves, sclerae, pinna)
- DDx: **Rheumatoid Arthritis**
- Tophus = clustering of MSU crystals surrounded by innate and adaptive immune cells

### Diagnosis of Gout

**Diagnostic categories:**

| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Definite** | MSU crystals in synovial fluid OR tophi + acute monoarthritis |
| **Probable** | Podagra + hyperuricemia |
| **Possible** | Recurrent monoarthritis + hyperuricemia |

**Podagra** = acute synovitis of the 1st MTP joint

**Workup:**

**1. History and Physical — the "4 R's":**
- **Rapid** onset
- **Recurrent** attacks
- **Rare** in pre-menopausal women
- **Red** (erythematous, hot, swollen joint)

**2. Serum Uric Acid (SUA):**
- Uric acid becomes insoluble at **6.8 mg/dL** at 37°C
- SUA >6 mg% = 1.3x increased risk of gout
- **Up to 40% of patients may have a normal SUA during an acute attack** (crystals deposit into joint, lowering serum levels; pain also triggers ACTH/cortisol with uricosuric effect)
- Elevated SUA does not mean gout; normal SUA during flare does not rule out gout
- SUA levels vary by as much as 30-50%

**3. Synovial Fluid Analysis:**
- Inflammatory: WBC >2,000
- MSU crystals present
- **Crystals:** strongly negatively birefringent, **needle-shaped**; appear **yellow when parallel** to polarizer
- PPV = 100%; NPV = 80%
- Always send fluid for culture to rule out septic arthritis

**4. Imaging:**

| Modality | Finding | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|---|
| X-ray | Classic "rat bite erosion" (periarticular); takes ~10 years to form | 30% | 90% |
| Dual Energy CT (DECT) | Identifies urate deposits; use when fluid cannot be obtained | 78% | 93% |

- Gouty erosions are **periarticular** (vs. RA erosions which are articular)

## Objective 2: Clinical Features, Pathophysiology, and Diagnosis of Pseudogout

### Definition

**Pseudogout / Chondrocalcinosis / CPPD** is a form of inflammatory arthritis caused by **deposition of calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals** within cartilage and other articular tissue.

CPPD is a spectrum:

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Pseudogout** | CPP crystals in synovial fluid → acute arthritis |
| **Pyrophosphate Arthropathy** | Crystals + chondrocalcinosis OR chondrocalcinosis + history/exam → chronic arthritis |
| **Chondrocalcinosis** | Asymptomatic calcification of meniscal cartilage, knee, or triangular ligament of wrist |

Pseudogout is "a great mimicker" — important DDx in patients with:
- Seronegative RA workup
- Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) workup

### Associations/Causes of Pseudogout

- Trauma
- Joint surgery / post-operative phase
- Bisphosphonates
- **In patients <55 years old**, look for underlying metabolic causes:
  - **PTH** (hyperparathyroidism)
  - **Mg** (Gitelman/Bartter syndrome — hypomagnesemia)
  - **Fe** (hemochromatosis)
  - **Cu** (Wilson's disease)
  - **Phos** (hypophosphatasia)

### Diagnosis of Pseudogout

**1. History and Physical:**
- Similar presentation to gout
- Most commonly affects **knee** and **wrist**

**2. Labs:**
- Calcium, Magnesium, TSH, PTH (look for elevation), Copper, Iron

**3. Synovial Fluid Analysis:**
- Inflammatory: WBC >2,000
- CPP crystals present
- **Crystals:** weakly positively birefringent, **rhomboid-shaped**; appear **blue** under polarized light

| Crystal | Shape | Birefringence | Color under polarizer |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSU (Gout) | Needle | Strongly negative | Yellow (parallel) |
| CPP (Pseudogout) | Rhomboid | Weakly positive | Blue |

**4. Imaging:**
- **Chondrocalcinosis** seen on x-ray: calcification of cartilage, most commonly at knees and wrists; may also deposit in synovial lining, tendons, ligaments

### Basic Calcium Phosphate (BCP) Crystals / Hydroxyapatite

- **Hydroxyapatite** is a type of basic calcium phosphate crystal
- Deposits in articular tissues, skin, arteries, breast tissue
- Too small for light microscopy (20-100 nm); may appear as "shiny coins" when aggregated

**Milwaukee Shoulder** (calcium hydroxyapatite deposition):
- Classic presentation: **elderly female** with large swollen shoulder
- Non-inflammatory synovial effusions
- Severe damage on x-ray, large rotator cuff tears
- Treatment: conservative (NSAIDs, corticosteroid injection); possible surgery

**Calcific Periarthritis / Tendonitis** ("hydroxyapatite pseudopodagra"):
- Premenopausal females, age ~40-60
- Acute pain/swelling/erythema of 1st MTP
- X-ray shows calcific deposits (often resolved on follow-up imaging after treatment)

## Objective 3: Treatment of an Acute Attack of Gout and/or Pseudogout

Both gout and pseudogout are acute non-infectious arthritides — use **anti-inflammatory therapy** for both.

### Options

**1. Colchicine**

- **MOA:** Binds tubulin, prevents microtubule formation, affects neutrophil function
- **Dosing:**
  - Initial: **1.2 mg**, followed by **0.6 mg** one hour later
  - Thereafter: continue **0.6 mg bid** for 2-4 weeks
  - Longer therapy for: oligoarticular/polyarticular disease, tophaceous gout, recurrent attacks over short time frames
- **Contraindication:** GFR <30 cc/min (dose adjustment required)
- **Principal toxicity:** GI (nausea, emesis, diarrhea, bloating, cramping)

**2. NSAIDs**

| Drug | Loading Dose | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|
| Ibuprofen | 1,200-1,600 mg | 1,600-2,400 mg/day tid x 2-4 wk |
| Indomethacin | 100 mg | 25-50 mg tid x 2-4 wk |
| Naproxen | 1,000 mg | 500 mg bid x 2-4 wk |
| Celecoxib | 200 mg | 100-200 mg bid x 2-4 wk |

**Contraindications — ABC:**
- **A** = Anticoagulation, Aspirin allergy
- **B** = Bleeding (GI)
- **C** = Creatinine elevated, CHF

**3. Corticosteroids**

- **Prednisone:** 40 mg daily x 3 days, reduce by 10 mg every 3 days until done
- **Depomedrol:** 80-160 mg IM
- **Intraarticular:** Kenalog 40 mg or Depomedrol 80 mg
  - For large joints (knee/ankle): aspirate fluid and inject steroids directly — fewer systemic side effects
- Short-term side effects: mood changes, elevated BP, elevated glucose, insomnia, increased appetite, increased infection risk (at doses equivalent to ≥20 mg prednisone)

**4. Biologics (IL-1 inhibitors)**

Used when patients cannot tolerate colchicine, NSAIDs, or corticosteroids:
- **Anakinra** — IL-1 receptor antagonist (inhibits IL-1α and IL-1β)
- **Rilonacept** — soluble decoy receptor
- **Canakinumab** — anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody (EMA approved for gout)

## Objective 4: Prevention of Future Attacks of Gout

### Why Prevent?

Gout is highly recurrent:
- ~60% recurrence by Year 1
- ~78% recurrence by Year 2
- ~90% recurrence by Year 10

### Tools for Prevention

**1. Diet**

| Dietary Factor | Intake | Relative Risk of Gout |
|---|---|---|
| Meat | >1.9 servings/day | RR = 1.4 |
| Seafood | >0.56 servings/day | RR = 1.5 |
| Dairy | >2.5 servings/day | RR = 0.56 (protective) |
| Alcohol | 2 drinks/day | RR = 2.4 |

- Beer: RR = 2.5 per beer/day; Wine: no increase in risk
- Alcohol is the **single biggest dietary contributor** (inhibits renal clearance of urate)
- Diet alone only lowers SUA by ~10% on average; adherence is difficult
- Dairy is **protective**

**2. Stop drugs that raise urate**
- Thiazides, niacin, cyclosporine, low-dose ASA, pyrazinamide, ethambutol
- Switch to drugs that lower SUA when possible: **losartan**, **fenofibrate**, high-dose ASA (>3g/day)

**3. Urate-lowering drugs — target SUA <6.0 mg/dL**

| Drug Class | Drug | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Xanthine oxidase inhibitor | **Allopurinol** | Reduces uric acid production |
| Xanthine oxidase inhibitor | **Febuxostat** | Reduces uric acid production |
| Uricosuric | **Probenecid** | Increases renal excretion of urate |
| Recombinant uricase | **Pegloticase** | Converts urate to allantoin (more soluble) |

**Allopurinol dosing (ACR recommendations):**
- Start at **100 mg/day** (50 mg/day if CKD stage 4)
- Titrate monthly until SUA **6.0 mg/dL x 2 months**
- Monitor LFTs and CBC monthly
- **HLA-B*5801 testing** required before starting in Han Chinese, Thai, Africans, and Koreans — significantly increased risk of severe hypersensitivity reaction

**Probenecid (uricosuric):**
- First choice uricosuric
- Avoid if CC <50 ml/min, urolithiasis, or 24-hour urine uric acid >700 mg
- Fenofibrate and losartan have mild uricosuric effects useful as adjuncts

**Key clinical points:**
- **Never stop allopurinol or febuxostat** during an acute attack — stopping causes SUA fluctuation which can precipitate more flares
- Colchicine **treats** acute attacks; it does not **prevent** gout (urate-lowering therapy prevents)
- SUA can vary by 30-40%; treat to target, not to a single level
- Allopurinol **can** be used in CKD — start at 50 mg, titrate slowly with monitoring
- Allopurinol hypersensitivity occurs in <1% overall but is significantly more common in Asians and Blacks (HLA-B*5801)
- Treat acute attacks for **at least 2 weeks** — stopping at 1 week risks recurrence

---

## Key Summary Points

1. **Treat attack** = anti-inflammatory (colchicine, NSAIDs, or corticosteroids)
2. **Prevent attack** = xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol or febuxostat)
3. **SUA target** = 6.0 mg/dL
4. **Never stop** allopurinol or febuxostat once started
5. **Diagnose gout definitively** with MSU crystals in synovial fluid (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent, yellow when parallel to polarizer)
6. **Diagnose CPPD** with CPP crystals in synovial fluid (rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent, blue) or chondrocalcinosis on imaging
7. Always send synovial fluid for **gram stain and culture** to rule out septic arthritis
