## 1. Epidemiology and Common Mechanisms of TBI

### Definition
TBI is an alteration in brain function caused by:
- Direct blow/impact to the head
- Acceleration/deceleration without direct impact (e.g., whiplash in an MVA)
- Blast waves
- Foreign body penetrating the brain

Signs of "alteration in brain function" include: loss of consciousness, amnesia (retrograde or anterograde), altered mental status (confusion, disorientation, slow thinking), and any neurologic deficit. A direct head impact is **not required** to sustain a TBI.

### Epidemiology
- **Leading cause of death and disability** among children, young adults, and elderly in the US
- Global incidence: ~3-4 per 1,000 people
- **Most common causes:** Falls (~50%) followed by motor vehicle accidents (~15%), plus violence/assault, self-harm/suicide, sports injuries, blast injuries
- **Age trends:** Highest risk at extremes of age -- adults >75 (~17/1,000), children 0-4 (~16/1,000), young adults 15-24 (~10/1,000)
- **Sex trends:** Male:female ~1.5:1 for sustaining TBI; fatal TBI closer to 3:1

### TBI-Related Deaths
- Suicide: ~35% (highest risk >75 years)
- Falls: ~30% (highest risk >75 and <20)
- MVAs: ~17% (highest risk 15-24 years)

## 2. Social Determinants of Health and TBI Risk

### Motor Vehicle Accidents
- Higher risk in male patients (~3x overall TBI risk vs. female patients)
- Higher risk in American Indian/Alaskan Native communities, driven primarily by higher MVA rates with contributing factors including seatbelt use, alcohol use while driving, and socioeconomic stressors

### Military
- >500,000 TBI diagnosed in military members 2000-2024; >80% mild (concussion)
- Blast injuries from Iraq and Afghanistan are a significant source
- Intersection of TBI and PTSD can complicate recovery -- additive stress on the brain prolongs and complicates healing

### Vulnerable Populations
- **Homelessness:** 2-4x more likely to have history of TBI if patient has experienced homelessness
- **Incarceration:** ~50% of incarcerated persons have a history of TBI; ~2.5x risk of incarceration with history of TBI
- Key complicating factors: limited access to care/follow-up, intersection with mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD)

## 3. Anatomy and Pathology of TBI Injuries

### Diffuse Axonal Injury (DAI)
- **Mechanism:** Mechanical stress causes axonal shearing, disrupting axonal transport. Damage to plasma membrane around nodes of Ranvier disrupts transmission.
- **Clinical:** Loss of consciousness at time of injury is universal. LOC >6 hours highly correlates with DAI.
- **MRI findings:** Multiple lesions along white matter tracts at grey/white junction, corpus callosum (especially splenium), and midbrain. Microhemorrhages on specialized sequences (GRE/SWI). Can be imaging-negative up to 50% of the time.
- **Gross path:** Hemorrhage in corpus callosum; chronic cases show corpus callosum atrophy.
- **Mild TBI (concussion):** May have mild disruption to axonal neurofilament organization, not to the degree of DAI.

### Cerebral Contusion
- **Mechanism:** Bruising of brain tissue from impact of brain against irregular inner skull surface; damage to deep penetrating vessels.
- **Locations:** Frontal lobes and temporal tips most common.
- **Coup vs. contrecoup:** Coup = directly below point of trauma; contrecoup = opposite side. Deceleration injuries commonly have frontal/temporal coup and occipital contrecoup.
- **Clinical:** Depends on location -- frontal contusions commonly cause personality changes (apathy, disinhibition).
- **Imaging:** Bright (hyperdense) areas on CT in frontal/temporal regions.

### Epidural Hematoma
- **Mechanism:** Arterial laceration, most often **middle meningeal artery**; can be associated with temporal bone fracture.
- **Location:** Between skull and dura (potential space).
- **Imaging:** **Convex** (lens-shaped) on CT; acute blood is hyperdense (bright).
- **Clinical:** Classic presentation: unconscious -> lucid interval -> deterioration to unconsciousness as hematoma expands. Headache, N/V, drowsiness, seizures, possible aphasia/hemiparesis.
- **Neurologic emergency** requiring neurosurgical drainage (burr hole).
- Most common in trauma patients in their 20s, rare in patients >50-60.

### Subdural Hematoma
- **Mechanism:** Tearing of **bridging veins** (veins traveling from brain through dura to venous sinuses); most susceptible in frontal regions.
- **Location:** Between dura and arachnoid mater.
- **Imaging:** **Concave** (crescent-shaped) on CT. Acute = hyperdense; ~2 weeks = isodense; chronic = hypodense (CSF-like).
- **Clinical:** Headache, mental status changes, focal neurologic deficits. Can follow even mild trauma.
- **Older individuals at higher risk** due to cerebral atrophy allowing more brain movement within the skull.
- Can be chronic and even asymptomatic. Treatment: surgical drainage if symptomatic; observation if asymptomatic.

### Traumatic Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
- **Location:** Between arachnoid and pia mater; blood layers into sulci.
- Isolated traumatic SAH is uncommon; usually occurs with other hemorrhages (contusion/laceration).
- Most common **non-traumatic** cause of SAH: rupture of an arterial aneurysm.

## 4. Cytotoxic vs. Vasogenic Cerebral Edema

| Feature | Cytotoxic Edema | Vasogenic Edema |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Intracellular | Extracellular |
| **Mechanism** | Energy failure -> Na/K pump dysfunction -> Na accumulates in cell -> water follows via osmosis -> cell swelling | Blood-brain barrier disruption -> extra fluid enters extracellular space |
| **Cause in trauma** | Severe trauma with DAI, global ischemia | Around contusions/hematomas; also seen with tumors and abscesses |
| **Reversibility** | Irreversible (cellular death) | Potentially reversible (cells haven't necessarily died), but excess pressure can lead to cytotoxic edema |
| **Tissue affected** | Grey and white matter | White matter more than grey matter |

### Imaging of Diffuse Post-Traumatic Edema
- CT shows: loss of grey/white matter differentiation, effacement (loss) of sulci due to swelling
- Dangerous because the brain is in a fixed vault (skull) -> leads to intracranial hypertension

## 5. Elevated Intracranial Pressure, Hydrocephalus, and Herniation

### Monro-Kellie Doctrine
The cranium has a **constant volume** containing three components:
- Brain tissue (80%)
- CSF (10%) -- ~20 mL/hr (500 mL/day) produced/reabsorbed
- Blood (10%)

If any component expands in volume, intracranial pressure (ICP) rises.

**CPP = MAP - ICP** (Cerebral Perfusion Pressure = Mean Arterial Pressure - Intracranial Pressure). If ICP rises, cerebral blood flow decreases, compounding injury to already damaged tissue.

### Hydrocephalus
- **Communicating:** CSF resorption impaired (e.g., blood clogging arachnoid granulations) but ventricles not obstructed
- **Non-communicating:** CSF flow obstructed (e.g., herniation pinching off 3rd/4th ventricles causing backup in lateral ventricles)

### Herniation Syndromes
Herniation = brain tissue displaced due to pressure from hemorrhage or edema. ICP peaks ~2-4 days post-injury.

- **Subfalcine:** Cingulate gyrus pushes laterally under the falx cerebri
- **Central:** Diencephalon/midbrain descends; can flatten midbrain and compromise respiratory centers
- **Uncal:** Uncus (tip of temporal lobe) pushed over tentorium cerebelli; often causes **CN III palsy** -> ipsilateral pupil dilation (loss of parasympathetic fibers that constrict pupil)
- **Tonsillar:** Cerebellar tonsils push into foramen magnum; compresses medulla -> can compromise respiratory centers (fatal)

## 6. Clinical Signs of Basilar Skull Fracture

Basilar skull fractures can be **imaging-negative** and are diagnosed clinically:

- **Battle's sign:** Bruising behind the ear (mastoid ecchymosis)
- **Hemotympanum:** Blood behind the ear drum
- **Raccoon eyes:** Bilateral periorbital bruising sparing the eyelids (tarsal plate sparing)
- **CSF otorrhea/rhinorrhea:** Clear drainage from ear or nose = possible CSF leak; important for infection prevention

## 7. Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

Performed **30 minutes after injury** (or after initial resuscitation). Score ranges from 3-15.

| Component | Response | Score |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye Opening** | Spontaneous | 4 |
| | To speech | 3 |
| | To pain | 2 |
| | No response | 1 |
| **Best Verbal** | Oriented (time, place, person) | 5 |
| | Confused | 4 |
| | Inappropriate words | 3 |
| | Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| | No response | 1 |
| **Best Motor** | Obeys commands | 6 |
| | Moves to localized pain | 5 |
| | Flexion withdrawal from pain | 4 |
| | Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| | Abnormal extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| | No response | 1 |

### TBI Severity Classification
- **Mild TBI (concussion):** GCS 13-15
- **Moderate TBI:** GCS 9-12
- **Severe TBI:** GCS 3-8 (comatose = 8 or less)

**Limitations:** Intubation/sedation, intoxication, and emotional response to trauma can complicate scoring.

## 8. Evaluation, Management, and Prognosis

### Mild TBI (Concussion)
**Acute signs/symptoms:** Confusion, amnesia, headache, dizziness, nausea, tinnitus. Loss of consciousness is **not required.** GCS 13-15.

**Post-concussive symptoms:** Headache, dizziness, sleep impairment, fatigue, anxiety/irritability, difficulty concentrating.

**Management:**
- Acutely: observation, rest
- Gradual return to activities (no evidence for full physical/cognitive rest)
- Treat anxiety/depression and headache (often migraine or tension-type features)

**Prognosis:**
- Majority resolves within 7-10 days
- Minority develop post-concussive syndrome with prolonged symptoms; most resolve within ~3 months
- Risk of prolonged symptoms higher with: repeated concussions, female sex, pre-morbid stress/anxiety/depression

### Severe TBI
**Signs:** More severe mechanism, prolonged LOC (>1 min), may remain unconscious, neurologic deficits, post-traumatic seizures. GCS 3-8.

**Management:**
- Hospital admission (Neuro-ICU)
- Neuroprotective measures: closely monitor blood pressure, ICP, temperature
- Infection prevention (especially with open/basilar skull fractures)
- Seizure prevention (risk proportional to cortical damage; delayed seizures can occur months later)
- **Treat raised ICP:**
  - Hypertonic fluids (hypertonic saline 3%/23%, mannitol)
  - Neurosurgical intervention:
    - **EVD (External Ventricular Drain):** Placed in lateral ventricle to monitor ICP and drain CSF
    - **Hematoma evacuation:** For epidural, large subdural, or large intracerebral hemorrhages
    - **Hemicraniectomy:** Removal of part of skull to allow swelling

**Sequelae:** Frontal lobe signs (impulsivity, disinhibition, apathy), processing deficits, neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, anxiety, aggression), hemiparesis (UMN pattern), post-traumatic epilepsy.

**Prognosis:**
- Moderate TBI (GCS 9-12): 60% good recovery, 25% moderate disability, 5% severe disability, 10% death
- Severe TBI (GCS <8): ~1/3 good recovery, ~1/3 moderate-severe disability, ~1/3 death

## 9. Abusive Head Trauma

- Leading cause of death in US children <5; babies <1 year at greatest risk
- 25% of affected babies will die; nearly all survivors have sequelae (developmental delay, physical disabilities)
- Infants are vulnerable due to proportionally large head and poor head/neck control

### Recognition
- Story inconsistencies between injury and reported history
- Retinal hemorrhages (fundoscopic exam)
- Suspicious cutaneous bruising
- Subdural hemorrhages on brain imaging
- Skeletal survey for associated rib and long bone fractures

### Prevention
- Prenatal counseling
- Parent/caregiver education: inconsolable ("PURPLE") crying is normal; **never shake a baby**
- Screen for intimate partner violence and other abuse

---

## 10. Prevention Counseling

### Falls
- Remove loose rugs, use appropriate footwear and protective gear for sports
- Install grab bars/handrails for elderly
- Be cautious with fall-risk medications

### Motor Vehicle Accidents
- Always wear seatbelts
- Never drive under the influence
- Support infrastructure and education campaigns

### Abusive Head Trauma
- Educate caregivers about PURPLE crying and infant vulnerability to shaking

### Firearm Injuries
- Firearms involved in ~79% of homicides and ~53% of suicides
- Cranial gunshot wounds: >90% fatal
- **Counseling:** Safest to store firearms outside the home. If kept at home: unloaded, in a lockbox, ammunition locked separately.
- Firearm secure storage is a **suicide prevention strategy**
- 988 = Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 24/7)
