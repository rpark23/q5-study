## 1. Approach to the Chief Complaint of Problems Moving

The standard neurologic diagnostic framework applies to movement disorders:

1. **Localize on the y-axis:** Is the lesion central or peripheral?
   - Movement disorders in this lecture are **central** (brain-level)

2. **Localize on the x-axis:** More specifically, is it the pyramidal system or the extrapyramidal system (basal ganglia, cerebellum, thalamus, cortical loops)?

3. **Establish a time course:**

| Decline Pattern | Typical Etiology |
|---|---|
| Sudden step-down | Stroke, hemorrhage |
| Gradual stepwise | Demyelinating disease |
| Slow progressive | Degenerative (e.g., Huntington, spinocerebellar ataxia) |
| Relapsing-remitting | Demyelinating disease |
| Transient | TIA, seizure, migraine |

Movement disorders are **often neurodegenerative**, but can also result acutely from stroke affecting the cerebellum or basal ganglia.

## 2. Localizing Motor Disorders: Pyramidal vs. Extrapyramidal Systems

### Pyramidal System (Motor System)
- **Pathway:** Starts in the precentral gyrus (motor cortex), travels through the internal capsule and brainstem, crosses at the pyramidal decussation in the medulla (~80% of fibers), then descends as the corticospinal tracts
- **Function:** Voluntary muscle power and volitional movement
- **Disruption can occur at:** Motor cortex, subcortical white matter (internal capsule), brainstem, or spinal cord

**Signs of a pyramidal lesion (upper motor neuron signs):**
- Weakness (paralysis)
- Spastic muscle tone (velocity-dependent hypertonicity)
- Hyperreflexia

### Extrapyramidal System (Motor Modulation System)
- **Structures:** Basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus), substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus (STN), thalamus, red nucleus, reticular formation, cerebellum, pontine nuclei, inferior olivary nuclei, vestibular apparatus
- **Spinal output tracts:** Rubrospinal, tectospinal, vestibulospinal, olivospinal

**Functions of the extrapyramidal system:**
- Selective activation and suppression of movements
- Initiation of movement
- Setting rate and force of movements
- Coordination of movements in space and time
- Regulating learned/automatic movements (walking, handwriting)
- Regulation of muscle tone
- Control of posture and balance

**Signs of an extrapyramidal lesion (complex movement disorders):**

| Category | Signs |
|---|---|
| Abnormal rhythmic movement | Rest tremor, postural tremor, kinetic tremor |
| Paucity of movement | Bradykinesia, hypomimia, hypophonia |
| Excess of movement | Choreathetosis, ballism |
| Coordination issues | Dysmetria, ataxia, dysdiadochokinesia |

- **Bradykinesia:** slow movement
- **Hypomimia:** reduced facial expression
- **Hypophonia:** soft voice
- **Choreathetosis:** dance-like or writhing involuntary excess movements
- **Ballism:** more violent flinging excess movements
- **Ataxia:** general term for abnormal coordination
- **Dysmetria:** inability to reach a target smoothly (subtype of ataxia)
- **Dysdiadochokinesia:** inability to perform rapid alternating movements

## 3. Direct and Indirect Circuits of the Extrapyramidal System

The basal ganglia modulate movement through two main circuits. The key players:
- **Thalamus:** provides excitatory input to the cortex
- **GPi/SNr (globus pallidus interna / substantia nigra pars reticularis):** provide inhibitory input to the thalamus
- **SNc (substantia nigra pars compacta):** sends dopaminergic projections to the striatum

| Pathway | Net Effect on Thalamus | Net Effect on Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** (striatum inhibits GPi/SNr) | Less inhibition = more thalamic excitation | Pro-movement |
| **Indirect** (striatum inhibits GPe, releasing STN to excite GPi/SNr) | More inhibition = less thalamic excitation | Anti-movement |

**Conceptual shorthand:** Think of the direct and indirect pathways as a "volume control" on movement.
- Volume turned **up** (direct pathway dominant) = more movement, as in Huntington disease
- Volume turned **down** (indirect pathway dominant) = less movement, as in Parkinson's disease

## 4. Types of Increased Muscle Tone and Localization

| Tone | Localization | Description | Also Called |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rigidity** | Basal ganglia (fairly specific to parkinsonism) | Non-velocity dependent hypertonicity; increased tone throughout full range of movement | "Waxy," "lead-pipe" (+ tremor = "cogwheel") |
| **Spasticity** | Upper motor neuron lesions (pyramidal) | Velocity-dependent hypertonicity; resistance gives way suddenly | "Clasp-knife," "loading" range |
| **Hypotonia** | Usually peripheral nervous system | Less resistance than expected even at rest | "Floppy" |
| **Paratonia** | Cortical or frontal lobe dysfunction (or patient non-compliance) | Variable resistance or cooperation in all directions; not a true tone change | Highly variable |

- Extrapyramidal lesion: **rigidity**
- Pyramidal lesion: **spasticity**
- Peripheral nervous system lesion: **hypotonia**

## 5. Types of Tremor

When evaluating a tremor, assess: **where** (body region), **when** (rest, posture, or kinetic), and **frequency** (Hz).

| Disorder | Rest | Posture | Kinetic | Quality | Body Regions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parkinson's Disease** | +++ | + | + | Pill-rolling, 4-6 Hz | Hand (often unilateral then bilateral), +/- leg, jaw |
| **Essential Tremor** | + | +++ | ++ | Flexion-extension, 6-12 Hz | Hands (often bilateral), +/- whole head, voice |
| **Cerebellar disorders** | + | ++ | +++ | Coarse, zig-zag | Variable |

- **Intention tremor:** worsens as the limb nears a target; characteristic of essential tremor and cerebellar tremor
- **PD tremor:** classically a rest tremor; lower frequency and higher amplitude than essential tremor; jaw tremor if axial
- **ET tremor:** higher frequency; most severe with sustained posture; head tremor (yes-yes or no-no) and voice if axial
- **Cerebellar tremor:** coarse and irregular; kinetic > postural

## 6. Essential Tremor (ET)

### Presentation
- **Most common movement disorder** (8x more common than Parkinson's disease)
- Postural > kinetic tremor of hands; can involve head (yes-yes or no-no) and voice
- High frequency: 6-12 Hz
- Often positive family history: **autosomal dominant** with reduced penetrance
- **Attenuates with alcohol intake**
- Pathophysiology involves increased activity of the cerebellothalamocortical circuit (extrapyramidal circuitry)

### Treatment

**Pharmacologic (first-line):**
- **Propranolol** (Inderal) -- beta-blocker; contraindicated in asthma
- **Primidone** (Mysoline) -- also an anti-seizure medication

**Surgical:**
- **Focused ultrasound:** ablates part of the thalamus (VIM nucleus) unilaterally; non-invasive (no incision), results in one tremor-free hand
- **Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) in the thalamus (VIM):** electrode placed via burr hole, can be done bilaterally; more invasive but more versatile; can treat global tremor; contraindicated with cognitive impairment or advanced age

## 7. Cerebellar Disease

### Cerebellar Anatomy and Localization
- The cerebellum evaluates discrepancies between intention and action, then adjusts movements via the motor cortex and brainstem
- Comprises 10% of total brain volume but contains half of all brain neurons
- **Cerebellar output crosses twice** (to contralateral cortex, then cortex controls ipsilateral body): lesions cause **ipsilateral** deficits

| Lesion Location | Deficit |
|---|---|
| Cerebellar hemispheres | Ipsilateral limb ataxia |
| Vermis (center) | Truncal ataxia, nystagmus |

### Signs of Cerebellar Disease

**Appendicular (limb) findings:**
- **Dysmetria:** inability to smoothly reach a target (overshoot = hypermetria, undershoot = hypometria); tested with finger-nose-finger
- **Dysdiadochokinesia:** impaired rapid alternating movements (palm-up/palm-down test)

**Oculomotor findings:**
- **Nystagmus:** sustained, multidirectional; vertical nystagmus is nearly always central (not vestibular/ear)
- **Saccade abnormalities:** "eye dysmetria" -- overshooting or undershooting when shifting gaze

**Voice findings:**
- **Dysarthria:** slurred speech
- **Scanning speech:** loss of normal prosody (arrhythmic, monotone)

**Gait findings:**
- **Truncal titubation:** bobbing of the trunk
- **Wide-based gait,** sometimes staggering
- Difficulty with tandem walk (heel-to-toe)

### Common Etiologies of Ataxia

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Acquired | Stroke, multiple sclerosis, alcohol (vermis atrophy leading to truncal ataxia) |
| Hereditary/neurodegenerative | Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs), Friedreich's ataxia, Fragile X tremor/ataxia syndrome |
| Toxic/metabolic | Ethanol, heavy metals, phenytoin |
| Paraneoplastic | Anti-Yo antibodies with ovarian/breast cancer or SCLC |
| Autoimmune | Multiple sclerosis, gluten enteropathy |
| Infectious | Viral encephalitis, HIV, Whipple's disease |
| Structural | Stroke, tumor, Dandy-Walker malformation |

### Diagnostic Approach
- **Categorize onset:** acute (stroke), subacute (MS flare), or chronic (genetic syndrome, alcohol)
- **Workup:** MRI (pattern of atrophy, structural lesions) and genetic testing when hereditary etiology is suspected
- **Treatment:** Largely supportive (PT/OT); treat underlying cause when possible; emerging antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) therapies in pipeline for genetic forms (similar to Huntington)

## 8. Huntington Disease

### Genetics
- **Autosomal dominant**; CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion on **chromosome 4** in the gene encoding "huntingtin"
- Age of onset inversely related to number of repeats (more repeats = earlier onset)

| CAG Repeats | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| >40 | Definite disease |
| 27-39 | Intermediate alleles (36-39 highest risk; may have behavioral abnormalities) |
| <26 | Normal, no risk |

- Paternal transmission is meiotically unstable and prone to repeat expansion in offspring
- ~25,000 cases in the US; more prevalent in European/Caucasian ancestry (5.7/100,000 in Europe/North America/Australia vs. 0.38/100,000 worldwide) due to smaller baseline CAG repeat counts in lower-prevalence populations

### Clinical Triad

**1. Motor: progressive hyperkinetic movements**
- **Chorea:** involuntary, dance-like excess movements (from the Greek for "dance")
- Slow loss of voluntary motor control over time
- Late-stage chorea can transition into a parkinsonian, akinetic-rigid state
- **Motor impersistence:** inability to sustain a motor command (e.g., "milkmaids grip" -- squeeze/release/squeeze/release)

**2. Neuropsychiatric symptoms**
- Early: depression, anxiety, irritability, apathy
- Later: paranoia, delusions, disinhibition (road rage)
- Suicide risk: **5-10x** the general population (~7% for affected individuals)

**3. Cognitive: progressive decline**
- Early: executive dysfunction (decision-making, task switching, multitasking)
- Late: memory loss, eventual dementia (near-universal)

### Pathophysiology
- Loss of **medium spiny neurons** in the **caudate** (and putamen) due to accumulation of toxic mutant huntingtin protein
- These neurons normally dampen motor activity; their loss shifts the balance toward the **direct pathway** (pro-movement)
- Net result: less inhibition of thalamus, more thalamic excitation of cortex = **excess movement (chorea)**
- Neuronal and synaptic loss from the cortex and caudate also drives cognitive changes
- **Gross pathology:** cerebral atrophy and atrophy of the basal ganglia (especially concave caudate on coronal imaging)

### Management

| Symptom | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Chorea | VMAT2 inhibitors (tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, valbenazine); antipsychotics (risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole) |
| Depression/anxiety | SSRIs; supportive care |
| Dementia | Supportive care (acetylcholinesterase inhibitors not effective) |
| Function | PT/OT for prolongation of community living |
| Planning | Advanced care planning; caregiver support |

- VMAT2 inhibitors work by dopamine depletion; can worsen depression
- Antipsychotics block dopamine transmission and can benefit both chorea and psychiatric symptoms

### Frontiers
- Gene silencing approaches: **antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)** and RNA interference to reduce mutant huntingtin expression
  - Shown to decrease huntingtin concentration; clinical efficacy not yet established in early trials
- Splicing modifiers and monoclonal antibodies under investigation
- No disease-modifying therapy currently approved; treatment remains symptomatic
