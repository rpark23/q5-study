## 1. Definition of Coma

**Consciousness** = arousability + responsiveness to stimuli (auditory, tactile, visual). It can be disrupted by chemical/electrical disturbances, insufficient oxygen or blood flow, or elevated intracranial pressure (ICP).

**Coma** = absence of BOTH arousal AND awareness ("unarousable unresponsiveness," Plum & Posner). It is a **medical emergency** representing advanced brain failure.

A comatose person:
- Cannot be awakened (eyes closed)
- Fails to respond normally to pain or light
- Has no sleep-wake cycles
- Takes no voluntary actions

### Spectrum of Consciousness

Terms are imprecise; it is better to describe the exam directly (e.g., "drowsy but arousable and opens eyes briefly to loud voice, does not follow commands, grimaces to sternal rub").

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Drowsy | More sleepy than normal |
| Lethargic / Obtunded / Stuporous | Depressed consciousness but responds to some stimulation |
| Comatose | Unarousable and unresponsive |
| Delirium | Waxing and waning mental status with always-impaired attention; can range from agitated to hypoactive |
| Metabolic encephalopathy | Non-structural confusional state/delirium/coma (no mass lesion) |

## 2. Coma Mimics

These states are **not coma** but may appear similar:

- **Persistent vegetative state (PVS):** Patient opens eyes and arouses to stimuli but has no awareness of environment; can have sleep-wake cycles and primitive reflexes. Not coma. Poor prognosis if >3 months (non-traumatic) or >12 months (traumatic).
- **Locked-in syndrome:** Paralyzed but conscious and with intact sensation. Lesion of bilateral ventral pons (corticobulbar + corticospinal tracts); vertical eye movements may be preserved (midbrain spared).
- **Complete peripheral nerve paralysis:** Cognitively intact but paralyzed (e.g., Guillain-Barre Syndrome, botulism).
- **Post-ictal state after seizure:** Minimally responsive/difficult to arouse after a seizure.
- **Abulia/akinetic mutism:** Minimal responsiveness from large frontal lobe dysfunction.
- **Psychogenic unresponsiveness:** e.g., catatonia from psychiatric illness.
- **Minimally conscious state:** Some reproducible non-reflexive behavior (fixation, simple command-following) indicating conscious perception; not vegetative.
- **Brain death = death:** Irreversible loss of all cortical, subcortical, and brainstem function. Requires: (1) coma, (2) absent brainstem reflexes, (3) failed apnea test — evaluated by two independent medical parties at separate time points.

## 3. Simon's Rule of 4: Approach to Coma Evaluation

Simon's Rule of 4 is a quick screen to identify the lesion and initiate management. The standard neurologic exam is modified because comatose patients cannot report symptoms; mental status is assessed by response to stimuli.

### (1) First Things to Do
- ABCs; intubate if needed
- Give **thiamine 100mg IV first**, then dextrose (D50); consider naloxone (opioid reversal), flumazenil (benzodiazepine reversal)
- Blood and urine labs; protect the neck; look for body trauma

> **Why thiamine before dextrose?** Thiamine (B1) is a cofactor for glucose metabolism. Giving glucose to a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy.

### (2) Two Localizations of Coma

| Localization | Anatomy | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Ascending reticular activating system (ARAS)** | Network of neurons in the **upper pons and midbrain**; projects to the diencephalon | Lesions here directly impair arousal/alertness |
| **Bilateral diffuse cerebral hemispheric dysfunction** | Both cerebral hemispheres (or bilateral thalami, which relay to hemispheres) | A unilateral lesion can also cause coma if large enough to exert remote effects on the contralateral hemisphere or brainstem |

### (3) Three Etiologies of Coma

| Etiology | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Non-structural (metabolic)** | No visible lesion; diffuse neuronal dysfunction. Hallmark: **intact pupils, intact CNs, symmetric motor exam** (though many exceptions exist) |
| **Structural (lesional)** | Focal or associated with a physical lesion; can be symmetric or asymmetric. Structural causes often produce **focal neurologic deficits** |
| **Electrical (seizures/status epilepticus)** | Diagnosed by history and EEG; treated with anti-seizure medications |

Mnemonics for AMS/coma etiologies: **MOVE STUPID** or **I MIND THE VIP** (lists of disease categories, not required to memorize).

### (4) Four Things to Examine

**(i) Pupils (CN II afferent, CN III efferent)**
- Tests midbrain function
- Normal: equal, round, and reactive to light bilaterally
- Parasympathetic (PANS) fibers run superficially on CN III; compression of CN III causes ipsilateral pupil dilation ("blown pupil") and loss of constriction
- A full CN III palsy also causes the eye to deviate "down and out" (unopposed CN IV/VI)

**(ii) Doll's Eyes / Oculocephalic Reflex (OCR) and Cold Calorics (CN VIII afferent, CN III/VI efferent)**
- Tests pons to midbrain
- **Normal OCR:** Turn head one way; eyes move in the opposite direction (like an old-fashioned weighted-eye doll)
- **Metabolic coma:** OCR intact
- **Brainstem lesion:** OCR absent or dysconjugate
- Awake patients can override OCR with fixation; only perform if cervical spine is stable
- If OCR untestable: **cold calorics** — infuse 50cc ice water into one ear; normal response in coma is slow conjugate deviation of both eyes toward the ice; absent or dysconjugate response suggests brainstem pathology

**(iii) Response to Pain (CN V afferent, CN VII/corticospinal efferent)**
- Tests pons and primary sensorimotor tracts
- Apply supraorbital pressure, trapezius pinch, sternal rub, or nailbed pressure
- Responses ranked best to worst: **localizes > withdraws > grimaces > posturing > no response**

**Posturing:**
- **Decorticate (flexor):** Arms flexed, legs extended. Lesion **in or above midbrain** (at/above red nucleus). Bad.
- **Decerebrate (extensor):** Arms and legs extended. Lesion **below red nucleus** (pons/medulla). Worse.

**(iv) Meningeal Signs**
- **Kernig's sign:** Flex hip 90 degrees; extending the knee causes pain
- **Brudzinski's sign:** Flex neck; hips and knees reflexively bend
- Presence suggests meningeal irritation (infection, subarachnoid blood, leptomeningeal carcinomatosis)

## 4. Localization of Coma

Coma localizes to: **(i) ARAS** (upper pons/midbrain) or **(ii) bilateral diffuse hemispheres** (or bilateral thalami).

### Brainstem Localization Using Cranial Nerves

| CN Involved | Level |
|---|---|
| CN II, III, IV | Midbrain |
| CN V | Pons |
| CN VI, VII, VIII, IX | Pons to medulla junction |
| CN X, XI, XII | Medulla / lower brainstem |

**Posturing level:**
- Decorticate: lesion at or above midbrain
- Decerebrate: lesion below midbrain (pons/medulla)

### "Crossed Signs" = Brainstem Localization

Ipsilateral CN deficit + contralateral hemibody UMN signs = lesion in the **brainstem** where sensorimotor tracts decussate.

**Case 1 example:** Left blown pupil (CN III) + left eye laterally deviated (CN III palsy) + right decorticate posturing (left CST) + right Babinski = **left midbrain lesion**.

This pattern is consistent with **left uncal herniation** compressing the left midbrain: the left uncus herniates over the edge of the tentorium cerebelli, compressing CN III (blown pupil, down-and-out eye), the left corticospinal tracts (right UMN signs), and the ARAS (coma). Hemorrhages within the compressed midbrain = **Duret hemorrhages**.

## 5. Etiologies of Structural vs. Non-Structural Coma

### Structural Causes

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Symmetric** | Diffuse axonal injury (DAI), SAH, hydrocephalus, venous sinus thrombosis, bilateral thalamic strokes, basilar artery occlusion, midline brainstem lesion |
| **Asymmetric** | Mass lesion (tumor, stroke, ICH, EDH, SDH, abscess) with herniation; brainstem infarct/hemorrhage |

### Non-Structural (Metabolic) Causes

- Drugs/anesthesia (barbiturates, benzodiazepines, opiates)
- Toxins (alcohol, lead, cyanide, CO)
- Metabolic: DKA, hyperosmolar non-ketotic coma, electrolyte disturbances (Na, Ca, Mg, pH), uremia, liver failure, thiamine B1 deficiency, cobalamin B12 deficiency, thyroid dysfunction
- Hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoperfusion/shock, hypo/hyperthermia, cardiopulmonary arrest
- Meningitis

### Electrical Causes
- Seizures/status epilepticus (convulsive or non-convulsive) or prolonged post-ictal state
- Diagnosed with EEG; treated with anti-seizure medications

## 6. Brainstem Localization via Cranial Nerve Deficits

Use the cranial nerve deficits identified on the Simon's Rule of 4 coma exam to pinpoint the brainstem level (midbrain vs. pons vs. medulla):

| Exam Finding | What Is Tested | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Pupils | CN II (afferent), CN III (efferent), Edinger-Westphal nucleus | Midbrain |
| Doll's eyes / cold calorics | CN VIII, CN III, CN VI | Midbrain to pons |
| Response to pain (grimace) | CN V (afferent), CN VII (efferent) | Pons |
| Decorticate posturing | Functional separation at red nucleus | At/above midbrain |
| Decerebrate posturing | Functional separation below red nucleus | Pons/medulla |

## 7. Neuropathology Correlates of AMS/Coma

### Wernicke's Encephalopathy (B1/Thiamine Deficiency)

**Classic triad:** ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + confusion/coma

- Common in alcohol dependence and malnutrition (cancer, GI surgery, TPN patients)
- Do not miss: treatable, and if untreated leads to **irreversible Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome** (severe anterograde and retrograde amnesia, confabulation) or death
- **MRI (FLAIR):** Bright signal in mammillary bodies, periaqueductal midbrain, periventricular region of 3rd ventricle
- **Gross pathology:** Hemorrhagic lesions of mammillary bodies
- **Rx:** 100-300mg thiamine IV/day; always give thiamine **before** dextrose

### Alcohol (EtOH) Toxicity

- Cerebellar degeneration in 1% of chronic users: atrophy of the **superior cerebellar vermis**, cell loss in cerebellar folia
- Clinical: truncal instability, wide-based ataxic gait, slurred speech, disinhibition
- Severe intoxication: coma, potentially fatal respiratory depression
- Withdrawal extreme: **delirium tremens** (agitated delirium, tremors, tachycardia, hyperthermia, hallucinations, seizures); typically 12-72 hours after last consumption

### B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency

- Causes **subacute combined degeneration**: myelopathy (dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal tracts), peripheral neuropathy, neuropsychiatric symptoms, macrocytic anemia
- **MRI:** Bright signal in dorsal columns on T2 sagittal spinal cord
- **Pathology:** Vacuolated myelin (demyelination) in dorsal columns (sensory) and lateral corticospinal tracts (motor)
- **Dx:** Serum B12 low, homocysteine elevated, methylmalonic acid (MMA) elevated (MMA is more specific than homocysteine)
- **Rx:** IM B12 1000mcg daily x 1 week, then weekly x 1 month, then monthly

### Cerebellum Review

- **Midline (vermis/flocculonodular) lesion:** Truncal ataxia, wide-based staggering gait
- **Lateral hemisphere lesion:** Ipsilateral limb (appendicular) ataxia (fibers are double crossed, so left cerebellar lesion = left limb ataxia)

## 8. Initial Evaluation and Management of AMS/Coma

### Evaluation

1. **ABCs** with intubation if needed; monitor vitals
2. **Thiamine 100mg** before dextrose (D50); consider naloxone, flumazenil
3. Protect neck; examine for body trauma; perform quick Simon's coma exam
4. **Labs:** CBC, electrolytes, liver function, ABG, tox screen, EtOH level
5. Investigate suicide attempts/toxins; consider gastric lavage
6. **Urgent head CT** — must rule out a mass before LP because LP in a patient with a mass or posterior fossa crowding can precipitate fatal herniation
7. If no obvious cause and seizures suspected: **EEG** for status epilepticus (including non-convulsive)

### Treatment

| Etiology | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Non-structural (metabolic)** | Treat underlying cause; if meningeal signs/fever: empiric antibiotics/antivirals; DVT/PE prophylaxis; excellent nursing care |
| **Structural** | Neurosurgical options (drain, shunt, biopsy, evacuation, resection); for acute ischemic stroke: recanalization (IV tPA/TNK, endovascular); for elevated ICP: head of bed 30 degrees, mannitol 1g/kg or hypertonic (3%) saline, consider dexamethasone (not for ischemic stroke), consider ICP monitoring; DVT/PE prophylaxis |
| **Electrical** | Anti-seizure medications; EEG-guided management |

> **Key principle:** A newly comatose patient is a medical emergency. Rapid diagnosis and management can prevent permanent brain injury or death.
