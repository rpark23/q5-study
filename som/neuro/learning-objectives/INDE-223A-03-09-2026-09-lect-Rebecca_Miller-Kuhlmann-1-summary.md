## Overview

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a **progressive neurodegenerative condition** characterized by:
- Increasing prevalence with advancing age
- Accumulation of **alpha-synuclein** (misfolded protein) in dopaminergic neurons
- Both **motor** (movement) and **non-motor** manifestations
- It is the **second most common neurodegenerative disease** after Alzheimer's disease

---

## 1. Epidemiology and Risk Factors

### Epidemiology
- **Lifetime risk:** ~2% for males, ~1.3% for females (aged 40+) in the USA
- **Prevalence:** ~1 million in the US; ~5 million worldwide
- Affects **1 in 100** people over age 60; 60,000 new US cases per year
- Risk **increases substantially with age**
- **Higher incidence in male** patients (unlike Alzheimer's which is female-predominant)
- Climate change data suggests warming may increase PD prevalence independent of aging

### Risk Factors

| Category | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| Environmental | Pesticide/solvent/air pollution exposure, high dairy consumption, vascular risk factors (DM, HTN), head injury, melanoma, methamphetamine use |
| Genetic | 5-10% of patients have a genetic etiology; 90% have no identifiable genetic cause; GBA gene variant (also linked to Gaucher disease) increases risk, especially in Ashkenazi Jewish patients |

### Protective Factors
- **Physical activity:** ~34% relative risk reduction with moderate-to-vigorous exercise; aerobic exercise may also slow progression
- **Smoking:** Risk decreases up to 70% with increased duration — not counseled to start, but important to understand the dose-response relationship (possible neuroprotection from nicotine)
- **Coffee/caffeine:** Lower PD risk, likely via adenosine A2a receptor blockade; black tea also protective

## 2. Motor and Non-Motor Symptoms

### Clinical Case: Trouble Walking
58-year-old woman with progressive difficulty walking over several years, "difficulty getting started," one fall while turning. Exam: increased tone in right UE (not velocity-dependent), rest tremor (right > left, diminishes with voluntary activity), retropulsion on pull test, hesitancy in gait initiation, shuffling, decreased arm swing. This presentation illustrates the cardinal features of PD.

### Cardinal Motor Features: TRAP
**Diagnosis requires bradykinesia PLUS at least one of: tremor OR rigidity**

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **T**remor | Rest tremor ("pill-rolling"), ~4-6 Hz, often asymmetric; diminishes with voluntary movement |
| **R**igidity | Increased muscle tone that is NOT velocity-dependent (distinguishes from spasticity) |
| **A**kinesia/Bradykinesia | Slowness and reduced amplitude of movement; required for diagnosis |
| **P**ostural instability | Develops later in disease; causes falls; early falls are a red flag |

- Onset is **asymmetric** and the disease is **progressive**
- Up to 1/3 of patients may initially not have tremor, though ~90% develop it eventually
- Postural instability develops later — **recurrent falls within 3 years of diagnosis** is a red flag suggesting an alternative diagnosis

### Additional Motor Features
- **Hypomimia** (masked facies, decreased blink rate)
- **Hypophonia** (soft speech)
- **Dysphagia** (bradykinesia of swallow musculature)
- **Micrographia** (handwriting becomes small)
- **Gait abnormalities:** shuffling, freezing of gait, en bloc turning, reduced arm swing
- Hand incoordination (from bradykinesia, not cerebellar)

### Non-Motor Features ("The Parkinson's Iceberg")
The non-motor symptoms are often hidden and easily missed:

| Category | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Autonomic | Orthostatic hypotension, urinary urgency, constipation, sexual dysfunction, sweating/temperature dysregulation |
| Sensory/Sleep | Hyposmia (loss of smell — often precedes motor symptoms), REM-sleep behavioral disorder (acting out dreams) |
| Psychiatric | Depression, anxiety (SSRIs can help — ask explicitly), visual hallucinations, impulse control disorders |
| Cognitive | Bradyphrenia, memory difficulty; can progress to Parkinson's Disease with Dementia (PDD) |

- Loss of smell often precedes the disease by years (not specific to PD, but common in neurodegeneration)
- Visual hallucinations are not uncommon later in PD; the cognitive pattern that develops mirrors Lewy Body Disease

## 3. Parkinsonism vs. Parkinson's Disease

- **Parkinsonism** = a syndrome of bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity, without specifying cause
- **Parkinson's disease** = one specific neurodegenerative **cause** of parkinsonism

### Other Causes of Parkinsonism
| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Drug-induced** | Anti-dopaminergic (antipsychotic) medications, especially first-generation (e.g., haloperidol); typically symmetric |
| **Vascular parkinsonism** | Strokes/ischemia to basal ganglia; may be non-progressive |
| **Lewy Body Dementia (LBD)** | Cognitive symptoms precede or occur within 1 year of motor symptoms |
| **Atypical Parkinsonism / Parkinson's Plus** | PSP (vertical gaze palsy, frontal-predominant cognition), MSA (severe autonomic dysfunction), CBD (apraxia, sensorimotor issues) — not tested in detail |

### LBD vs. PDD (1-Year Rule)
| | LBD | PDD |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Dementia precedes or occurs within **1 year** of motor symptoms | Motor symptoms present for **at least 1 year** before dementia |
| Shared features | Both involve alpha-synuclein/Lewy bodies; visuospatial and executive dysfunction > memory loss; visual hallucinations common |

- 10-35% of new PD diagnoses have mild cognitive impairment at baseline
- By 20 years after PD onset, ~80% have developed dementia
- Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine) can help treat hallucinations in both conditions

## 4. Neuroanatomy, Neuropathology, and Neurochemistry

### Key Anatomy
- **Substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc)** in the midbrain: the primary site of dopaminergic neuronal loss in PD
- SNc neurons project via the **nigrostriatal pathway** to the **striatum** (caudate + putamen) of the basal ganglia
- Over **80% of dopaminergic cells are lost before motor symptoms appear** — the brain compensates until this threshold is crossed
- End result of dopamine deficiency: **inhibition of normal movement**

### Basal Ganglia Circuitry in PD

**Normal circuitry — SNc excites direct pathway and inhibits indirect pathway, net effect = movement:**
- **Direct pathway** (pro-movement): Striatum inhibits GPi/SNr → GPi/SNr inhibits thalamus → thalamus excites cortex. Net: double inhibition = excitation of motor cortex
- **Indirect pathway** (anti-movement): Striatum inhibits GPe → GPe inhibits STN → STN excites GPi/SNr → GPi/SNr inhibits thalamus. Net: inhibition of motor cortex

**In PD — SNc output is lost:**
- Loss of SNc turns DOWN the excitatory direct pathway
- Loss of SNc turns UP the inhibitory indirect pathway
- Net result: **less excitatory input to the motor cortex** → paucity of movement

### Neuropathology
- **Alpha-synuclein** is the pathologic misfolded protein that accumulates in dopaminergic neurons
- **Lewy bodies** are round intraneuronal inclusions composed of alpha-synuclein; they are the histological hallmark of PD (and LBD)
- On gross pathology: **pallor of the substantia nigra** (normally dark due to neuromelanin in dopaminergic neurons)

### Braak Staging (Synuclein Spread)
| Braak Stages | Location | Clinical Correlate |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Medulla, pontine tegmentum, olfactory bulb | Non-motor symptoms: GI, autonomic, sleep, smell (pre-clinical) |
| 3-4 | Substantia nigra, midbrain, forebrain | **Motor symptoms develop** — leads to diagnosis |
| 5-6 | Cortex | Cognitive difficulties, mood changes (usually years into diagnosis) |

- Pathologic change **precedes motor symptoms by years to decades** (up to 40-45 years in some studies)
- LBD involves earlier/more prominent cortical involvement, which is why cognitive symptoms present first

### Neurochemistry
- Tyrosine → (tyrosine hydroxylase) → **Levodopa** → (AADC) → **Dopamine**
- Dopamine is broken down by two enzymes: **COMT** and **MAO-B**
- **Levodopa can cross the blood-brain barrier; dopamine cannot** — this is why levodopa is used therapeutically
- Additional neurotransmitters affected in PD:
  - **Norepinephrine** depletion (locus ceruleus) → autonomic dysfunction
  - **Serotonin** depletion → depression/anxiety
  - **Increased acetylcholine** turnover → may contribute to dementia

### Diagnosis

**Clinical criteria (all required):**
1. Bradykinesia (required)
2. Tremor OR rigidity (at least one required)
3. Progression over time
4. Symptoms respond to dopaminergic medications

**Red flags (question the diagnosis if present within 5 years):**
- Requires wheelchair
- No progression of motor symptoms
- Severe dysphonia/dysphagia or autonomic failure early
- Absence of non-motor symptoms
- Recurrent falls within 3 years of diagnosis
- Bilateral symmetric parkinsonism

**DaT scan (SPECT imaging):**
- Radiotracer binds dopamine transporters; positive scan shows "period shape" (loss of uptake in putamen > caudate)
- Supports but does not replace clinical diagnosis
- Most useful in atypical cases; negative in essential tremor, vascular, and medication-induced parkinsonism

## 5. Medical Treatment of Parkinson's Disease

### Overview
- All current therapies are **symptomatic only** — they do not slow or stop disease progression
- Research goals: neuroprotective (prevent PD) and neurorestorative (reverse pathology) treatments

### When to Start Pharmacologic Treatment
Let patient values drive the decision. Consider:
- Are symptoms interfering with work/ADLs?
- Is there social stigma (e.g., visible tremor)?
- Are dominant hand or gait affected?
- Disease progresses whether or not we "save" treatments for later — no benefit to delaying

### Dopaminergic Medications

| Class | Mechanism | Examples | Key Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbidopa-Levodopa** | Levodopa is converted to dopamine in brain; carbidopa prevents peripheral conversion (reducing nausea, increasing CNS delivery) | Sinemet (carbidopa 25/levodopa 100), IR and CR formulations | Most potent agent; motor fluctuations and dyskinesias develop after 5-10 years; therapeutic window narrows with disease progression |
| **Dopamine agonists** | Directly stimulate post-synaptic dopamine receptors | Pramipexole (Mirapex), ropinirole (Requip), rotigotine patch (Neupro), apomorphine (Apokyn) | Fewer motor fluctuations than levodopa but MORE psychiatric side effects |
| **MAO-B inhibitors** | Block breakdown of dopamine in the synaptic cleft | Selegiline, rasagiline (Azilect) | Symptomatic only (early neuroprotection claims not confirmed) |
| **COMT inhibitors** | Block enzymatic breakdown of levodopa/dopamine; prolongs levodopa effect | Entacapone, tolcapone (Stalevo = carbidopa + levodopa + entacapone) | Only effective as adjunct to levodopa, not with dopamine agonists alone |
| **Amantadine** | Stimulates dopamine release; also used to help calm dyskinesias | Amantadine | Mechanism less well understood |

### Side Effect Comparison: Levodopa vs. Dopamine Agonists

| Side Effect | Higher risk with Levodopa | Higher risk with DA Agonists |
|---|---|---|
| Dyskinesias | X | |
| Motor fluctuations | X | |
| Dopamine dysregulation syndrome | X | |
| Edema | | X |
| Somnolence/sleep attacks | | X |
| Impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive eating) | | X |
| Hallucinations/confusion | | X |
| Nausea | | X |

### Levodopa Therapeutic Window Over Time
- **Early disease:** smooth, extended response; dyskinesias absent or infrequent
- **Moderate disease:** wearing-off phenomena; increased dyskinesias
- **Advanced disease:** short, unpredictable on/off responses; "on" time associated with dyskinesias
- The dyskinesia threshold drops and the efficacy threshold rises as disease progresses, making dosing increasingly difficult

### Non-Motor Symptom Treatment
- Constipation: water, dried fruit, stool softeners
- REM-sleep behavioral disorder: melatonin
- Depression/anxiety: SSRIs (ask explicitly — patients may not volunteer)
- Urinary urgency: alpha-blockers (use cautiously given orthostasis risk in PD)

### Non-Pharmacologic Treatment
- **Exercise** (most evidence-based non-pharmacologic treatment):
  - Multiple RCTs support improvement in gait, speed, balance, quality of life
  - Aerobic exercise specifically associated with decreased risk of developing PD and may slow progression
  - Dance (tango), tai chi, walking training have highest evidence for symptom improvement
- **Cueing and assistive devices:** laser cane for freezing, musical cueing, U-turns instead of pivoting
- **Speech therapy** and **swallow therapy**
- Rationale: basal ganglia modulates automatic movements; external cues reroute through non-automatic pathways

## 6. Surgical and Procedural Treatments

### Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)
- High-frequency pulsatile current from electrode implanted in brain; mimics functional ablation
- Targets: **subthalamic nucleus (STN)** or **globus pallidus interna (GPi)**
- Net effect of either target: **decrease thalamic inhibition** → allow more thalamic excitation of motor cortex → rebalance circuitry to resemble normal
- Think of DBS as **electrical rebalancing** (vs. chemical rebalancing with medications)

**DBS is effective for:**
- Tremors, rigidity, bradykinesia, dyskinesias
- NOT a cure; does not alter disease progression; does NOT improve balance

**Good DBS candidates:**
- Symptoms present for >5 years (strong diagnosis of PD, not atypical)
- Still responds to levodopa but with troublesome on/off fluctuations OR classic tremor unresponsive to medication
- No significant cognitive impairment
- Good social supports and realistic surgical goals

**Avoid DBS in:**
- Significant cognitive impairment
- Advanced age (risk/benefit shifts; rarely performed after age 80)
- Patients whose primary disability is balance (DBS will not help this)

### Newer Therapies (toward smoother dopamine coverage)
- **Sinemet CR, Rytary, Crexont:** Extended-release carbidopa-levodopa formulations to smooth out dopamine levels
- **Duopa:** Continuous carbidopa-levodopa infusion via J-tube; for patients with on/off who are not DBS candidates
- **Vyalev (2024):** Carbidopa-levodopa subcutaneous pump

### In the Pipeline
- **Gene implantation:** Promising and appears safe in preliminary trials (Phase II); not yet proven effective
- **Stem cell transplantation:** Some benefit seen but also worsening dyskinesias in some; on hold, active research
- **DBS variations:** New stimulation types and brain targets
- **Disease-modifying pharmacotherapies:** In clinical trials

## Summary Table: Therapies for PD

| Approach | Drugs |
|---|---|
| Directly provide dopamine precursor | Carbidopa-levodopa (Sinemet) |
| Dopamine agonist | Ropinirole, pramipexole, rotigotine, apokyn |
| Inhibit dopamine breakdown (COMT) | Entacapone, tolcapone |
| Inhibit dopamine breakdown (MAO-B) | Selegiline, rasagiline |
| Stimulate dopamine release / reduce dyskinesias | Amantadine |
| Rebalance basal ganglia circuitry directly | DBS (STN or GPi) |

## Practice Question Key Concepts

- **TRAP** = Tremor (resting), Rigidity, Akinesia/Bradykinesia, Postural instability — these are the cardinal features; **hyperreflexia is NOT a feature of PD** (reflexes are normal)
- Primary site of dopaminergic neuronal loss = **substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc)**
- Histological hallmark = **Lewy bodies containing alpha-synuclein**
- Motor symptoms first appear in **Braak stages 3-4**
- Carbidopa is combined with levodopa to **prevent peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine** (reducing side effects, increasing CNS delivery)
- Dopamine agonists vs. levodopa = **fewer dyskinesias, more psychiatric side effects**
- **1-year rule:** LBD = dementia precedes or occurs within 1 year of motor onset; PDD = motor symptoms present for at least 1 year before dementia
- DBS targets = **STN and GPi**; MAO-B inhibitors = **inhibit dopamine breakdown in synaptic cleft**
