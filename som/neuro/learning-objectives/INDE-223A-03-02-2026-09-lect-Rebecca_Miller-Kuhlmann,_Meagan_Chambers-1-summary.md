## 1. Define dementia and differentiate it from normal aging, MCI, and delirium

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Normal aging** | Some slowing of processing speed and word-finding difficulty; no impairment in daily function |
| **Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)** | Cognitive decline greater than expected for age but does NOT significantly impair daily functioning |
| **Dementia (Major Neurocognitive Disorder)** | Significant decline in one or more cognitive domains that interferes with daily independence |
| **Delirium** | Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and cognition, usually reversible |

### MCI vs Dementia
- **MCI:** Cognitive symptoms are present and greater than expected for age, but the person can still manage their daily activities independently. MCI can progress to dementia (not always).
- **Dementia:** The cognitive decline is severe enough to impair daily function. The person loses independence.

### Delirium vs Dementia

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Acute | Chronic |
| Course | Waxing/Waning | Slowly progressive |
| Duration | Days to weeks | Months to years |
| Attention | Impaired | Often relatively normal until late |
| Reversible? | Usually | Rarely |

- Delirium is commonly triggered by an underlying medical condition (e.g., infection, medication, metabolic disturbance). Dementia is a chronic, progressive disease.
- Patients with dementia are at high risk for delirium when acutely ill.

## 2. Describe the cognitive domains affected in dementia

There are six main cognitive domains. Dementia can affect one or more.

| Cognitive Domain | What it means | Example of impairment |
|---|---|---|
| **Memory** | Encoding and retrieving new information | Forgetting recent conversations |
| **Visuospatial** | Processing spatial relationships and navigating environments | Getting lost in familiar places; trouble copying a figure |
| **Language** | Naming, fluency, comprehension, and repetition | Word-finding difficulty; inability to name objects |
| **Executive function** | Planning, organizing, sequencing, multitasking | Trouble managing finances or following complex instructions |
| **Behavior / Social cognition** | Appropriate behavior, empathy, impulse control | Disinhibition, apathy, personality changes |
| **Motor** | Movement, praxis (learned skilled movements) | Apraxia (trouble with previously learned tasks like tying a tie) |

- **Function** is also assessed — impairment in daily activities is what separates dementia from MCI.
- **Key cognitive screening tools:** Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA / MOCA); a score of 26-30 is typically normal on MoCA.

### Clinical Cases (Overview)

- **Case 1** (Early memory loss, MOCA 25): Subtle short-term memory deficit — could be early AD or MCI
- **Case 2** (Behavioral changes, disinhibition): Likely bvFTD — behavior and executive domains first
- **Case 3** (Visuospatial, fluctuating, visual hallucinations): Likely DLB — visuospatial and fluctuating prominent
- **Case 4** (Progressive language loss): Primary progressive aphasia — language domain first
- **Case 5** (Rapid decline over 7 months, myoclonus): Prion disease (CJD)

## 3. Describe the most common dementias — clinical features and underlying pathology

### 3a. Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

#### Clinical Features
- Most common dementia; accounts for ~60-80% of dementia cases
- Typical presentation: **short-term memory loss** first, then spreads to other cognitive domains (language, visuospatial, executive)
- **Atypical presentations:** Posterior cortical atrophy (visuospatial first), logopenic aphasia (language first), frontal variant (executive/behavior first)
- Eventually loses independence; death typically 8-10 years after diagnosis

#### Epidemiology and Risk Factors
- Age is the biggest risk factor; prevalence doubles every 5 years after age 65
- **APOE e4** allele: most common genetic risk factor; increases risk ~3x per allele (heterozygous) and ~8-12x (homozygous)
- **APOE e2:** protective
- Rare early-onset familial AD: mutations in **APP**, **PSEN1**, or **PSEN2** genes — autosomal dominant
- Modifiable risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, sleep, physical inactivity, hearing loss, air pollution

#### Pathology (Neuropathology — Dr. Chambers)

**Gross findings:**
- **Cortical atrophy** — widened sulci and narrowed gyri, particularly in temporal and parietal lobes
- **Medial temporal lobe (hippocampal) atrophy** on MRI — a classic finding

**Two hallmark microscopic lesions:**

| Lesion | Protein | Location | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neuritic (senile) plaques** | Amyloid-beta (A-beta) | Extracellular | Dense core of A-beta surrounded by dystrophic neurites; identified by Congo red (pink/red), silver stains, or amyloid IHC |
| **Neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs)** | Hyperphosphorylated tau | Intraneuronal | Twisted filaments of tau inside neurons; identified by silver stains (Bielschowsky) or tau IHC |

- **Amyloid precursor protein (APP)** is cleaved by beta- and gamma-secretase to produce A-beta. In AD, A-beta accumulates abnormally.
- **Tau** normally stabilizes microtubules; in AD, it becomes hyperphosphorylated, detaches from microtubules, and aggregates into tangles.
- **Braak staging:** Describes the spread of NFTs through the brain in six stages (I-II: entorhinal; III-IV: limbic/hippocampal; V-VI: neocortex). Clinically relevant — matches the progression of symptoms.
- **Selective vulnerability:** Neurons in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus are first and most severely affected — explains why memory is the first symptom.
- **Congo red stain:** Amyloid plaques show apple-green birefringence under polarized light.

**Biomarkers:**
- **CSF:** decreased A-beta 1-42, increased tau, increased phospho-tau
- **PET:** amyloid PET (florbetapir/florbetaben) and tau PET can visualize pathology in vivo
- **Blood:** plasma p-tau 217 — emerging, high sensitivity/specificity for AD

#### Treatments
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine): symptomatic only; increase acetylcholine by blocking its breakdown
- **Memantine** (NMDA receptor antagonist): symptomatic; used in moderate-severe AD
- **Anti-amyloid immunotherapies** (lecanemab, donanemab): slow disease progression by clearing amyloid plaques; approved for early AD with confirmed amyloid pathology; major side effect is **ARIA** (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities — brain edema/microhemorrhages)

### 3b. Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD)

#### Overview
- Group of disorders caused by **frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD)**
- Affects frontal and temporal lobes primarily
- More common in **younger patients** (onset 45-65 years); often misdiagnosed as psychiatric disease early on
- Two main clinical syndromes:

#### Behavioral Variant FTD (bvFTD)
- **Core features:** Early personality/behavior changes — **disinhibition**, apathy, loss of empathy, repetitive/compulsive behaviors, dietary changes (overeating, carbohydrate craving)
- Executive dysfunction is prominent; memory is often relatively preserved early
- MRI: frontal and anterior temporal lobe atrophy

#### Primary Progressive Aphasia (PPA)
Language is the primary affected domain. Three variants:

| PPA Variant | Key Feature | Pathology |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonfluent/Agrammatic PPA** | Effortful, halting speech; agrammatism | Usually FTLD-tau |
| **Semantic PPA (Semantic Dementia)** | Loss of word meaning; fluent but empty; can't name objects; loss of semantic knowledge | Usually FTLD-TDP-43 |
| **Logopenic PPA** | Word-finding pauses; impaired repetition; often AD pathology | Usually AD (A-beta/tau) |

#### C9orf72 Variant: FTD + ALS
- Mutation in the **C9orf72** gene causes a combined FTD + ALS syndrome
- Most common genetic cause of both familial ALS and familial FTD

#### Pathology (Neuropathology — Dr. Chambers)

**Gross findings:**
- Atrophy of frontal and temporal lobes — can be severe; "knife-edge" atrophy of gyri in severe cases

**Key proteins in FTLD:**

| Protein | Diseases | Inclusions |
|---|---|---|
| **Tau** | Pick disease, CBD, PSP, some FTD | Pick bodies (round, tau-positive cytoplasmic inclusions in Pick disease) |
| **TDP-43** | Most common FTLD protein; also ALS | Cytoplasmic TDP-43 inclusions; nuclear clearing |
| **FUS** | Less common FTLD | FUS-positive inclusions |

- **Pick disease** (a specific FTLD-tau): Classic histology shows **Pick bodies** — round, intraneuronal tau-positive inclusions. Also shows "ballooned neurons" (achromatic neurons). Silver stain positive.
- **FTLD-TDP-43:** TDP-43 is a nuclear RNA-binding protein. In disease, it mislocalizes to cytoplasm and forms inclusions. Seen in most FTD-TDP and ALS cases.
- Immunohistochemistry for tau, TDP-43, or FUS is used to classify FTLD subtype.

#### Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE)
- Caused by repetitive head trauma (seen in athletes, military personnel)
- Pathology: **tau-positive NFTs** distributed around blood vessels, at the depths of sulci — distinct pattern from AD
- Progressive cognitive and behavioral symptoms; only diagnosed at autopsy

### 3c. Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB)

#### Clinical Features
Four **core clinical features** (need 2 probable, 1 possible DLB):
1. **Fluctuating cognition** — day-to-day or hour-to-hour changes in alertness and attention
2. **Recurrent visual hallucinations** — typically well-formed, detailed (e.g., people, animals)
3. **REM Sleep Behavioral Disorder (RBD)** — acting out dreams during REM sleep; can precede dementia by years
4. **Parkinsonism** — bradykinesia, rigidity, rest tremor (develops after cognitive symptoms in DLB)

**Additional supportive features:** Severe neuroleptic sensitivity (antipsychotics can be dangerous), reduced dopamine transporter (DAT) uptake on SPECT/PET, abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy

**Key cognitive domains affected:** Visuospatial (prominent), executive, attention; **memory less impaired early** than in AD

#### DLB vs Parkinson's Disease Dementia (PDD)
- **1-year rule:** If cognitive symptoms precede or occur within 1 year of parkinsonism → **DLB**
- If parkinsonism precedes cognitive symptoms by more than 1 year → **PDD**
- Same underlying pathology (alpha-synuclein Lewy bodies); distinction is primarily clinical and temporal

#### Epidemiology
- Up to 30% of all dementia cases; mean age of onset ~75 years
- Mean survival ~6.1 years after diagnosis

#### Treatments
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (rivastigmine preferred): help with cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms
- **Dopaminergic medications** (levodopa): use cautiously — can worsen hallucinations; motor benefit is often less than in PD
- **Melatonin:** for REM sleep behavioral disorder
- **SSRIs/SNRIs:** for depression/anxiety
- **AVOID typical antipsychotics** (e.g., haloperidol): risk of severe neuroleptic sensitivity reaction — can be life-threatening

#### Pathology (Neuropathology — Dr. Chambers)

**Gross findings:**
- **Pallor of the substantia nigra** in the midbrain (loss of dopaminergic neurons) — visible to the naked eye at autopsy; a classic high-yield finding

**Microscopic findings:**
- **Lewy body:** Round, eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusion with a pale halo — the hallmark of alpha-synucleinopathy
- In PD: Lewy bodies concentrated in **substantia nigra**
- In DLB: Lewy bodies concentrated in the **neocortex and limbic system** (cortical Lewy bodies)
- At advanced stages, Lewy bodies are found widely in both diseases

**Staining:**
- H&E: Lewy bodies appear as round, pink cytoplasmic structures with a faint halo
- **Alpha-synuclein immunohistochemistry (IHC):** Best method to identify Lewy bodies, especially cortical Lewy bodies that are less visible on H&E

## 4. List the histopathological and genetic tools used to assist in diagnosis of neurodegenerative disorders

### Histopathological Tools

| Tool | What it detects | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **H&E stain** | General morphology; plaques, tangles, Lewy bodies (with halo), spongiform change | All neurodegenerative diseases |
| **Congo red stain** | Amyloid (pink/red); apple-green birefringence under polarized light | AD amyloid plaques |
| **Silver stains** (Bielschowsky, Bodian) | NFTs, neuritic plaques, Pick bodies | AD, FTD |
| **Tau IHC** | Hyperphosphorylated tau (NFTs, Pick bodies, CTE tangles) | AD, Pick disease, CTE, PSP, CBD |
| **Alpha-synuclein IHC** | Lewy bodies (substantia nigra and cortex) | PD, DLB |
| **TDP-43 IHC** | Cytoplasmic TDP-43 inclusions | FTLD-TDP, ALS |
| **Amyloid IHC (A-beta)** | Amyloid plaques | AD |
| **PrP IHC (3F4)** | Prion protein deposits | CJD / prion diseases |
| **Western blot (PrP 3F4)** | PrP-Sc (proteinase K-resistant prion protein) | Prion disease subclassification |

### Genetic Tools

| Gene / Marker | Disease | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **APOE e4** | AD | Most common genetic risk factor |
| **APP, PSEN1, PSEN2** | Early-onset familial AD | Autosomal dominant mutations |
| **MAPT (tau gene)** | FTD-tau, some PSP/CBD | Mutations cause inherited FTD |
| **GRN (progranulin)** | FTD-TDP | Autosomal dominant FTD |
| **C9orf72** | FTD + ALS | Hexanucleotide repeat expansion; most common genetic FTD/ALS |
| **SNCA (alpha-synuclein gene)** | PD, DLB | Mutations/duplications |
| **PrP gene (PRNP)** | Familial CJD, FFI, GSS | Inherited prion diseases |

### In Vivo Biomarkers (Clinical Use)
- **CSF tau, phospho-tau, A-beta 1-42:** Support AD diagnosis
- **Amyloid PET / Tau PET:** Visualize pathology in living patients; required for anti-amyloid therapy eligibility
- **DAT-SPECT:** Reduced dopamine transporter signal supports DLB/PD
- **RT-QuIC (CSF):** Highly sensitive (85%) and specific (99%) for prion disease; detects PrP-Sc seeding activity

## 5. Common particulars of neurodegenerative diseases with regard to protein aggregation

All major neurodegenerative diseases involve **misfolded protein aggregation** leading to selective neuronal loss.

| Disease | Misfolded Protein | Affected Region | Clinical Presentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alzheimer's disease** | Tau + Amyloid-beta | Hippocampus → neocortex | Short-term memory loss first |
| **Frontotemporal dementia** | Tau or TDP-43 or FUS | Frontal and temporal lobes | Executive dysfunction / behavior changes first |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | Alpha-synuclein | Cortex, various areas | Fluctuating cognition, hallucinations, parkinsonism |
| **Parkinson's disease** | Alpha-synuclein | Substantia nigra | Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor; cognitive changes later |
| **CJD (Prion disease)** | PrP-Sc (misfolded PrP) | Various cortical areas | Rapidly progressive dementia + myoclonus |
| **ALS** | TDP-43, FUS, SOD1 | Motor neurons | Progressive weakness/atrophy |
| **Huntington's disease** | Huntingtin | Striatal neurons (caudate) | Chorea, psychiatric features, dementia |

**Key concept:** Prion protein (PrP-Sc) is unique — it is **transmissible/infectious**, unlike all other misfolded proteins in this group. It acts as a template, causing normal PrP-C to misfold on contact, creating a self-propagating cascade.

**Selective vulnerability:** Each disease affects specific neuron populations because of differences in protein expression, connectivity, and neuronal vulnerability. This explains why each disease has a characteristic pattern of symptoms.

## 6. Describe presentation and workup for rapidly progressive dementia, and specifically, common features of prion disease

### Rapidly Progressive Dementia (RPD)

- Defined as dementia developing over **weeks to months** (not years)
- Less likely to be a typical neurodegenerative cause (though AD, LBD, FTD can occasionally present rapidly)
- Requires **broad workup** — the differential is wide and includes treatable causes

**Workup categories (RT-QuiC mnemonic):**
- Infectious (Lyme, HIV, syphilis, fungal, viral encephalitis)
- Autoimmune/paraneoplastic (anti-NMDAR, LGI1, CASPR2, others)
- Metabolic (thyroid, liver, electrolytes, B12)
- Malignant (CNS lymphoma, carcinomatous meningitis)
- Prion disease

**Workup includes:** Blood count, metabolic panel, liver function, thyroid, B12, rheumatologic and autoimmune antibodies, paraneoplastic antibodies, RPR (syphilis), HIV; CSF (cell count, protein, glucose, oligoclonal bands, tau, 14-3-3, RT-QuIC); MRI brain with and without contrast (DWI/ADC, FLAIR); EEG; urine analysis; consider PET scan, body imaging for malignancy

### Prion Diseases

**Mechanism:**
- Prions are infectious proteins — no DNA or RNA
- **PrP-C:** Normal cellular prion protein
- **PrP-Sc:** Misfolded prion protein with excess beta-sheet structure
- PrP-Sc causes normal PrP-C to misfold on contact → chain reaction → aggregation → neuronal death

**Forms of prion disease:**

| Disease | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Sporadic CJD** | Spontaneous PrP-C to PrP-Sc conversion; most common (~85%) |
| **Familial CJD** | Mutations in the PrP (PRNP) gene |
| **Variant CJD** | Dietary ingestion; linked to bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") |
| **Gerstmann-Straussler-Scheinker (GSS)** | Genetic; PRNP mutation |
| **Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI)** | Genetic; PRNP mutation |
| **Kuru** | Ingestion of human brain tissue (Papua New Guinea funeral practice; now rare) |

### Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD) — Clinical Presentation

**Clinical vignette (Case 5):** 72-year-old man with 7 months of progressive cognitive decline including apraxia, executive dysfunction, short-term memory loss; exam showed simultaneagnosia and agraphesthesia; MOCA 12/30. MRI showed cortical ribboning — **sporadic CJD**

**Hallmark signs:**
- **Rapidly progressive mental deterioration** (weeks to months)
- **Myoclonus** (sudden muscle jerks)

**Other features:**
- Spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign (40-80%)
- Akinesia, ataxia, nystagmus (~67%)
- Visual symptoms

**Workup findings:**
- **MRI:** DWI/ADC signal changes in cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum; **"cortical ribboning"** — bright DWI signal along cortical grey matter
- **EEG:** Characteristic slow spike-and-wave or triphasic sharp wave complexes (1-2/sec)
- **CSF 14-3-3 protein:** General marker of neuronal injury; sensitivity 43-100%, specificity 67-100%; can be falsely positive in stroke/encephalitis
- **CSF RT-QuIC:** Real-time quaking-induced conversion assay; uses CSF prions to seed misfolding of normal PrP-C in vitro; sensitivity ~85%, specificity ~99% — most specific test
- Elevated CSF tau (higher = more probable CJD)

**Prognosis:** Fatal; treatment is palliative. Death typically within 6 months (median); usually within 1 year of diagnosis.

### Neuropathology of Prion Disease

**Gross:** Brain atrophy may be severe or very subtle

**Microscopic hallmark — Spongiform encephalopathy:**
- Numerous small **vacuoles** (holes) in gray matter neuropil, involving neuronal membranes
- Gives a sponge-like appearance on H&E
- Thalamus is high-yield area for sampling

**Confirmatory tests:**
- **PrP IHC (3F4):** Identifies abnormal prion protein deposits
- **Western blot (PrP 3F4):** Identifies proteinase K-resistant PrP-Sc bands — definitive diagnosis
- Tissue sent to **National Prion Disease Pathology Surveillance Center** (CDC-supported)

**CJD autopsy precautions:**
- Triggered by: rapidly progressive dementia (<2 years) + myoclonus, visual/cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal/extrapyramidal dysfunction, akinetic mutism, or supporting lab/imaging findings
- Protocol: formic acid treatment (inactivates prions), scout sections from frontal lobe/basal ganglia/thalamus/cerebellum; disinfect and discard instruments
- Formic acid inactivates prion proteins

## Quick Reference: Neurodegenerative Disorder Comparison

| Disease | First Symptoms | Key Pathology | Protein | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer's disease | Short-term memory loss | NFTs + amyloid plaques; hippocampal atrophy | Tau + A-beta | ChEIs, memantine, anti-amyloid Abs |
| bvFTD | Behavior/personality changes | Frontal/temporal atrophy; tau or TDP-43 inclusions | Tau or TDP-43 | Symptomatic only |
| PPA | Language | Focal atrophy; depends on variant | Tau, TDP-43, or A-beta | Symptomatic only |
| DLB | Visuospatial + fluctuation + hallucinations | Cortical Lewy bodies; substantia nigra pallor | Alpha-synuclein | ChEIs, cautious dopaminergics |
| CJD | Rapid cognitive decline + myoclonus | Spongiform change; PrP-Sc deposits | PrP-Sc | Palliative only |
