## PART 1: INTRO TO CNS TUMORS

## 1. Approach to a CNS Mass Lesion

Apply the same **3-step neurologic approach** to any CNS mass:

1. **Formulate key symptoms + signs** and establish a time course
2. **Localize the lesion** (y-axis: CNS vs. PNS; x-axis: specific region)
3. **Etiological Dx**: What is it? Use the mnemonic **I MIND THE VIP** (N = neoplastic)

### Time Course and Typical Etiologies

| Time Course | Decline Pattern | Typical Etiology |
|---|---|---|
| Acute (seconds–minutes) | Sudden | Stroke, hemorrhage |
| Subacute (days–weeks) | Stepwise decline | Infection, subdural hematoma, fast tumor |
| Chronic/progressive (months–years) | Gradual | Degenerative, slow tumor |
| Waxing and waning (days–weeks) | Fluctuating | Demyelinating |
| Episodic (minutes–hours) | Comes and goes | TIA, seizure, migraine |

Most CNS tumors present **subacutely** (high-grade) or **chronically/progressively** (low-grade), but can present **acutely** with hemorrhage, increased ICP, seizure, or hydrocephalus.

### Workup Algorithm

1. **Neuroimaging first**
   - CT head for acute headache (rule out bleed, herniation)
   - MRI brain with contrast preferred for most other cases
   - Contrast (gadolinium for MRI; dye for CT) highlights BBB breakdown, mass lesions, vascular structures
   - **Must image first** if patient has: AMS/coma, recent seizure, focal neurologic deficits including papilledema, or is immunocompromised
2. **Consider LP after imaging** — only if patient is not at risk for herniation (no mass effect, no posterior fossa crowding, no elevated ICP). LP contraindications include: signs of raised ICP, spinal epidural abscess, coagulation defects (INR > 1.4, platelets < 50K)
3. **Consider neurosurgery** — for biopsy (WHO grading, molecular characterization), ICP relief (drain, decompression), or resection
4. **Consider other treatments** — radiation, chemotherapy, medical Rx

## 2. Neuro-Anatomical Compartments

The CNS is protected by layers: **skin > subcutaneous tissue > skull > dura mater > leptomeninges (arachnoid, pia)**. Tumors, infections, and inflammation tend to be contained within specific spaces between these layers.

| Compartment | Location | Typical Pathology |
|---|---|---|
| **Epidural** | Between skull and dura | Hematoma, abscess (lens-shaped) |
| **Subdural** | Between dura and arachnoid | Hematoma, abscess (empyema — rare), meningioma (common) |
| **Subarachnoid** | Between arachnoid and pia; contains CSF and Circle of Willis | SAH, (lepto)meningitis, carcinomatous meningitis |
| **Intraparenchymal** | Within brain tissue | ICH, primary tumors, abscess (with capsule), metastases |

**Key points:**
- **Spinal epidural metastases** are the most common neurological complication of cancer after brain metastases (common primaries: lung, breast, lymphoma, myeloma, prostate, sarcoma); can cause cord compression requiring emergent surgical decompression
- **Carcinomatous meningitis** = cancer cells infiltrating the subarachnoid space

## 3. Signs, Causes, and Consequences of Increased ICP / Herniation

### Signs of Increased ICP
Symptoms arise from **brain invasion**, **compression of structures**, or **increased ICP**. Headache is the most common symptom (up to 50% of patients with a CNS mass).

**Signs of raised ICP:**
- Headache worse with position change or Valsalva maneuver
- Nocturnal headache that awakens the patient
- Nausea and vomiting
- **Bilateral CN VI palsies** (or unilateral) — CN VI is susceptible to downward traction from elevated ICP due to its long course along the clivus; produces **horizontal diplopia** with inability to abduct the affected eye

Use the **SNOOP** mnemonic to screen for secondary headache red flags requiring neuroimaging:
- **S**ystemic symptoms/illness (fever, cancer, immunocompromised, anticoagulated)
- **N**euro symptoms/signs (focal deficits, AMS, seizure, papilledema, stiff neck)
- **O**nset: first or worst, sudden (especially if < 5 yo or > 40 yo)
- **O**ther associated conditions (head trauma, awakens from sleep, worse with Valsalva)
- **P**rior headache history is different (worsening pattern, rapidly progressive)

### Brain Herniation Syndromes

The skull is a **fixed vault** — if any component increases in volume, ICP rises.

| Herniation Type | Mechanism | Key Signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Subfalcine** | Cingulate gyrus pushes under falx cerebri | Midline shift on imaging; may compromise ACA |
| **Central** | Diencephalon/midbrain descends | Impaired consciousness, respiratory compromise |
| **Uncal/transtentorial** | Uncus (medial temporal lobe) herniates over tentorium | Ipsilateral CN III palsy: **"blown pupil"** (fixed, dilated) + eye resting **down and out**; contralateral hemiparesis; ultimately coma |
| **Tonsillar** | Cerebellar tonsils herniate through foramen magnum | Compression of medulla; lethal effects on respiration and blood pressure — **often fatal**; can be triggered by LP in posterior fossa crowding |

## 4. Communicating vs. Non-Communicating Hydrocephalus

### Normal CSF Flow
CSF is produced by the **choroid plexus** in the ventricles and flows:

Lateral ventricles → Foramen of Monro → 3rd ventricle → Cerebral (Sylvian) aqueduct → 4th ventricle → Foramina of Luschka (lateral) and Magendie (midline) → Subarachnoid space → Arachnoid granulations → Reabsorbed into bloodstream

### Types of Hydrocephalus

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-communicating (obstructive)** | CSF flow is physically blocked within the ventricular system | Tumor obstructing 4th ventricle (e.g., medulloblastoma), aqueductal stenosis, cyst |
| **Communicating (non-obstructive)** | CSF still flows but resorption is impaired at arachnoid granulations | Infectious meningitis or blood products clogging granulations; less commonly, excess CSF production |

Both types enlarge the ventricles and raise ICP. On **MRI T1**: CSF is dark; on **MRI T2**: CSF is bright. Hydrocephalus can be congenital (seen in 90% of neural tube defects with myelomeningocele) or acquired.

## PART 2: ALTERED MENTAL STATUS AND COMA

## 5. Definition of Coma

**Consciousness** = arousability + responsiveness to stimuli from the environment.

**Coma** = "unarousable unresponsiveness" (Plum & Posner) — the absence of **BOTH**:
- Arousal (eyes closed, cannot be awakened)
- Awareness (no response to pain or light, no sleep-wake cycles, no voluntary actions)

Coma is a **medical emergency** representing advanced brain failure. Patients typically require intubation for airway protection.

**Spectrum of altered consciousness** (least to most severe): wide awake → drowsy → confused/altered → lethargic → obtunded → stuporous → comatose

**Delirium** = a specific confusional state with **waxing and waning** mental status and **impaired attention** (always present); may present as agitated (hyperactive) or hypoactive. Disorganized thinking, hallucinations, and delusions are common.

---

## 6. Coma Mimics

The following are **not** coma — care must be taken to distinguish them:

| State | Description |
|---|---|
| **Persistent vegetative state (PVS)** | Seemingly awake (opens eyes, sleep-wake cycles) but **unaware** of environment; no purposeful movements or meaningful communication. Poor prognosis if > 3 months (non-trauma) or > 12 months (trauma) |
| **Locked-in syndrome** | Paralyzed (bilateral ventral pons lesion affecting CBT + CST) but **conscious** and sensation intact; vertical eye movements often spared |
| **Complete peripheral nerve paralysis** | GBS/botulism: cognitively intact but fully paralyzed peripherally |
| **Post-ictal state** | Minimally responsive/difficult to arouse after a seizure |
| **Abulia/akinetic mutism** | Minimal responsiveness from large frontal lobe dysfunction |
| **Psychogenic unresponsiveness** | e.g., catatonia from psychiatric illness |
| **Minimally conscious state** | Some reproducible purposeful behavior (fixation, pursuit, simple commands) but no reliable communication |
| **Brain death** | Irreversible loss of all cortical, subcortical, and brainstem function = **death**; requires coma + absent brainstem reflexes + failed apnea test, confirmed by two independent evaluators |

## 7. Simon's Rule of 4 for Coma Evaluation

A rapid framework to identify the lesion and initiate management.

### 1. First Things to Do
- **ABCs** + vitals (watch for Cushing's reflex: HTN + bradycardia = impending herniation)
- Give **thiamine (100 mg IV) first**, then **D50** (dextrose) — glucose before thiamine can precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy
- Consider **naloxone** (opioid intoxication) and **flumazenil** (benzodiazepine overdose)
- Blood and urine tests

### 2. Two Localizations of Coma
**(i) Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS)** = reticular formation of the upper pons and midbrain; integral to inducing and maintaining alertness; lesions here disrupt arousal

**(ii) Bilateral diffuse cerebral hemispheric dysfunction** (or bilateral thalami, which relay to cortex); a unilateral lesion can also cause coma if large enough to exert mass effect on the contralateral hemisphere or brainstem

### 3. Three Etiologies of Coma
1. **Non-structural (toxic-metabolic encephalopathy):** no visible lesion; diffuse neuronal dysfunction; *hallmark: spared pupils, intact CNs, symmetric motor exam* (many exceptions exist)
2. **Structural (lesional):** symmetric or asymmetric; focal neurologic deficits are common
3. **Electrical (seizures/status epilepticus):** diagnosed by history and EEG

### 4. Four Things to Examine

**(i) Pupils**
- Tests CN II (afferent) → midbrain Edinger-Westphal nucleus → CN III (efferent, parasympathetic fibers run superficially)
- **Blown pupil** (fixed, dilated) = PANS fibers on CN III compressed → pupil constrictor dysfunction → ipsilateral pupil dilation
- Spared pupils in metabolic coma; asymmetric/fixed pupils suggest structural cause

**(ii) Doll's Eyes / Oculocephalic Reflex (OCR) or Cold Calorics**
- OCR: turn head → eyes move opposite direction (normal). **Intact in metabolic coma; absent or dysconjugate in brainstem lesions**
- Cold calorics (CN VIII afferent, CN III/VI efferent): infuse 50 cc ice water into ear canal; normal = slow conjugate eye deviation toward the ice. Abnormal = brainstem pathology
- Only perform OCR if cervical spine is stable

**(iii) Response to Pain**
- Apply supraorbital pressure, trapezius pinch, sternal rub, or nailbed pressure
- Responses (best to worst): **localizes > withdraws > grimaces > posturing > no response**
- **Decorticate posturing** (arm flexion, leg extension) = lesion **in or above the midbrain/red nucleus** — bad
- **Decerebrate posturing** (extension of all limbs) = lesion **below the red nucleus (pons/medulla)** — really bad

**(iv) Meningeal Signs**
- **Kernig's sign**: pain on knee extension with hip flexed 90°
- **Brudzinski's sign**: hips and knees flex when neck is flexed
- Positive signs suggest meningeal irritation (infection, SAH, carcinomatous meningitis)

## 8. Localization of Coma

Coma requires injury to either **(i) ARAS** or **(ii) bilateral diffuse cerebral hemispheres** (including bilateral thalami). Isolated basal ganglia, cerebellar, medullary, or spinal cord lesions do not produce coma.

### Localizing Within the Brainstem Using CN Deficits

| CN Involved | Level |
|---|---|
| CN II, III, IV | Midbrain |
| CN V | Pons |
| CN VI, VII, VIII, IX | Pons to medulla |
| CN X, XI, XII | Medulla / lower brainstem |

**Summary for Simon's Coma Exam:**
- Pupils (CN II, III) → midbrain level
- Doll's eyes/cold calorics (CN VIII, III, VI) → midbrain to pons level
- Response to pain (CN V, VII) → pons level
- Decorticate posturing → at/above midbrain; Decerebrate posturing → below midbrain

### Case 1 Example: Unresponsive in the Street
Middle-aged man, unresponsive, no response to thiamine/dextrose/naloxone.
- Exam: Left blown pupil (CN III palsy = left midbrain), left eye laterally deviated, right arm flexion + right leg extension (decorticate posturing = left CST involvement), right Babinski sign
- **"Crossed signs"** (left CN deficits + right hemibody UMN signs) → brainstem localization = **left midbrain**
- Etiology: structural, asymmetric; most likely **uncal/transtentorial herniation** compressing the left midbrain CN III and ARAS
- GCS: E1V1M3

## 9. Etiologies of AMS/Coma

### Structural — Symmetric
Trauma with DAI, SAH, hydrocephalus, venous sinus thrombosis, bilateral strokes (thalamus, large vascular territories), basilar artery occlusion, midline brainstem lesion

### Structural — Asymmetric
Mass lesion (tumor, stroke, ICH, EDH, SDH, abscess) with herniation; brainstem infarct/hemorrhage

### Non-Structural (Metabolic/Toxic)
Hallmark: **spared pupils, intact CNs, symmetric motor exam** (exceptions exist)

Common causes (use mnemonic **MOVE STUPID** or **I MIND THE VIP**):
- Drugs/anesthesia (barbiturates, benzodiazepines, opiates)
- Toxins (alcohol, CO, cyanide, heavy metals)
- Metabolic: hypoglycemia, DKA, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state, Na/Ca/Mg/pH disturbances, uremia, liver failure
- Vitamin deficiencies (B1/thiamine, B12/cobalamin)
- Endocrine (thyroid), hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoperfusion/shock, hypo/hyperthermia, cardiopulmonary arrest
- Meningitis (can also be structural if abscess)

### Electrical
- Seizures, status epilepticus, or prolonged post-ictal state
- Diagnosed by EEG; treated with anti-seizure medications

## 11. Neuropathology Correlates of AMS/Coma

### Wernicke Encephalopathy (B1/Thiamine Deficiency)
**Classic triad:** ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + confusion/coma

- Thiamine is a cofactor for energy metabolism enzymes (transketolase, pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase)
- **Do not give dextrose before thiamine** — glucose can precipitate Wernicke's in a thiamine-deficient state
- Pathology: hemorrhagic lesions of **mammillary bodies**, hypothalamus, periventricular thalamus, periaqueductal gray (seen as bright signal on FLAIR MRI)
- If untreated → irreversible **Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome**: severe anterograde and retrograde amnesia, confabulation
- Rx: thiamine 100–300 mg/day

### Alcohol (EtOH) Toxicity
- Cerebellar degeneration (1% of chronic users): atrophy of **superior cerebellar vermis**; truncal instability, ataxia, wide-based gait, slurred speech, disinhibition
- Severe intoxication → coma and potentially fatal respiratory depression
- **Delirium tremens** (withdrawal, 12–72 hours after last drink): agitated delirium, tremors, tachycardia, hyperthermia, hallucinations, provoked seizures

### B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency
- Causes **subacute combined degeneration**: myelopathy (dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal tracts) + peripheral neuropathy
- Pathology: vacuolated myelin in dorsal columns (sensory) and lateral CSTs (motor)
- MRI T2: bright signal in dorsal spinal cord
- Dx: serum B12 low; homocysteine elevated; methylmalonic acid (MMA) elevated (MMA is more specific for B12 deficiency than folate deficiency)
- Rx: IM B12 1000 mcg daily x 1 week, then weekly x 1 month, then monthly

### Selected Neuropathology Patterns
| Cause | Predilection Site |
|---|---|
| B1 (thiamine) | Mammillary bodies, periaqueductal gray |
| B12 (cobalamin) | Dorsal columns + lateral CSTs of spinal cord |
| Hypoglycemia | Pyramidal cortical neurons, hippocampus CA1, Purkinje cells |
| CO poisoning | Globus pallidus necrosis; also CA1, Purkinje cells |
| Ethanol (chronic) | Superior cerebellar vermis |
| Methanol | Retinal ganglion cells, optic nerves, bilateral putamen |

## 12. Early Management of AMS/Coma

### Evaluation
1. **ABCs** with intubation if needed; assess vitals
2. **Thiamine 100 mg IV** before dextrose; then D50 (50 mL); consider naloxone (opioid OD) and flumazenil (BZD OD)
3. Protect cervical spine; look for body trauma; quick neuro exam to categorize as structural / non-structural / electrical
4. **Labs:** CBC, electrolytes, liver function, ABG, toxicology screen, EtOH level
5. Investigate suicide attempts and toxins; consider gastric lavage
6. **Urgent head CT** — always image before LP to rule out mass / posterior fossa crowding (LP in this context can precipitate fatal tonsillar herniation)
7. If no obvious cause and no recovery from apparent post-ictal state → **EEG** for status epilepticus

### Treatment

| Type | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Non-structural (metabolic)** | Treat underlying cause; if meningeal signs + fever, start empiric antibiotics/antivirals; DVT/PE prophylaxis; nursing care |
| **Structural** | Neurosurgical options (drain, shunt, biopsy, resection, evacuation); for ischemic stroke: consider IV tPA/TNK or endovascular recanalization; if raised ICP: elevate HOB 30°, mannitol 1 g/kg or hypertonic (3%) saline, consider dexamethasone (note: not for ischemic stroke), consider ICP monitoring |
| **Electrical** | Anti-seizure medications; may require pharmacologic coma induction for refractory status epilepticus |
