## 1. PNS Patterns of Lesions and Localization

### General PNS (Lower Motor Neuron) Pattern
- No cortical signs or cognitive problems
- Reflexes: low, absent, or normal
- Tone: flaccid (decreased)
- Bulk: atrophy, fasciculations
- Sensory +/- motor deficits may follow a specific nerve distribution

### Key Patterns for Localization
- **Symmetric distal stocking-glove** sensorimotor deficits with decreased/absent reflexes → **polyneuropathy**
- **Sensory and/or motor deficits following a specific distribution** → root/plexus/nerve lesion
- **Pure motor, EOM involved, preserved reflexes, fatiguable** → **NMJ disease**
- **Pure motor, symmetric, proximal > distal** → **myopathy**
- **UMN + LMN signs combined** → **motor neuron disease** (anterior horn cell)

---

## 2. The 3-Step Approach to a Neurologic Diagnosis

1. **Formulate key symptoms + signs** and establish a time course
2. **Anatomical localization** — where is the lesion?
3. **Etiological diagnosis** — what is it?

PNS diseases usually present with a **subacute to chronic** time course (exceptions: GBS is subacute over days; traumatic nerve transection is acute).

## 3. Neuroanatomy Review

### Motor Exam
Three components: **bulk**, **tone**, and **strength**.

- **Bulk:** Look for atrophy (focal vs. diffuse) and fasciculations (spontaneous abnormal muscle twitches — suggest denervation)
- **Tone:** Resistance to passive range of motion. In PNS disease → hypotonia (floppy/flaccid). Infants show frog-leg posture and head lag.
- **Strength:** Graded on the MRC scale (0–5/5):
  - 5/5 = normal
  - 4/5 = movement against gravity + resistance
  - 3/5 = movement against gravity only (anti-gravity)
  - 2/5 = movement only side-to-side (not against gravity)
  - 1/5 = flicker/trace contraction
  - 0/5 = no contraction

**Functional assessment:** Proximal weakness → difficulty combing hair, climbing stairs, rising from a chair. Distal weakness → difficulty with grip, fine motor, foot drop/tripping.

### Sensory Exam
- **Most reliable tests:** Light touch, vibration, proprioception (dorsal columns), Romberg test, pinprick/temperature (spinothalamic tract)
- **Romberg test:** Patient stands with narrow base, eyes closed. Positive if sways/loses balance → proprioceptive deficit (peripheral sensory nerves or posterior dorsal columns)
- Key terms: anesthesia (numbness), hypoesthesia (decreased), hyperesthesia (increased), paresthesias (tingling), allodynia (pain from non-noxious stimuli)

### UMN vs. LMN Facial Weakness
- **LMN (Bell's palsy):** Entire ipsilateral hemiface is weak (forehead, eye closure, mouth)
- **UMN (stroke):** Only lower 2/3 of face is weak (forehead spared due to bilateral innervation)

### Extraocular Muscles

| CN | Function | Dysfunction |
|---|---|---|
| **III** | Levator palpebrae + all EOM except SO and LR; parasympathetics to pupil constrictor | Ptosis, eye down-and-out, dilated pupil |
| **IV** | Superior oblique (depression + intorsion) | Hypertropic eye, vertical diplopia worse looking toward nose + down |
| **VI** | Lateral rectus (abduction) | Horizontal diplopia, esotropic eye |

## 4. Lesions at Each PNS Site

### Radiculopathy (e.g., Herniated Disc)
- Compression of nerve root (commonly L5/S1) from degenerative spine disease
- Dermatomal/myotomal distribution, often painful, usually unilateral
- Positive straight leg raise, sensory > sensorimotor
- DDx: trauma, tumors, metastasis, infection, DM

### Sensory Ganglionopathy (e.g., Shingles)
- Reactivation of latent VZV in sensory dorsal root ganglia
- Painful vesicular rash in a **single dermatomal** distribution
- ~20% develop postherpetic neuralgia (chronic neuropathic pain)

### Brachial Plexopathy
- **Erb-Duchenne palsy** (C5-C6, upper trunk): birth trauma → "waiter's tip posture" (weak deltoid, tricep, wrist extension)
- **Klumpke's palsy** (C8-T1, lower trunk): "claw/benediction hand" (ulnar atrophy/weakness). May have Horner syndrome if T1 involved
- Scapular winging → injury to long thoracic nerve → anterior serratus muscle

### Peripheral Neuropathy

**Mononeuropathy** — single nerve, focal, asymmetric
- **Bell's palsy:** Entire ipsilateral hemiface weak (LMN CN VII). Thought to be viral (HSV) or idiopathic. Treat with steroids within 72 hours +/- valacyclovir. Eye care for corneal protection.
- **Carpal tunnel:** Median nerve entrapment

**Mononeuropathy multiplex** — multiple focal distal nerves affected stepwise
- Subacute onset (faster than chronic neuropathies)
- Must rule out **vasculitis** (ischemic infarction of individual nerves) with nerve/muscle biopsy
- Can mimic polyneuropathy over time as pattern becomes confluent

**Polyneuropathy** — diffuse, length-dependent, symmetric, distal > proximal ("stocking-glove")
- Most common cause: **diabetes mellitus** (~50% of all neuropathies); 50% idiopathic
- Sensory numbness/pain often the first symptom, progresses over months–years
- Hypo/areflexia; affects >20 million people in the US
- **Diagnosis:** Look for reversible causes (HbA1c, vitamin deficiencies, thyroid, HIV/RPR if risk factors). EMG/NCS helps localize and characterize (axonal vs. demyelinating)
- **Treatment:** Treat underlying cause; neuropathic pain medications (gabapentin, pregabalin, TCAs, SSRIs); PT/OT; assistive devices

**Hereditary neuropathies — Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT)**
- Most common inherited peripheral neuropathy (~125,000 in US)
- Most common genetic defect: **PMP-22 gene duplication** → demyelinating → slowed nerve conduction
- Characteristic findings: high arches (pes cavus), hammer toes, stork leg/inverted champagne bottle appearance, intrinsic hand wasting
- Pathology: "onion bulbs" — concentric Schwann cell layers from repeated demyelination/remyelination

### Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) = Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (AIDP)
- **Subacute** symmetric **ascending flaccid paralysis**, weakness > sensory symptoms over days
- Can ascend to respiratory muscles and cranial nerves; prominent autonomic dysfunction (BP/HR lability)
- Pain can be prominent (inflammation of proximal sensory nerve roots)
- Exam: symmetric weakness > sensory, areflexia (ascending loss)
- **Diagnosis:** Clinical! LP shows **albuminocytologic dissociation** (high protein 50–100, normal WBC). EMG/NCS: decreased nerve conduction velocity (demyelinating)
- **Pathogenesis:** Molecular mimicry — immune response to prior infection targets peripheral nerve myelin. Common triggers: *Campylobacter jejuni* > CMV, EBV, HIV, Zika
- **Treatment:** Supportive care + **IVIg** or **PLEX** (plasma exchange). This is a treatable polyneuropathy!
- **Prognosis:** Excellent, usually monophasic, nadir < 4 weeks

### Neuromuscular Junction — Myasthenia Gravis (MG)
- Autoimmune: antibodies against acetylcholine receptor (AChR Ab+ in ~85%)
- **Hallmark: pure motor fatiguable weakness** — provoked by exercise, improves with rest
- EOM involved in ~80–90%: ptosis, non-anatomic diplopia
- Bulbar weakness (15–20%): nasal speech, dysphagia
- Respiratory involvement (especially orthopnea); limb weakness proximal > distal
- Sensation and reflexes: **normal**
- Bimodal: younger women (20–30s) and older men (60–80s)
- **Treatment:**
  - Symptomatic: cholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) — partially effective for mild disease
  - Immunologic (for serious disease): PLEX, IVIg, steroids, steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab)
  - New FDA-approved biologics (2021–2023): eculizumab, efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan
  - Thymectomy (if thymoma, or if <60 years with generalized MG)

### Myopathy (Muscle Disease)
- Progressive weakness, **proximal > distal**, sensory spared, reflexes normal/low
- Serum CK elevated
- Confirm with EMG/NCS (myopathic pattern) and/or muscle biopsy

**Acquired myopathies:**
- **Inflammatory:** dermatomyositis, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis (IBM), autoimmune necrotizing myopathy
- **Drug/toxic:** steroid, statin, alcohol
- **Metabolic/endocrine:** hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency

**Dermatomyositis vs. polymyositis:**
- Both: proximal weakness, elevated CK, responsive to immunotherapy
- **Dermatomyositis** has skin findings: heliotrope rash (eyelids), Gottron's papules (knuckles), dilated nailbed capillaries. Increased cancer risk — screen!
- **IBM:** Most common sporadic myopathy >65 years; slowly progressive quadriceps and distal upper extremity weakness; does NOT respond to immunosuppression

**Hereditary myopathies (muscular dystrophies):**

- **Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD):** X-linked recessive, absence of dystrophin; onset age 2–6, non-ambulatory by 10–12, death ~18–20 years. Gower's sign, calf pseudohypertrophy. Cardiac and cognitive involvement. Treatment: glucocorticoids + emerging gene therapies
- **Becker Muscular Dystrophy (BMD):** Milder, variable onset/severity, truncated but partially functional dystrophin
- **Myotonic Dystrophy:** 2nd most common; most common adult-onset dystrophy. Autosomal dominant, CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion (DMPK gene). Multi-system: cataracts, cardiomyopathy, cognitive impairment. Characteristic **myotonia** (delayed muscle relaxation). Anticipation: earlier onset in successive generations

**Congenital myopathies:**
- **Nemaline myopathy:** Red rod-like inclusions on trichrome stain; weakness and hypotonia
- **Central core disease:** Central clearing in muscle fibers; RYR1 gene mutations; **risk of malignant hyperthermia** with inhalational anesthetics

### Motor Neuron Disease — ALS (Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis)
- Anterior horn cell degeneration → **combined UMN + LMN signs**, pure motor
- Insidious, progressive, often **asymmetric** weakness
- Affects limbs → bulbar muscles (dysarthria, dysphagia) → respiratory failure
- Fasciculations + atrophy (LMN) with hyperreflexia + Babinski (UMN)
- EMG/NCS: widespread motor denervation, sensory spared
- **Median survival ~4 years** from symptom onset
- Treatment: limited efficacy — riluzole, edaravone, sodium-phenylbutyrate-taurursodiol; tofersen for SOD1 variants; multidisciplinary supportive care

**Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA):**
- Autosomal recessive, SMN gene deletion (chromosome 5q)
- Loss of anterior horn cells → hypotonia, progressive weakness in infants
- Previously fatal by age 2; now treatable with gene therapies: nusinersen (antisense oligonucleotide), onasemnogene (gene replacement), risdiplam (RNA splicing modifier) — FDA approved 2016–2020

## 5. Neuropathology: Diagnostic Approach to Nerve Biopsy

- **Sural nerve** is the standard biopsy nerve (purely sensory → minimal postoperative deficit)
- 1/3 of peripheral neuropathies are idiopathic
- **Not useful in:** Most axonal neuropathies (nonspecific), GBS (sural nerve not primarily involved)
- **Most useful in conditions with distinctive pathology:** vasculitis, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, leprosy, pediatric genetic neuropathies

## 6. Axonal Neuropathies: Wallerian vs. Dying Back Degeneration

| Feature | Dying Back Axonopathy | Wallerian Degeneration |
|---|---|---|
| **Pattern** | Symmetric stocking-glove, length-dependent | Focal or multifocal |
| **Mechanism** | Failure of axonal transport; longest nerves affected first | Axonal degeneration distal to site of injury |
| **Classic cause** | DM neuropathy, toxic/metabolic | Vasculitis (nerve ischemia), trauma |
| **Analogy** | Dried lawn | Mowed lawn |
| **Recovery** | Slow, if any | Slow (axons grow 1–2 mm/day) but regeneration begins immediately; muscle can become reinnervated |
| **Cell body change** | — | Central chromatolysis (rounding, peripheral nucleus displacement, Nissl dispersion) |

Nerves with very long axons are most vulnerable to failure of anterograde/retrograde transport → length-dependent neuropathy.

## 7. Neuropathology: Key Examples by Cellular Component

### Demyelinating Neuropathies
- **GBS:** Immune-mediated segmental demyelination of peripheral nerves
- **Diphtheria:** Toxin-induced myelin injury (not direct infection)
- **Leprosy:** *M. leprae* invades Schwann cells. Lepromatous form: symmetric polyneuropathy in cool extremities. Tuberculoid form: granulomatous inflammation injuring cutaneous nerves
- **CMT:** Repeated demyelination/remyelination → onion bulbs → palpable nerve enlargement. PMP-22 gene dosage effect (3 copies → CMT1A; deletion → HNPP)

### Axonal Neuropathies
- **Vasculitis:** Necrotizing arteritis → ischemia → Wallerian degeneration (classic cause of mononeuritis multiplex). Often seen in polyarteritis nodosa
- **Diabetic neuropathy:** 4 patterns — distal symmetric polyneuropathy (most common), radiculoplexopathy, mononeuropathy/mononeuritis multiplex, autonomic neuropathy. Prevalence up to 80% after 15 years of disease
- **Metabolic/nutritional:** Uremia, alcohol, vitamin deficiencies (B1, B12, B6, E), toxic exposures

## 8. Muscle Pathology

### Normal Skeletal Muscle
- Long spindle-shaped cells bundled in fascicles, enveloped by fibrous connective tissue
- Two main fiber types in mosaic pattern: **Type 1** (fatigue-resistant, more mitochondria/myoglobin, weight-bearing) and **Type 2** (fast-twitch, fatigue-sensitive, more glycogen)

### Neurogenic Disease of Muscle
- Denervated fibers atrophy but do not die; can recover with reinnervation
- Patterns: single fiber atrophy → fiber type grouping (reinnervation) → group atrophy
- Serum CK is NOT elevated (no myofiber necrosis)

### Muscular Dystrophy Pathology (General)
- Fiber degeneration and regeneration, marked variation in fiber diameter
- Internal nuclei, endomysial fibrosis, fat replacement of muscle
- CK elevated due to muscle cell necrosis
- **Dystrophin** links contractile apparatus to cell membrane; loss → membrane instability under contractile stress → calcium influx → cell death
- DMD: absent dystrophin (immunohistochemistry diagnostic). BMD: truncated but partially functional dystrophin
- Myotonic dystrophy: many central nuclei, ring fibers, sarcoplasmic masses

### Inflammatory Myopathy Pathology
- **Dermatomyositis:** Perifascicular atrophy (outer edges of fascicles selectively affected); complement-mediated capillary disease
- **Polymyositis/immune-mediated:** Endomysial inflammation with CD8+ cytotoxic T cells attacking non-necrotic muscle fibers; MHC-1 upregulation
- **IBM:** Rimmed vacuoles; does not respond to immunosuppression
