# CNS Tumors Tutorial Summary

## Learning Objectives
1. Internalize an approach to a patient who presents with a CNS mass lesion
2. Know the signs of increased intracranial pressure (ICP)
3. Differentiate communicating vs. non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus
4. Familiarize yourself with common CNS tumors in peds vs. adults; anterior vs. posterior fossa
5. Recognize neuroimaging and pathologic features of CNS tumors
6. Recognize management and treatment options

---

## 1. Approach to a Patient with a CNS Mass Lesion

**3-step neuro diagnosis:**
1. **Formulate key symptoms + signs** and establish a time course
2. **Localize the lesion** (where is it?)
3. **Etiological diagnosis** (what is it?) -- use "I MIND THE VIP" mnemonic

**Time course matters:** Most CNS tumors present subacutely -- days-weeks for high grade, months-years for low grade. They can also present acutely with hemorrhage, increased ICP, mass effect, seizure, or hydrocephalus.

**Workup approach:**
- **Neuroimaging first:** CT head for acute HA (rule out bleed/herniation); MRI brain +/- contrast preferred for all others
- **Consider LP** if not at risk for downward herniation (no increased ICP signs, no mass effect on imaging, no CN palsies, no posterior fossa crowding)
  - LP contraindications: lesion on imaging or increased ICP, spinal epidural abscess, coagulation defects (INR > 1.4, platelets < 50K)
- **Consider neurosurgery** for biopsy/pathologic dx, WHO grading, molecular characterization, or ICP relief (drain, decompress, resect)
- **Consider other treatments:** radiation, chemotherapy, medical Rx

**Must image first if patient has:** altered mental status, focal neuro deficits (including papilledema), immunocompromised status, or recent seizure.

## 2. Signs of Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

The skull is a fixed vault, so symptoms occur from brain invasion, compression, or increased ICP.

**Headache** is the most common presenting symptom (~50%). Screen with **SNOOP** red flags:
- **S**ystemic sx (fever, rash, cancer, immunocompromised, anticoagulated)
- **N**euro sx (focal deficits, altered mental status, seizure, papilledema, stiff neck)
- **O**nset (first/worst, sudden -- especially if <5 or >40 yo)
- **O**ther conditions (head trauma, awakens from sleep, worse with Valsalva/exercise/postural)
- **P**rior HA history different (worsening pattern, progressive severity/frequency)

**Signs of increased ICP:**
- HA worse with position or Valsalva
- Nocturnal HA that awakens patient
- Nausea/vomiting
- **Bilateral CN VI palsies** (or unilateral) -- CN VI has a long course along the clivus, making it susceptible to downward traction from elevated ICP. Produces **horizontal diplopia**, worse viewing far objects. Exam: incomplete abduction ("incomplete burial of the sclera" on lateral gaze).

## 3. Communicating vs. Non-Communicating Hydrocephalus

**Normal CSF flow:** Choroid plexus (lateral ventricles) -> foramen of Monro -> 3rd ventricle -> cerebral (Sylvian) aqueduct -> 4th ventricle -> foramina of Luschka (lateral) + Magendie (midline) -> subarachnoid space -> arachnoid granulations -> reabsorbed into blood

**Non-communicating (obstructive):** CSF flow is physically blocked (by cyst, tumor, aqueductal stenosis). Example: tuberous sclerosis patient with a subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) obstructing CSF flow.

**Communicating (non-obstructive):** CSF still flows but resorption at the arachnoid granulations is impaired (e.g., from infectious meningitis or blood products clogging granulations). Less commonly from excessive CSF production.

Both types cause increased ICP and ventricular enlargement. Can be congenital (e.g., 90% of neural tube defects with myelomeningocele) or acquired.

## 4. Common CNS Tumors

Primary CNS tumors: ~30/100,000 in US. Meningioma and glial tumors (glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma) account for 2/3 of cases. Most are sporadic with no risk factors. **CNS metastases are the most common brain tumor overall** (from lung, breast, melanoma).

### Schwannomas
- **What:** Benign encapsulated tumors arising from Schwann cells on peripheral nerves (including cranial nerves)
- **Epidemiology:** Sporadic tumors affect all ages; predilection for CN VIII (vestibular schwannoma/acoustic neuroma, presents 5th-6th decade). Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = high suspicion for **NF2**
- **Presentation:**
  - *Vestibular:* Hearing loss, tinnitus, dizziness; +/- imbalance/ataxia, facial numbness/weakness (mass effect on brainstem, CN V, CN VII)
  - *Peripheral:* Weakness +/- sensory loss; non-invasive, symptoms from pressure on surrounding structures
- **Imaging:** Well-circumscribed, sometimes cystic, heterogeneously contrast-enhancing. **Extra-axial** (located outside the brain parenchyma). Classic CPA (cerebellopontine angle) tumor on T1+contrast MRI
- **Pathology:** Antoni A (dense, organized) and Antoni B (loose) patterns; **Verocay bodies**
- **Treatment:** Surgical resection (complete resection curative in ~98% of acoustic neuromas; partial resection recurrence 15-20%), radiation (smaller tumors or non-surgical candidates), observation (small, slow-growing, asymptomatic -- MRI q6-12 months)
- **Sequelae:** Hearing loss (vestibular schwannoma), numbness/weakness (peripheral)

### Meningiomas
- **What:** Most common primary "brain" tumor (37% of histologically diagnosed brain tumors). Arises from **arachnoid cap cells** of dura mater (neural crest origin). Majority are WHO Grade I ("benign"), slow growing
- **Epidemiology:** Median age 65, F:M 2:1, 29K/year in US, Blacks > whites. Risk factors: increased age, estrogen/progesterone use, increased BMI, cranial radiation (latency ~2 decades). 50% of NF2 patients have intracranial meningiomas
- **Presentation:** Often asymptomatic/incidental. When symptomatic: mass effect (sx depend on location), seizures from cortical irritation, increased ICP (HA worse with Valsalva/lying down, N/V, diplopia, altered mental status)
- **Imaging:** **Extra-axial**, homogenously contrast-enhancing (bright), well-circumscribed, **dural tail**, supratentorial > infratentorial
- **Pathology:** **Whorls of cells** and **psammoma bodies**
- **Treatment:** Observation + annual scans (asymptomatic, slow-growing at ~0.2 cm/yr). Surgical resection (primary Rx for symptomatic; gross total resection often curative with low recurrence; may be partial if near skull base/venous sinuses). Radiation (unresectable or adjunct after subtotal resection; often stereotactic radiosurgery). Chemotherapy is ineffective. Anti-seizure drugs if seizures present

### Glioblastoma (GBM)
- **What:** WHO Grade IV malignant glial tumor. **Most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults.** Previously called glioblastoma multiforme
- **Epidemiology:** Usually adults >60, males slightly > females, 3/100K per year. Risk factors: genetic syndromes (Li-Fraumeni/p53, von Hippel-Lindau, NF-1), prior CNS irradiation
- **Subtypes:** 90% are primary (de novo), IDH-1 wild-type -- older adults, more aggressive. 10% are secondary, IDH-1 mutant -- arise from lower grade tumor, younger, better prognosis
- **Presentation:** Space-occupying lesion (increased ICP, HA, N/V), focal CNS deficits (progressive, depends on location -- weakness, numbness, aphasia, confusion, personality changes, visual deficits), acute seizures (especially with new worrisome HA)
- **Imaging:** Usually **supratentorial**, variably contrast-enhancing with possible **necrotic core** (dark center), surrounding edema. **Never fully resectable** (microscopic spread extends cm beyond radiographic mass). Infiltrates along **white matter tracts**, may cross midline via corpus callosum creating **"butterfly" pattern**
- **Pathology:** **Palisading necrosis**, microvascular proliferation, atypical/pleomorphic nuclei, mitotically active. GFAP positive
- **Treatment:** Surgical resection (greater extent = better survival), radiation (prolongs survival, 60Gy in 2.0Gy fractions), **temozolomide (TMZ)** chemotherapy (DNA alkylating agent, FDA approved, survival benefit with radiation), **tumor treating fields (TTF)** (FDA approved with TMZ post-radiation, 20.9 vs 16.0 months survival). Bevacizumab for recurrent GBM
- **Prognosis:** Virtually uniform progression and death within months to (few) years. Better prognostic factors: younger age, better Karnofsky score, more extensive resection, **MGMT promoter methylation** (predicts TMZ benefit -- silences DNA repair enzyme, increasing sensitivity to TMZ; 5-yr survival 23.4% methylated vs 12.6% unmethylated). IDH-mutant also has better prognosis

### Medulloblastoma
- **What:** Most common malignant pediatric brain tumor. WHO Grade IV. **Posterior fossa**, midline cerebellum usually. "Small blue cell" embryonal tumor
- **Epidemiology:** Peak incidence ages 5-9, 10% of CNS tumors in children <19
- **Presentation:** Increased ICP symptoms (nocturnal/early AM headaches, N/V), **cerebellar symptoms** (ataxia, truncal instability, arm/leg clumsiness, dizziness/diplopia). Grows fast -- symptoms develop over weeks rather than months
- **Imaging:** Contrast-enhancing, midline cerebellar mass. Often causes **secondary obstructive hydrocephalus**
- **Pathology:** Small blue cells; **Homer Wright rosettes**. Precursor may be cerebellar granule cell neurons
- **Treatment:** Surgery (maximal safe resection, confirms dx, alleviates ICP) + radiation (whole brain/spine + extra dose to tumor site) + chemotherapy (vincristine & cisplatin + lomustine or cyclophosphamide)
- **Prognosis:** 75% achieve long-term survival. Better for children >5 yo, worse for young children/infants and adults. TP53 mutation in SHH subtype confers poorer prognosis
- **Treatment sequelae:** Ototoxicity (25%), neurologic sequelae from surgery/radiation (25%), neurocognitive impairment from radiation (especially younger children)

### Pilocytic Astrocytoma (PA)
- **What:** Common pediatric brain tumor, WHO Grade I (low grade glioma), often in cerebellum
- **Epidemiology:** Peak incidence age 5-14. NF-1 patients have increased risk (often optic nerve)
- **Presentation:** Slow growing, gradual symptoms. In kids: tends to occur in deep midline structures (cerebellum 2/3 of pediatric cases, brainstem, chiasm/hypothalamic region). In adults: 1/2 in cerebral hemispheres. Site-specific sx: ataxia/gait disturbance if cerebellar, vision loss if optic chiasm. General: HA, N/V, developmental delay/regression
- **Imaging:** Mass with **solid and cystic parts**, large, well-defined borders. Classic: **cystic mass with enhancing mural nodule** (fluid-filled cyst is dark on T1, mural nodule enhances)
- **Pathology:** **"Piloid" (hair-like) astrocytes**, biphasic pattern (loose-dense), **Rosenthal fibers**
- **Treatment:** Complete surgical resection (often curative). If not possible (brainstem): combination surgery/chemo/radiation
- **Prognosis:** Excellent -- >90% have 10-year survival

### Ependymomas
- **What:** 3rd most common pediatric brain tumor. WHO Grade II or III. Glial tumor arising from ependymal lining of ventricles. Most common in **posterior fossa/4th ventricle**
- **Epidemiology:** Most common <5 years old, rare in adults. Associated with NF2, tuberous sclerosis. Among pediatric posterior fossa tumors: glioma > medulloblastoma > ependymoma
- **Presentation:** 80% present with increased ICP due to **obstructive hydrocephalus** (4th ventricle location), ataxia, CN deficits
- **Imaging:** Heterogeneous enhancement, obstructive hydrocephalus with enlarged ventricles
- **Pathology:** **Perivascular pseudorosettes** (tumor cells surrounding a blood vessel) and **true ependymal rosettes** (have their own lumen, NOT a blood vessel)
- **Treatment:** Maximal safe surgical resection + radiation (after surgery) + chemotherapy (adjuvant in subtotal resection; no targeted treatments exist)
- **Prognosis:** Group A (infancy, 1q gain of function): poor, 5-yr survival 47%. Group B (childhood-adults, chromosomal instability): good, 5-yr survival 83%

### CNS Metastases
- **What:** The **most common brain tumor** overall -- 10x more common than primary brain tumors. Mets = Stage IV cancer
- **Sources:** Brain: breast, lung, melanoma. Spine: breast, lung, prostate, thyroid, renal cell. Hemorrhagic mets: melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, thyroid, lung, breast, hepatocellular
- **Epidemiology:** 30% of adults with cancer have CNS mets
- **Presentation:** Space-occupying lesion or hemorrhagic mets with increased ICP. **New worrisome HAs in a cancer patient require emergency evaluation.** Epidural/leptomeningeal mets: initially radicular, CN palsies, progressing to cortical/spinal symptoms. **Back pain + sudden onset weakness + UMN signs +/- urinary retention = neurologic emergency** for spinal cord compression (high dose steroids + neurosurgery + radiation oncology)
- **Imaging:** Solitary or multiple enhancing lesions. MRI brain or spine +/- contrast; CT head for acute worrisome HA. Must image before LP
- **Diagnosis:** Imaging, LP (opening pressure, cytology -- may need up to 3 large volume 10cc collections; flow cytometry), neurosurgical biopsy

## Key Concept

**Molecular subtyping of tumors has dramatically influenced cancer management** -- examples include IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation in glioblastoma, and molecular grouping in ependymomas and medulloblastomas.
