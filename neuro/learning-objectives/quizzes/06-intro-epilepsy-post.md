# Introduction to Epilepsy — Post-Quiz

**1. A 72-year-old man in rural Guatemala has been having recurrent episodes of staring and right arm shaking for 2 years. He has never seen a neurologist and has not been treated. His family cannot afford transportation to the nearest city hospital. Which of the following best explains why this patient has not received treatment?**

A) Epilepsy is rare in low-income countries, so physicians do not suspect it
B) Anti-seizure medications are contraindicated in elderly patients
C) The treatment gap in low-income countries reaches 75%, driven by poverty and limited access to care
D) Epilepsy in adults over 65 is typically self-limited and does not require treatment

**2. A 45-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department after a witnessed generalized convulsion lasting 2 minutes. She has no prior seizure history. Which of the following best characterizes this event?**

A) This is an unprovoked seizure and she meets criteria for epilepsy
B) This is a provoked seizure due to alcohol withdrawal, and she does not yet meet criteria for epilepsy
C) This meets criteria for epilepsy because any seizure in a patient over 40 is considered epilepsy
D) This is a psychogenic non-epileptic event because it was associated with substance use

**3. A 28-year-old medical student presents after two episodes of loss of consciousness with shaking, occurring 3 months apart. There were no identifiable triggers for either event. Which feature of these episodes is most helpful in supporting that they are true seizures rather than another diagnosis?**

A) The episodes occurred 3 months apart
B) The episodes were stereotyped, transient, and episodic with clear onset and offset
C) The patient is a young adult
D) The patient had post-event confusion

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C
- **A) Epilepsy is rare in low-income countries, so physicians do not suspect it.** Incorrect. Epilepsy is actually more common in low-income countries, with twice the prevalence and incidence compared to high-income countries. Poverty is itself a risk factor for epilepsy.
- **B) Anti-seizure medications are contraindicated in elderly patients.** Incorrect. Anti-seizure medications are not contraindicated in the elderly. Adults over 65 represent one of the peak incidence groups for epilepsy and are routinely treated with ASMs.
- **C) The treatment gap in low-income countries reaches 75%, driven by poverty and limited access to care.** Correct. The treatment gap describes the proportion of people with epilepsy who do not receive needed treatment. In low-income countries, this reaches 75%, compared to 50% in middle-income and 10% in high-income countries. Poverty, limited infrastructure, and lack of access to specialists all contribute.
- **D) Epilepsy in adults over 65 is typically self-limited and does not require treatment.** Incorrect. Epilepsy in older adults is not self-limited and does require treatment. Common causes in this age group include stroke, neurodegenerative diseases, and brain tumors.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) This is an unprovoked seizure and she meets criteria for epilepsy.** Incorrect. This seizure has a clear reversible trigger (alcohol withdrawal) and is therefore a provoked seizure. A single provoked seizure does not meet criteria for epilepsy.
- **B) This is a provoked seizure due to alcohol withdrawal, and she does not yet meet criteria for epilepsy.** Correct. Alcohol withdrawal is a classic reversible trigger that makes this a provoked seizure. Provoked seizures are distinguished from unprovoked seizures, and the diagnosis of epilepsy requires unprovoked seizures. She needs treatment for alcohol withdrawal, but this event alone does not warrant a diagnosis of epilepsy.
- **C) This meets criteria for epilepsy because any seizure in a patient over 40 is considered epilepsy.** Incorrect. Age alone does not determine an epilepsy diagnosis. The criteria require unprovoked seizures regardless of patient age.
- **D) This is a psychogenic non-epileptic event because it was associated with substance use.** Incorrect. PNEE are involuntary episodes due to functional neurologic disorder, not due to substance use. Alcohol withdrawal seizures are true seizures with abnormal electrical activity in the brain, not psychogenic events.

### Q3. Answer: B
- **A) The episodes occurred 3 months apart.** Incorrect. While the timing is relevant for meeting epilepsy diagnostic criteria (more than 24 hours apart), the interval between events does not help distinguish true seizures from mimickers.
- **B) The episodes were stereotyped, transient, and episodic with clear onset and offset.** Correct. The hallmark clinical features of seizures are that they are stereotyped (identical presentation each time), transient (lasting seconds to minutes), and episodic with clear onset and offset. These features are the most helpful in recognizing an event as a seizure. A good history establishes the diagnosis in 90% of cases.
- **C) The patient is a young adult.** Incorrect. While epilepsy can occur in young adults, age alone does not help distinguish seizures from other causes of transient loss of consciousness such as syncope.
- **D) The patient had post-event confusion.** Incorrect. While post-ictal confusion is common after generalized tonic-clonic seizures, it can also occur after syncope or other conditions. It is suggestive but not the most distinguishing feature compared to the stereotyped, transient, episodic nature of seizures.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) Right cerebellar hemisphere.** Incorrect. Cerebellar lesions cause ataxia, dysmetria, and coordination deficits, not seizures with automatisms and epigastric aura. Seizures do not originate from the cerebellum.
- **B) Brainstem reticular formation.** Incorrect. Brainstem pathology causes cranial nerve deficits and long tract signs. Seizures arise from the cerebral cortex, not the brainstem.
- **C) Cerebral cortex, likely left temporal lobe.** Correct. The 3-step approach identifies this as: (1) episodic events (seizures), (2) localized to the cerebral cortex. The epigastric rising sensation, automatisms (lip smacking, fumbling), and impaired consciousness point to temporal lobe origin. The aphasia (inability to speak) in this right-handed patient lateralizes to the left (dominant) hemisphere, consistent with left temporal lobe epilepsy, likely mesial temporal sclerosis.
- **D) Thoracic spinal cord.** Incorrect. Spinal cord lesions cause myelopathic symptoms (weakness, sensory level, bowel/bladder dysfunction), not seizures or automatisms.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Focal seizure with impaired consciousness; treat with carbamazepine.** Incorrect. The brief duration (5-10 seconds), absence of post-event confusion, bilateral generalized 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern on EEG, and typical age of onset all point to generalized absence seizures, not focal seizures.
- **B) Generalized absence seizure; treat with ethosuximide.** Correct. This is a classic presentation of childhood absence epilepsy: elementary school-aged child with behavioral arrest (staring/zoning out), brief duration (seconds), immediate return to activity with no post-ictal period, and 3-4 Hz generalized spike-and-wave discharges on EEG. Ethosuximide is a first-line treatment for absence seizures. Approximately 80% of children outgrow this by age 21.
- **C) Generalized tonic-clonic seizure; treat with phenytoin.** Incorrect. Tonic-clonic seizures involve full body stiffening and rhythmic jerking, not brief staring spells. The clinical description and EEG pattern are characteristic of absence seizures.
- **D) Focal aware seizure; treat with levetiracetam.** Incorrect. The generalized 3 Hz spike-and-wave EEG pattern indicates a generalized, not focal, seizure type. Focal seizures produce focal EEG changes, not generalized discharges.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) Todd paralysis; indicates a postictal phenomenon from any seizure type.** Incorrect. Todd paralysis is a transient postictal weakness or paralysis that occurs after a seizure, not the sequential spread of motor activity during a seizure.
- **B) Jacksonian march; indicates a focal seizure spreading along the motor homunculus in the contralateral cortex.** Correct. A Jacksonian march is the sequential spread of seizure activity along adjacent representations of the sensorimotor homunculus. In this case, the spread from thumb to hand to forearm to upper arm to face corresponds to the organized somatotopic representation in the contralateral (left) motor cortex. This is a classic focal seizure pattern.
- **C) Myoclonic jerk; indicates a generalized seizure from bilateral cortical involvement.** Incorrect. Myoclonic jerks are brief, irregular, generalized jerks that do not show the sequential spreading pattern described. The orderly march from one body part to adjacent body parts is pathognomonic for Jacksonian march.
- **D) Absence seizure; indicates generalized 3 Hz spike-and-wave activity.** Incorrect. Absence seizures present with behavioral arrest and staring, not sequential unilateral motor spread. The described phenomenon is distinctly focal, not generalized.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) Any seizure in an adolescent automatically qualifies as epilepsy.** Incorrect. Age alone does not determine epilepsy diagnosis. The specific diagnostic criteria must be met regardless of patient age.
- **B) Sleep deprivation makes this a provoked seizure, so epilepsy criteria do not apply.** Incorrect. While sleep deprivation can lower seizure threshold, a brain MRI revealing a cortical mass changes the clinical picture significantly. The structural lesion provides the basis for the epilepsy diagnosis, not the sleep deprivation trigger.
- **C) One unprovoked seizure plus an MRI finding (cortical brain tumor) conferring a probability of further seizures of 60% or more meets the diagnostic criteria for epilepsy.** Correct. One of the three ways to diagnose epilepsy is one unprovoked seizure plus a probability of further seizures of 60% or more. A cortical brain tumor on MRI is a classic example of a finding that confers high recurrence risk, meeting this threshold. This patient does not need to wait for a second seizure to be diagnosed with epilepsy.
- **D) Generalized tonic-clonic seizures are always classified as epilepsy regardless of number.** Incorrect. The seizure type does not determine whether it qualifies as epilepsy. The same diagnostic criteria (2 unprovoked seizures >24 hours apart, one seizure with high recurrence risk, or an epilepsy syndrome) apply regardless of seizure type.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) She should try at least 5 medications before considering any other treatment.** Incorrect. Drug-refractory epilepsy is defined as failure of 2 adequate medication trials at therapeutic doses. Evidence shows that after two medication failures, the chance of achieving seizure freedom with additional medications is low, and surgical evaluation should be pursued.
- **B) She meets criteria for drug-refractory epilepsy, and surgical options such as open resection or laser ablation should be evaluated.** Correct. Having failed two adequate medication trials at therapeutic doses defines drug-refractory epilepsy. At this point, referral to a comprehensive epilepsy center is indicated for surgical evaluation. Open resection achieves 58-85% seizure-free rates for temporal lobe epilepsy, and laser ablation achieves approximately 62% seizure-free rates. Device options (VNS, RNS, DBS) are also available.
- **C) Vagus nerve stimulation should always be tried before surgical resection.** Incorrect. VNS is a palliative device therapy that reduces seizure frequency but rarely achieves seizure freedom. Surgical resection offers much higher seizure-free rates and should be evaluated first in appropriate candidates.
- **D) Drug-refractory epilepsy has no further treatment options beyond medications.** Incorrect. Multiple treatment options exist for drug-refractory epilepsy, including open resection, laser ablation, VNS, RNS, and DBS. Referral to a comprehensive epilepsy center is critical.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Generalized tonic-clonic seizures; confirmed with brain MRI.** Incorrect. Several features argue against true epileptic seizures: prolonged duration (10-20 minutes), asynchronous limb movements, eyes closed, no self-injury, and a normal EEG during the event. True generalized tonic-clonic seizures are always associated with epileptiform activity on EEG.
- **B) Psychogenic non-epileptic events (PNEE); confirmed with video-EEG monitoring.** Correct. PNEE is the most common seizure mimicker. Features suggestive of PNEE include prolonged duration, asynchronous movements, eyes closed during events, lack of injury, and a normal EEG during the episode. Video-EEG monitoring is the gold standard for diagnosis, capturing clinical events while simultaneously demonstrating the absence of epileptiform discharges.
- **C) Absence seizures; confirmed with 24-hour ambulatory EEG.** Incorrect. Absence seizures are brief (seconds), present with behavioral arrest and staring (not prolonged shaking), and show characteristic 3-4 Hz generalized spike-and-wave discharges on EEG.
- **D) Syncope; confirmed with tilt-table testing.** Incorrect. Syncope presents with brief loss of consciousness due to transient cerebral hypoperfusion, not prolonged shaking episodes lasting 10-20 minutes with asynchronous movements.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) 12 months seizure-free; anxiety.** Incorrect. California requires 6 months seizure-free for driving, not 12 months. While anxiety is a co-morbidity in epilepsy, depression is the most common psychiatric co-morbidity.
- **B) 3 months seizure-free; bipolar disorder.** Incorrect. Three months is too short; the California requirement is 6 months seizure-free. Bipolar disorder is not the most common psychiatric co-morbidity.
- **C) 6 months seizure-free; depression.** Correct. In California, patients with epilepsy must be seizure-free for at least 6 months to drive. Depression is the most common psychiatric co-morbidity in epilepsy, affecting 1 in 10 people with the condition. This patient's symptoms of hopelessness, anhedonia, and insomnia strongly suggest depression and warrant screening and treatment.
- **D) 24 months seizure-free; PTSD.** Incorrect. Twenty-four months is not the California requirement (it is 6 months). PTSD, while possible, is not the most common psychiatric co-morbidity in epilepsy.

### Q11. Answer: B
- **A) He now has epilepsy and will need lifelong anti-seizure medications.** Incorrect. A febrile seizure is a provoked seizure (triggered by fever) and does not meet the criteria for epilepsy. Most children with simple febrile seizures have an excellent prognosis.
- **B) This is likely a febrile seizure, which is a provoked seizure; most children with simple febrile seizures do not develop epilepsy.** Correct. Febrile seizures are the most common seizure type in children and are provoked by fever. Simple febrile seizures (generalized, lasting less than 15 minutes, occurring once in 24 hours, with normal neurologic exam) carry a low risk of developing epilepsy. While febrile seizures are listed as a risk factor, the vast majority of children do not go on to develop epilepsy.
- **C) Febrile seizures always progress to mesial temporal sclerosis in adulthood.** Incorrect. While there is an association between prolonged or complex febrile seizures and later mesial temporal sclerosis, simple febrile seizures do not "always" progress to MTS. The relationship is complex and most children with febrile seizures do not develop temporal lobe epilepsy.
- **D) He should undergo immediate surgical evaluation for epilepsy.** Incorrect. Surgical evaluation is reserved for drug-refractory epilepsy (failure of at least two medications). A single febrile seizure does not warrant surgical evaluation.

### Q12. Answer: C
- **A) Place a spoon in the patient's mouth to prevent tongue biting.** Incorrect. Placing objects in the mouth of a seizing patient is explicitly contraindicated. It can cause dental injury, aspiration, or injury to the rescuer. This is a common myth about seizure first aid.
- **B) Restrain the patient's arms and legs to prevent injury.** Incorrect. Restraining a seizing patient is contraindicated and can cause musculoskeletal injury. The appropriate action is to cushion the head, clear the area of dangerous objects, and let the seizure run its course.
- **C) Call 911 because a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes may be status epilepticus, which is a medical emergency; position the patient on their side.** Correct. Status epilepticus is defined as a continuous seizure lasting 5 minutes or more and is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment. At 6 minutes, this patient may be in status epilepticus. Calling 911 is essential, as rescue medications (e.g., 2 mg lorazepam) and advanced care are needed. Positioning on the side helps prevent aspiration.
- **D) Attempt CPR immediately because the patient is likely in cardiac arrest.** Incorrect. A generalized tonic-clonic seizure is not cardiac arrest. The patient's movements indicate brain activity, not cardiac arrest. CPR is not indicated during an active seizure. The priority is seizure first aid: protect the airway, time the seizure, and call for help if it exceeds 5 minutes.
