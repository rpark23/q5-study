# Introduction to Neuropathology — Pre-Quiz

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) The skull can expand to accommodate increases in brain volume during inflammation.** Incorrect. The skull is a rigid, non-expandable structure in adults. This is precisely why the closed-box principle exists -- there is no room for expansion.
- **B) Intracranial contents are limited to brain tissue, CSF, and blood, so any added mass raises intracranial pressure.** Correct. The Monro-Kellie doctrine states that the cranium is a fixed-volume compartment containing brain parenchyma, CSF, and blood. Any addition (e.g., a mass lesion) must displace one of these components or pressure will rise.
- **C) The brain is protected from external injury because the skull absorbs all traumatic force.** Incorrect. While the skull does provide mechanical protection, the closed-box principle specifically refers to the fixed intracranial volume and pressure dynamics, not impact absorption.
- **D) CSF production ceases when intracranial pressure rises above a critical threshold.** Incorrect. CSF production continues relatively independent of intracranial pressure. The closed-box principle describes volume-pressure relationships, not CSF production regulation.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) Subfalcine herniation.** Incorrect. Subfalcine herniation involves displacement of brain tissue under the falx cerebri. It can cause anterior cerebral artery compression but is not the lethal herniation type that compresses respiratory centers.
- **B) Uncal herniation.** Incorrect. Uncal herniation involves the medial temporal lobe herniating through the tentorial notch. It can cause third nerve palsy and posterior cerebral artery compression but does not directly compress the medullary respiratory centers.
- **C) Tonsillar herniation.** Correct. Tonsillar herniation occurs when the cerebellar tonsils are displaced downward through the foramen magnum, directly compressing the medulla oblongata and its respiratory centers. This is the only lethal form of herniation.
- **D) Transtentorial herniation.** Incorrect. Transtentorial herniation involves downward displacement of brain through the tentorial notch. While serious, the lecture specifically identifies tonsillar herniation as the only lethal form because of direct medullary compression.

### Q3. Answer: B
- **A) Gray matter lacks a blood-brain barrier.** Incorrect. Gray matter does have a blood-brain barrier. The increased susceptibility to hypoxic-ischemic injury relates to metabolic demand, not barrier integrity.
- **B) Gray matter contains neuronal cell bodies with higher metabolic demands.** Correct. Neuronal cell bodies in gray matter have very high metabolic requirements, including oxygen and glucose consumption. This makes them particularly vulnerable when blood supply or oxygenation is compromised.
- **C) Gray matter is located closer to the skull surface and is exposed to more mechanical stress.** Incorrect. While cortical gray matter is superficial, deep gray matter structures (basal ganglia, thalamus) are also vulnerable. The susceptibility relates to metabolic demand, not anatomical position.
- **D) Gray matter has a lower density of glial cells and therefore less structural support.** Incorrect. Gray matter contains abundant neuropil and glial cells. The selective vulnerability is driven by the high metabolic rate of neurons, not a lack of glial support.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) Hypothalamus.** Incorrect. The hypothalamus is relatively resistant to hypoxia-ischemia and ranks near the bottom of the vulnerability hierarchy in adults.
- **B) Brain stem.** Incorrect. The brain stem is more resistant than cortical and hippocampal structures. It falls in the lower half of the adult vulnerability hierarchy.
- **C) Hippocampus (CA1/Sommer's sector).** Correct. The hippocampal CA1 region (Sommer's sector) is the single most vulnerable brain region to global hypoxia-ischemia in adults. This is the top of the hierarchical vulnerability list and is a classic site for red dead neurons after cardiac arrest.
- **D) Spinal cord.** Incorrect. The spinal cord is the least vulnerable structure on the hierarchical list for hypoxia-ischemia in adults. It is the most resistant of the listed structures.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Identifying reactive astrocytes.** Incorrect. Reactive astrocytes are identified using GFAP immunohistochemistry, not Luxol Fast Blue.
- **B) Staining myelin blue to detect demyelination.** Correct. Luxol Fast Blue is a special stain that selectively binds to myelin, turning it blue. Loss of blue staining indicates demyelination, making it essential for evaluating white matter diseases such as multiple sclerosis and leukodystrophies.
- **C) Highlighting microglial activation.** Incorrect. Microglia are identified using CD68 immunohistochemistry, not LFB.
- **D) Detecting amyloid plaques.** Incorrect. Amyloid plaques are typically detected with silver stains or amyloid-beta immunohistochemistry. LFB is specific for myelin.

### Q6. Answer: D
- **A) GFAP.** Incorrect. GFAP (Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein) is a marker for astrocytes and reactive gliosis, not axonal injury.
- **B) Synaptophysin.** Incorrect. Synaptophysin marks presynaptic vesicle proteins and is used to identify synaptic structures, not acute axonal damage.
- **C) CD68.** Incorrect. CD68 is a marker for microglia and macrophages. While these cells respond to injury, CD68 does not directly detect axonal damage.
- **D) Beta-APP (Beta-Amyloid Precursor Protein).** Correct. Beta-APP is upregulated immediately after axonal injury and accumulates at sites of axonal disruption. It is the most sensitive immunohistochemical marker for detecting acute axonal injury, including diffuse axonal injury after trauma.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Neurons undergoing chromatolysis with an enlarged cell body and peripheral nucleus.** Incorrect. This describes chromatolysis, which is a regenerative response to axonal injury, not neuronal death. Chromatolysis features cell body enlargement, not shrinkage.
- **B) Shrunken neurons with nuclear pyknosis and intense cytoplasmic eosinophilia, appearing 6-12 hours after irreversible hypoxic-ischemic injury.** Correct. Red dead neurons are the earliest morphologic marker of neuronal death. They show cell shrinkage, loss of the nucleolus, loss of Nissl substance, and intense eosinophilic (red) staining of the cytoplasm on H&E.
- **C) Neurons distended with storage material and a displaced nucleus.** Incorrect. This describes neuronal storage disorders (e.g., Tay-Sachs disease), where indigestible material accumulates and balloons the cell body.
- **D) Mineralized neuronal remnants that stain purple on H&E.** Incorrect. This describes ferrugination (mineralization), which is a later process seen especially in neonatal brains after hypoxic-ischemic injury. It represents mineral deposition in already-dead neurons.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) The formation of axonal spheroids after traumatic brain injury.** Incorrect. Axonal spheroid formation results from disrupted axoplasmic flow in sheared axons and is a feature of diffuse axonal injury, not neuronophagia.
- **B) Engulfment and removal of dead or dying neurons by microglia.** Correct. Neuronophagia is the process in which activated microglia surround and phagocytose damaged or dead neurons. It is a classic histological finding in viral encephalitis and results in residual microglial nodules.
- **C) The process by which oligodendrocytes remyelinate damaged axons.** Incorrect. Oligodendrocytes are responsible for myelination, but neuronophagia specifically refers to microglial phagocytosis of neurons.
- **D) Proliferation of astrocytes around areas of neuronal loss.** Incorrect. This describes reactive gliosis (astrogliosis), which is the astrocytic response to CNS injury. Neuronophagia is specifically a microglial process.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) Neurofibrillary tangles.** Incorrect. Neurofibrillary tangles are composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein and are characteristic of Alzheimer disease, not Parkinson disease.
- **B) Negri bodies.** Incorrect. Negri bodies are intracytoplasmic inclusions found in neurons infected with rabies virus. They are not associated with Parkinson disease.
- **C) Lewy bodies.** Correct. Lewy bodies are intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions composed primarily of alpha-synuclein. They are found in neurons of the substantia nigra and other brain regions in Parkinson disease and are the pathological hallmark of the condition.
- **D) Cowdry type A bodies.** Incorrect. Cowdry type A inclusion bodies are intranuclear inclusions associated with viral infections such as CMV, Herpes simplex, and subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE).

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) Production of myelin sheaths around CNS axons.** Incorrect. Myelin production in the CNS is the function of oligodendrocytes, not astrocytes. In the PNS, Schwann cells serve this role.
- **B) Induction of blood-brain barrier properties in endothelial cells and neurotransmitter uptake.** Correct. Astrocytes have numerous critical functions including inducing blood-brain barrier properties in cerebral endothelial cells through their perivascular foot processes, and uptake and inactivation of neurotransmitters (especially glutamate). They also promote angiogenesis and buffer the extracellular space.
- **C) Generation of cerebrospinal fluid within the choroid plexus.** Incorrect. CSF is produced by the choroid plexus epithelium, not by astrocytes. The choroid plexus is a specialized structure within the ventricular system.
- **D) Phagocytosis of dead neurons as the primary immune cell of the CNS.** Incorrect. Microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS and are responsible for phagocytosis of dead neurons (neuronophagia). Astrocytes respond to injury with gliosis but are not primarily phagocytic.
