# CNS Demyelinating Diseases — Pre-Quiz

### Q1. Which glial cell is responsible for myelinating axons in the CNS?

- A) Schwann cell
- B) Astrocyte
- C) Oligodendrocyte
- D) Microglia

### Q2. What distinguishes primary demyelination from secondary demyelination?

- A) Primary demyelination is caused by axon destruction; secondary demyelination targets myelin directly
- B) Primary demyelination involves direct stripping of myelin from intact axons; secondary demyelination results from axon loss
- C) Primary demyelination occurs only in the PNS; secondary demyelination occurs only in the CNS
- D) Primary demyelination is always genetic; secondary demyelination is always autoimmune

### Q3. Which immune cells cross the blood-brain barrier and initiate the inflammatory cascade in MS lesions?

- A) Eosinophils
- B) Natural killer cells
- C) Activated T cells
- D) Mast cells

### Q4. Oligoclonal bands detected in the CSF of MS patients are produced by which cell type?

- A) Oligodendrocytes
- B) Clonally expanded B cells
- C) CD8+ T cells
- D) Astrocytes

### Q5. What is the approximate female-to-male ratio in multiple sclerosis?

- A) 1:1
- B) 2:1
- C) 3-4:1
- D) 10:1

### Q6. Which infection is found in nearly 100% of MS patients and is considered a risk factor for the disease?

- A) Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
- B) Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6)
- C) Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)
- D) Varicella-zoster virus (VZV)

### Q7. What percentage of MS patients initially present with a relapsing-remitting clinical course (RRMS)?

- A) 50%
- B) 65%
- C) 85%
- D) 95%

### Q8. A lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) in the brainstem produces which clinical finding?

- A) Optic neuritis
- B) Lhermitte sign
- C) Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)
- D) Scanning speech

### Q9. What are "Dawson's fingers" on MRI?

- A) Enhancing cortical lesions along the central sulcus
- B) Periventricular lesions extending perpendicular to the ventricles
- C) Ring-enhancing lesions in the basal ganglia
- D) Longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions

### Q10. The McDonald criteria for diagnosing MS require demonstration of which two principles?

- A) Dissemination in space and dissemination in time
- B) Positive oligoclonal bands and elevated IgG index
- C) Bilateral optic neuritis and transverse myelitis
- D) Gadolinium-enhancing lesions and cerebral atrophy

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C
- **A) Schwann cell.** Incorrect. Schwann cells myelinate axons in the peripheral nervous system (PNS), not the CNS. Each Schwann cell myelinates only a single axon, in contrast to oligodendrocytes.
- **B) Astrocyte.** Incorrect. Astrocytes provide metabolic support, maintain the blood-brain barrier, and regulate ion concentrations, but they do not produce myelin in the CNS.
- **C) Oligodendrocyte.** Correct. Oligodendrocytes are the myelinating cells of the CNS. A single oligodendrocyte wraps its processes around multiple axons in a spiral fashion, forming compact myelin sheaths that enable saltatory conduction.
- **D) Microglia.** Incorrect. Microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS. They participate in immune surveillance and phagocytosis but are not involved in myelin production.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) Primary demyelination is caused by axon destruction; secondary demyelination targets myelin directly.** Incorrect. This reverses the definitions. Primary demyelination targets myelin directly while axons remain relatively intact; secondary demyelination is the consequence of axon destruction.
- **B) Primary demyelination involves direct stripping of myelin from intact axons; secondary demyelination results from axon loss.** Correct. In primary demyelination (e.g., MS), macrophages strip myelin from axons that are initially preserved. In secondary demyelination (e.g., from infarct or trauma), the axon is destroyed first, and the myelin degenerates because there is no longer an axon to ensheath.
- **C) Primary demyelination occurs only in the PNS; secondary demyelination occurs only in the CNS.** Incorrect. Both types of demyelination can occur in either the CNS or PNS. For example, MS (CNS) and Guillain-Barre syndrome (PNS) are both primary demyelinating diseases.
- **D) Primary demyelination is always genetic; secondary demyelination is always autoimmune.** Incorrect. Primary demyelination can be autoimmune (MS) or genetic (leukodystrophies). Secondary demyelination typically follows vascular, traumatic, or degenerative injury to axons.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Eosinophils.** Incorrect. Eosinophils are primarily involved in parasitic infections and allergic responses. They are not the principal cells initiating the inflammatory cascade in MS lesions.
- **B) Natural killer cells.** Incorrect. While NK cells may have some role in immune regulation, the initiation of MS pathology is driven by activated T cells that cross the blood-brain barrier and are reactivated in the perivascular space.
- **C) Activated T cells.** Correct. In MS, activated T cells cross the blood-brain barrier, become reactivated in the perivascular space, and initiate an inflammatory cascade. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells infiltrate acute lesions, recruiting additional immune cells that destroy myelin.
- **D) Mast cells.** Incorrect. Mast cells are involved in allergic and anaphylactic reactions. They are not the primary initiators of the immune-mediated demyelination seen in MS.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Oligodendrocytes.** Incorrect. Oligodendrocytes are the myelin-producing cells of the CNS. They are targets of the immune attack in MS, not producers of oligoclonal bands.
- **B) Clonally expanded B cells.** Correct. Oligoclonal bands represent limited classes of antibodies (immunoglobulins) produced by clonally expanded B cell populations within the CNS. They are detected in the CSF but not in serum and are present in 90-95% of MS patients.
- **C) CD8+ T cells.** Incorrect. CD8+ T cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes that infiltrate MS lesions and directly damage myelin. However, they do not produce antibodies; antibody production is a function of B cells and plasma cells.
- **D) Astrocytes.** Incorrect. Astrocytes contribute to gliosis (scarring) in chronic MS lesions and provide structural support, but they do not produce antibodies or oligoclonal bands.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) 1:1.** Incorrect. MS does not affect males and females equally. There is a well-established female predominance in MS incidence.
- **B) 2:1.** Incorrect. While MS is more common in females, the ratio is higher than 2:1. A 2:1 ratio underestimates the female predominance seen in epidemiological studies.
- **C) 3-4:1.** Correct. MS has an approximately 3-4:1 female-to-male ratio. This sex difference is thought to relate to hormonal and immune modulation, as disease activity changes during pregnancy (decreasing in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters and increasing postpartum).
- **D) 10:1.** Incorrect. While some autoimmune diseases approach a 10:1 female-to-male ratio (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), MS has a less extreme but still significant female predominance of 3-4:1.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Cytomegalovirus (CMV).** Incorrect. CMV is a common herpesvirus but has not been established as a major risk factor for MS. Unlike EBV, CMV seropositivity does not approach near-universal prevalence in MS patients.
- **B) Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6).** Incorrect. While HHV-6 has been investigated as a potential environmental trigger for MS, the association is not as strong or consistent as that established for EBV.
- **C) Epstein-Barr virus (EBV).** Correct. Nearly 100% of MS patients are EBV-seropositive, compared to 83-90% of the general adult population. MS patients also have higher EBV antibody levels, and a history of clinical EBV infection (infectious mononucleosis) nearly doubles the risk of developing MS.
- **D) Varicella-zoster virus (VZV).** Incorrect. VZV causes chickenpox and shingles but is not the infection most strongly associated with MS risk. EBV has the strongest and most consistent association with MS among infectious agents.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) 50%.** Incorrect. This significantly underestimates the proportion of MS patients who present with a relapsing-remitting course. RRMS is the most common initial presentation by a wide margin.
- **B) 65%.** Incorrect. While a majority, 65% underestimates the true proportion. RRMS accounts for a larger percentage of initial MS presentations.
- **C) 85%.** Correct. Approximately 85% of MS patients initially present with RRMS, characterized by distinct flares of neurologic symptoms with periods of recovery in between. The remaining approximately 15% present with primary progressive MS (PPMS), which involves progressive disability from onset without distinct relapses.
- **D) 95%.** Incorrect. While RRMS is the most common presentation, 95% overestimates it. Approximately 15% of patients present with PPMS, making the RRMS proportion closer to 85%.

### Q8. Answer: C
- **A) Optic neuritis.** Incorrect. Optic neuritis results from inflammation of the optic nerve, not the MLF. It presents with vision loss, a relative afferent pupillary defect (rAPD), and red desaturation.
- **B) Lhermitte sign.** Incorrect. Lhermitte sign (an electric shock sensation down the spine with neck flexion) results from demyelination of the posterior columns of the cervical spinal cord, not from a brainstem MLF lesion.
- **C) Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO).** Correct. The MLF connects the abducens nucleus (CN VI) to the contralateral oculomotor nucleus (CN III), coordinating conjugate horizontal gaze. A lesion in the MLF disrupts this pathway, causing INO, which is characterized by impaired adduction of the ipsilateral eye and nystagmus of the abducting contralateral eye. INO is a classic brainstem finding in MS.
- **D) Scanning speech.** Incorrect. Scanning (telegraphic) speech is a cerebellar finding resulting from impaired coordination of the muscles of speech. It results from cerebellar lesions, not MLF lesions in the brainstem.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Enhancing cortical lesions along the central sulcus.** Incorrect. Dawson's fingers are not cortical lesions. They are a characteristic pattern of white matter lesions found in a specific periventricular distribution.
- **B) Periventricular lesions extending perpendicular to the ventricles.** Correct. Dawson's fingers are a classic MRI finding in MS, consisting of ovoid white matter lesions oriented perpendicular to the lateral ventricles. They appear bright on T2/FLAIR sequences and reflect demyelination along the perivenular spaces radiating outward from the ventricles.
- **C) Ring-enhancing lesions in the basal ganglia.** Incorrect. Ring-enhancing lesions in the basal ganglia are more characteristic of infections (e.g., toxoplasmosis) or tumors. Dawson's fingers are non-ring-enhancing periventricular lesions with a distinctive perpendicular orientation.
- **D) Longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions.** Incorrect. Longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions (spanning greater than 3 vertebral bodies) are characteristic of neuromyelitis optica (NMO), not MS. MS spinal cord lesions are typically shorter, spanning less than 3 vertebral bodies.

### Q10. Answer: A
- **A) Dissemination in space and dissemination in time.** Correct. The McDonald criteria require evidence of both dissemination in space (inflammation in at least 2 distinct CNS regions) and dissemination in time (CNS inflammation at 2 different time points). These can be met clinically, radiographically, or by a combination of both.
- **B) Positive oligoclonal bands and elevated IgG index.** Incorrect. While oligoclonal bands are found in 90-95% of MS patients and support the diagnosis, the McDonald criteria are fundamentally built on demonstrating dissemination in space and time, not solely on CSF findings.
- **C) Bilateral optic neuritis and transverse myelitis.** Incorrect. This combination of severe bilateral optic neuritis and transverse myelitis is more suggestive of neuromyelitis optica (NMO) than MS. The McDonald criteria are broader and require evidence of lesions in multiple CNS areas over time.
- **D) Gadolinium-enhancing lesions and cerebral atrophy.** Incorrect. While gadolinium-enhancing lesions can help demonstrate dissemination in time (indicating active inflammation), cerebral atrophy is not a specific component of the McDonald criteria. The criteria require both spatial and temporal dissemination.
