**Q1.** A 72-year-old right-handed woman is brought to the emergency department after her husband noticed she suddenly stopped speaking mid-sentence during breakfast. On examination, she is alert but unable to produce fluent speech. She can follow simple commands. She has right-sided facial droop affecting the lower face with forehead sparing, and right arm and leg weakness with hyperreflexia and a positive Babinski sign on the right. Where is the most likely lesion?

A) Right frontal cortex
B) Left frontal cortex (Broca area) with involvement of the left corticospinal tract
C) Left peripheral CN VII and brachial plexus
D) Left brainstem (pons)

**Q12.** A 55-year-old man presents after being found wandering and confused. On examination, he has fluent but nonsensical speech with frequent paraphasic errors. He cannot follow two-step commands and is unable to repeat phrases. He does not appear to be aware that his speech is abnormal. He has a right visual field deficit. Motor strength, reflexes, and sensation to light touch are intact in all extremities. Where is the most likely lesion?

A) Left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca area)
B) Left posterior superior temporal gyrus (Wernicke area) with involvement of left optic radiations
C) Right parietal cortex
D) Bilateral frontal lobes

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Right frontal cortex.** Incorrect. A right cortical lesion would produce left-sided body weakness (contralateral deficits from the corticospinal tract crossing at the medullary pyramids). This patient has right-sided weakness. Additionally, in a right-handed individual, language is almost always lateralized to the left hemisphere, so a right frontal lesion would not typically cause aphasia.
- **B) Left frontal cortex (Broca area) with involvement of the left corticospinal tract.** Correct. This patient has non-fluent (expressive) aphasia with preserved comprehension, localizing to the left Broca area. The contralateral (right-sided) face and body weakness with UMN signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski) indicates left corticospinal tract involvement. The forehead-sparing facial weakness is a UMN pattern (central facial palsy). A single left middle cerebral artery territory infarction explains all findings.
- **C) Left peripheral CN VII and brachial plexus.** Incorrect. A peripheral CN VII lesion would affect the entire ipsilateral hemi-face including the forehead, not just the lower face. A brachial plexus lesion would produce LMN signs (hyporeflexia, flaccidity), not UMN signs. This combination also fails Occam's Razor as it requires two separate lesions and does not explain the aphasia.
- **D) Left brainstem (pons).** Incorrect. While a left pontine lesion could cause right-sided weakness, it would be expected to produce brainstem signs (the "D's") such as diplopia, dysconjugate gaze, or dysphagia, and crossed signs (ipsilateral cranial nerve deficits with contralateral body weakness). Aphasia is a cortical sign and would not result from a brainstem lesion.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) Myelopathy with UMN signs.** Incorrect. Myelopathy (spinal cord disease) produces UMN signs including hyperreflexia, spasticity, and Babinski signs below the lesion level. This patient has absent reflexes and a stocking distribution of sensory loss, which is inconsistent with a cord lesion. Myelopathy would also typically show a discrete sensory level rather than gradient distal-to-proximal loss.
- **B) Length-dependent peripheral polyneuropathy.** Correct. This is a classic presentation of diabetic polyneuropathy. The symmetric distal stocking-distribution sensory loss, absent ankle reflexes, and distal weakness are hallmarks of a length-dependent polyneuropathy where the longest nerves are affected first. The gradual proximal progression (feet to mid-calf) and difficulty with balance in the dark (proprioceptive loss) further support this diagnosis.
- **C) Bilateral L5 radiculopathy.** Incorrect. While L5 radiculopathy can cause toe extensor weakness, bilateral radiculopathy would be uncommon and would not explain the diffuse stocking-distribution sensory loss or the absent ankle reflexes (which are S1-mediated). Radiculopathy follows a dermatomal pattern, not a length-dependent gradient.
- **D) Cerebellar ataxia.** Incorrect. Cerebellar lesions produce pure motor symptoms (ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor) without sensory deficits. This patient has prominent sensory findings (numbness, decreased vibration and light touch) and hyporeflexia, which are PNS features, not cerebellar.

### Q3. Answer: B
- **A) Bilateral cerebral cortex (parasagittal lesion).** Incorrect. While a parasagittal lesion (such as a falcine meningioma) can cause bilateral leg weakness by compressing the medial motor cortex bilaterally, it would not explain a discrete sensory level at T10. Cortical lesions would also typically produce cortical signs such as seizures or cognitive changes, and the sensory pattern would follow a cortical distribution rather than a spinal level.
- **B) Thoracic spinal cord at approximately T10.** Correct. This patient has the hallmarks of a spinal cord lesion (myelopathy): bilateral UMN signs below the lesion (spastic paraparesis, hyperreflexia, Babinski signs), a discrete sensory level (T10), bladder dysfunction (urinary retention), and absent cortical or cranial nerve involvement. The T10 sensory level localizes the lesion to the thoracic spinal cord. The subacute progressive time course suggests an inflammatory, compressive, or demyelinating process.
- **C) Bilateral lumbar plexopathy.** Incorrect. Lumbar plexopathy is a PNS lesion that would produce LMN signs (flaccidity, hyporeflexia, atrophy), not UMN signs. Bilateral plexopathy would be extremely unusual and would not produce a clear sensory level or Babinski signs.
- **D) Cauda equina syndrome.** Incorrect. Cauda equina syndrome affects the nerve roots below the conus medullaris and produces LMN signs (flaccidity, areflexia), saddle anesthesia, and bladder dysfunction. This patient has UMN signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski signs), which indicates the lesion is above the conus medullaris in the spinal cord proper.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Left cerebral cortex (motor strip).** Incorrect. A cortical (UMN) lesion would produce contralateral lower face weakness with forehead sparing because the upper face receives bilateral cortical innervation. This patient has involvement of the entire right hemi-face including the forehead, which indicates an LMN lesion. A cortical lesion would also not explain the hyperacusis or taste disturbance.
- **B) Right CN VII (peripheral, LMN lesion).** Correct. The entire right hemi-face is weak (including inability to raise the eyebrow and close the eye), which is the hallmark of an LMN CN VII lesion. The hyperacusis (sounds louder in the right ear) indicates involvement of the stapedial nerve branch, and the taste disturbance on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue indicates involvement of the chorda tympani branch. This presentation is classic for Bell palsy affecting the right facial nerve.
- **C) Right brainstem (pontine infarct).** Incorrect. While a pontine lesion could damage the CN VII nucleus and produce an LMN-type facial weakness, a brainstem stroke would be expected to cause additional neurologic deficits such as contralateral body weakness (crossed signs), dysconjugate gaze, or other cranial nerve palsies. This patient's examination is otherwise completely normal, making a peripheral nerve lesion far more likely.
- **D) Left internal capsule.** Incorrect. An internal capsule lesion would produce a UMN pattern of contralateral facial weakness (forehead-sparing) along with contralateral hemiparesis, as the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts pass through the internal capsule. It would not affect taste or the stapedial reflex, which are peripheral CN VII functions.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) Acute onset (seconds to minutes).** Incorrect. Acute onset over seconds to minutes suggests a vascular event such as stroke or hemorrhage. This patient's episodes develop over days and the overall course spans months, which is inconsistent with a vascular etiology.
- **B) Chronic progressive (months to years).** Incorrect. A chronic progressive course without remissions suggests a neurodegenerative process such as Alzheimer or Parkinson disease. This patient has distinct attacks with periods of recovery between them, indicating a relapsing pattern rather than steady progression.
- **C) Waxing and waning with attacks separated in time and space.** Correct. This is the classic relapsing-remitting pattern of multiple sclerosis. The episodes are separated in time (occurring months apart) and in space (different anatomical locations: sensory cortex/brainstem for arm numbness, optic nerve for vision loss, corticospinal tract for leg weakness). The subacute development over days with subsequent resolution is characteristic of inflammatory demyelinating disease. The MRI showing multiple periventricular white matter lesions confirms the diagnosis.
- **D) Episodic (minutes to hours) with complete resolution.** Incorrect. Episodic events lasting minutes to hours suggest TIA, seizures, or migraine. This patient's attacks last days to weeks, which is much longer than typical episodic events and more consistent with an inflammatory process.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Right cerebellar hemisphere.** Incorrect. Cerebellar lesions produce ipsilateral limb ataxia (due to double-crossing of cerebellar fibers), intention tremor (worsening on approach to a target), and dysmetria. Cerebellar tremor is an action/intention tremor, not a resting tremor. Rigidity and bradykinesia are not cerebellar features.
- **B) Left corticospinal tract.** Incorrect. A corticospinal tract (UMN) lesion would produce contralateral spastic weakness with hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign, not a resting tremor, rigidity, or bradykinesia. The pyramidal pattern of weakness (flexor > extensor in the upper limb) differs from the generalized slowness of bradykinesia.
- **C) Basal ganglia (extrapyramidal system).** Correct. This is a classic presentation of Parkinson disease, which results from degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra within the basal ganglia circuit. The cardinal features are all present: resting tremor (pill-rolling), rigidity (lead-pipe), bradykinesia (slowness), and postural instability. The Parkinsonian gait with stooped posture, shuffling, reduced arm swing, and festinating steps is characteristic. Basal ganglia lesions produce contralateral motor deficits, consistent with the right-sided predominance.
- **D) Right peripheral nerves.** Incorrect. Peripheral nerve lesions produce LMN signs including hyporeflexia, flaccidity, atrophy, and fasciculations, often with sensory deficits in a specific nerve distribution. Resting tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia are not features of peripheral nerve disease.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) Myasthenia gravis.** Incorrect. Myasthenia gravis (a neuromuscular junction disorder) produces fatiguable weakness that worsens with repetitive use and improves with rest. Reflexes are preserved and there are no sensory deficits. Critically, myasthenia gravis does not cause fasciculations, muscle atrophy (until very late), or UMN signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski). The combination of UMN and LMN signs rules out a pure NMJ disorder.
- **B) Cervical myelopathy.** Incorrect. Cervical myelopathy (spinal cord compression) produces UMN signs in the limbs below the lesion and may cause LMN signs at the level of the lesion in the arms. However, it would not explain the tongue fasciculations and atrophy (which indicate involvement of CN XII or its nucleus in the brainstem). Myelopathy also typically produces sensory deficits, which are absent here.
- **C) Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (motor neuron disease).** Correct. ALS is characterized by the simultaneous presence of UMN signs (brisk reflexes, Babinski signs) and LMN signs (fasciculations, atrophy, weakness) without sensory involvement. This patient demonstrates both: tongue fasciculations and hand atrophy (LMN, anterior horn cell degeneration) combined with hyperreflexia and bilateral Babinski signs (UMN, corticospinal tract degeneration). The bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria) with tongue involvement indicate brainstem motor neuron involvement. The absence of sensory deficits is a key feature of motor neuron disease.
- **D) Polymyositis.** Incorrect. Polymyositis is an inflammatory myopathy that causes symmetric proximal weakness (shoulder and hip girdle). It does not produce fasciculations, tongue atrophy, hyperreflexia, or Babinski signs. Myopathies are pure motor problems with preserved reflexes (until late) and no UMN features.

### Q8. Answer: C
- **A) Left homonymous hemianopsia.** Incorrect. A homonymous hemianopsia (loss of the same visual field in both eyes) results from a lesion posterior to the optic chiasm, such as in the optic tract, lateral geniculate nucleus, or optic radiations. A pituitary tumor compresses the chiasm, not the post-chiasmal structures.
- **B) Right monocular vision loss.** Incorrect. Monocular vision loss indicates a pre-chiasmal lesion affecting one optic nerve or eye. A pituitary tumor sits at the chiasm and compresses the crossing fibers, affecting both eyes, not just one.
- **C) Bitemporal hemianopsia.** Correct. A pituitary macroadenoma grows superiorly and compresses the optic chiasm from below. The nasal retinal fibers that cross at the chiasm are preferentially affected, resulting in loss of the temporal visual fields bilaterally (bitemporal hemianopsia). This explains the patient's difficulty seeing objects approaching from the sides. The clinical symptoms of amenorrhea and galactorrhea suggest a prolactinoma, a common type of pituitary adenoma.
- **D) Right superior quadrantanopsia ("pie in the sky").** Incorrect. A superior quadrantanopsia results from a lesion in Meyer's loop (the inferior portion of the optic radiations in the temporal lobe), not from a chiasmal lesion. This would affect only one side and would be a post-chiasmal deficit.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Left cerebral cortex.** Incorrect. A cortical lesion would produce contralateral deficits (right face and body weakness) with cortical signs (the "A's" such as aphasia, neglect, or visual field cuts). It would not produce ipsilateral cranial nerve deficits combined with contralateral body weakness (crossed signs). Cortical lesions also do not typically cause diplopia or dysphagia.
- **B) Left brainstem.** Correct. "Crossed signs" (ipsilateral cranial nerve deficits with contralateral long tract signs) are the hallmark of brainstem lesions. In this case, the left-sided facial numbness and left limb ataxia represent ipsilateral cranial nerve and cerebellar involvement, while the right-sided arm and leg weakness represents contralateral corticospinal tract involvement. The additional brainstem "D's" present (diplopia, dysphagia) further confirm brainstem localization. The crossed pattern occurs because cranial nerves exit ipsilaterally while the corticospinal tract has not yet decussated (or has decussated depending on the brainstem level).
- **C) Cervical spinal cord.** Incorrect. A spinal cord lesion would not explain cranial nerve deficits (facial numbness, diplopia, dysphagia) because the cranial nerves originate above the spinal cord in the brainstem. Spinal cord lesions produce deficits only below the level of the lesion with no head or cranial nerve involvement.
- **D) Left thalamus.** Incorrect. A thalamic lesion could cause contralateral sensory deficits and sometimes motor weakness, but it would not produce ipsilateral cranial nerve palsies, diplopia, dysphagia, or crossed signs. Thalamic lesions do not produce the characteristic crossed pattern seen in brainstem disease.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Complete spinal cord transection.** Incorrect. A complete transection would produce bilateral motor and sensory loss below the lesion level. This patient has a distinctly lateralized pattern with ipsilateral motor and proprioceptive deficits but contralateral pain and temperature loss, which is inconsistent with complete bilateral cord destruction.
- **B) Central cord syndrome.** Incorrect. Central cord syndrome (from syringomyelia or hyperextension injury) classically produces a "cape-like" bilateral loss of pain and temperature at the level of the lesion (from damage to crossing spinothalamic fibers in the ventral commissure) with upper extremity weakness greater than lower extremity weakness. This does not match the lateralized pattern described.
- **C) Brown-Sequard syndrome (cord hemisection).** Correct. This is a textbook Brown-Sequard syndrome from a left cord hemisection. The findings include: (1) ipsilateral (left) UMN signs below the lesion (weakness, hyperreflexia, Babinski) from lateral corticospinal tract damage; (2) ipsilateral (left) loss of proprioception and vibration from dorsal column damage; and (3) contralateral (right) loss of pain and temperature starting several segments below the lesion from spinothalamic tract damage (these fibers already crossed from the right side). The 2-3 segment gap below the lesion for pain/temperature loss occurs because entering fibers ascend in Lissauer's tract before crossing.
- **D) Anterior spinal artery syndrome.** Incorrect. Anterior spinal artery syndrome spares the dorsal columns (proprioception and vibration are preserved) while causing bilateral loss of motor function and bilateral loss of pain and temperature below the lesion. This patient has preserved pain/temperature on the ipsilateral side and loss of proprioception ipsilaterally, which is inconsistent with an anterior spinal artery infarction.

### Q11. Answer: B
- **A) Bilateral CN III nuclei in the midbrain.** Incorrect. Bilateral CN III nuclear lesions would cause fixed, constant ptosis and ophthalmoplegia that does not fluctuate with activity or rest. A bilateral brainstem lesion would also likely produce additional neurologic deficits such as altered consciousness, contralateral weakness, or other cranial nerve palsies. The fatiguable, fluctuating nature of this patient's weakness argues against a fixed structural lesion.
- **B) Neuromuscular junction.** Correct. This is a classic presentation of myasthenia gravis, a neuromuscular junction disorder. The hallmark features are all present: fatiguable weakness that worsens with sustained activity (ptosis after sustained upgaze) and improves with rest (better in the morning), involvement of ocular muscles (ptosis, diplopia) and bulbar muscles (chewing difficulty), preserved reflexes, and no sensory deficits. The neuromuscular junction transmits signals from motor neurons to muscle fibers, and antibody-mediated dysfunction at this site produces fatiguable weakness because repeated stimulation depletes available acetylcholine receptors.
- **C) Extraocular muscles (myopathy).** Incorrect. While ocular myopathies (such as mitochondrial myopathy) can cause ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, they produce constant weakness that does not fluctuate with activity or improve with rest. Myopathies also typically cause proximal limb weakness, not the isolated fatiguable pattern seen here.
- **D) Bilateral cerebral cortex.** Incorrect. Bilateral cortical lesions could cause weakness but would be expected to produce cortical signs (aphasia, neglect, seizures, cognitive changes) and UMN signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski). Cortical lesions do not cause fatiguable weakness that improves with rest, and bilateral cortical events large enough to cause ptosis would produce much more extensive neurologic deficits.

### Q12. Answer: B
- **A) Left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca area).** Incorrect. Broca aphasia is a non-fluent, expressive aphasia where the patient has difficulty producing speech but relatively preserved comprehension. This patient has fluent but nonsensical speech with impaired comprehension, which is the opposite pattern. Broca aphasia patients are typically aware of and frustrated by their language difficulty, whereas this patient is unaware of his deficit (anosognosia).
- **B) Left posterior superior temporal gyrus (Wernicke area) with involvement of left optic radiations.** Correct. This patient has Wernicke aphasia: fluent but nonsensical speech with paraphasic errors, impaired comprehension, impaired repetition, and lack of awareness of the deficit. Wernicke area is located in the left posterior superior temporal gyrus. The right visual field deficit (right homonymous hemianopsia or right superior quadrantanopsia) indicates involvement of the left optic radiations, which pass near the Wernicke area in the temporal-parietal region. Both deficits can be explained by a single left middle cerebral artery posterior division infarction, satisfying Occam's Razor.
- **C) Right parietal cortex.** Incorrect. A right parietal lesion can cause left-sided neglect (agnosia) and left visual field deficits, but it would not cause aphasia in a typical right-handed individual whose language is left-lateralized. The fluent paraphasic speech and impaired comprehension are language deficits that localize to the left hemisphere.
- **D) Bilateral frontal lobes.** Incorrect. Bilateral frontal lobe lesions can cause behavioral changes, abulia (lack of motivation), frontal release signs, and executive dysfunction, but they do not produce the specific pattern of fluent paraphasic speech with impaired comprehension characteristic of Wernicke aphasia. Frontal lobe damage affecting language would produce a non-fluent (Broca-type) pattern, not a fluent one.
