# Introduction to Epilepsy — Pre-Quiz

**1. Epilepsy has a bimodal age distribution. Which two age groups have the highest incidence?**

A) Adolescents and middle-aged adults
B) Children under 10 and adults over 65
C) Neonates and adolescents
D) Young adults (20-30) and elderly over 80

**2. Which statement best describes health disparities in epilepsy treatment globally?**

A) The treatment gap is largest in high-income countries
B) Low-income countries have half the prevalence of high-income countries
C) The treatment gap reaches 75% in low-income countries
D) Racial disparities in treatment have been eliminated in the United States

**3. What is the definition of a seizure?**

A) A chronic disease of recurrent unprovoked brain events
B) An electrical disturbance in the cortex from abnormal excessive or hypersynchronous neuronal activity
C) A structural lesion in the brain that causes periodic loss of consciousness
D) A psychological event caused by emotional stress manifesting as involuntary movements

**4. Which of the following meets the diagnostic criteria for epilepsy?**

A) A single provoked seizure due to alcohol withdrawal
B) Two unprovoked seizures occurring 12 hours apart
C) Two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart
D) A single febrile seizure in a 2-year-old child

**5. Which combination of features best characterizes the clinical presentation of a seizure?**

A) Gradual onset, progressive, and permanent
B) Stereotyped, transient, and episodic with clear onset and offset
C) Variable presentation, prolonged, and continuous
D) Random, chronic, and slowly evolving over weeks

**6. In the 3-step approach to neurologic diagnosis, seizures are localized to which anatomical structure?**

A) Brainstem
B) Cerebellum
C) Cerebral cortex
D) Spinal cord

**7. A patient has a seizure that begins in one hemisphere and the patient remains fully aware throughout the event. How is this seizure classified?**

A) Generalized tonic-clonic seizure
B) Focal seizure with preserved consciousness (focal aware)
C) Absence seizure
D) Myoclonic seizure

**8. What is the most common cause of focal impaired consciousness seizures in adults?**

A) Brain tumor
B) Cortical stroke
C) Mesial temporal sclerosis
D) Cortical dysplasia

**9. What is the first-line treatment for epilepsy?**

A) Vagus nerve stimulation
B) Open surgical resection
C) Anti-seizure medications
D) Deep brain stimulation

**10. What is the most common psychiatric co-morbidity in patients with epilepsy?**

A) Schizophrenia
B) Bipolar disorder
C) Depression
D) Obsessive-compulsive disorder

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Adolescents and middle-aged adults.** Incorrect. Neither adolescents nor middle-aged adults represent peak incidence groups. The bimodal distribution skips these age ranges.
- **B) Children under 10 and adults over 65.** Correct. Epilepsy follows a bimodal distribution with the highest incidence in young children (under 10) and older adults (over 65). This reflects different underlying etiologies at each age extreme.
- **C) Neonates and adolescents.** Incorrect. While neonates can have seizures, the first peak broadly includes children under 10, and adolescents do not represent the second peak.
- **D) Young adults (20-30) and elderly over 80.** Incorrect. Young adults aged 20-30 are not a peak incidence group. The bimodal peaks are in early childhood and after age 65.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) The treatment gap is largest in high-income countries.** Incorrect. The treatment gap is smallest in high-income countries (approximately 10%) and largest in low-income countries.
- **B) Low-income countries have half the prevalence of high-income countries.** Incorrect. The opposite is true: low-income countries have twice the prevalence and incidence of epilepsy compared to high-income countries.
- **C) The treatment gap reaches 75% in low-income countries.** Correct. Approximately 75% of people with epilepsy in low-income countries do not receive needed treatment, compared to 50% in middle-income and 10% in high-income countries.
- **D) Racial disparities in treatment have been eliminated in the United States.** Incorrect. Significant disparities persist in the US. Black patients are less likely to receive optimal medications and surgical treatment, and Native Americans have the longest time to diagnosis (27 months).

### Q3. Answer: B
- **A) A chronic disease of recurrent unprovoked brain events.** Incorrect. This description more closely matches epilepsy, which is the disease. A seizure is a single event, not a chronic disease.
- **B) An electrical disturbance in the cortex from abnormal excessive or hypersynchronous neuronal activity.** Correct. A seizure is defined as an electrical disturbance in the brain (cortex) from abnormal excessive or hypersynchronous neuronal activity, producing neurologic symptoms such as altered consciousness, behavior changes, or sensorimotor dysfunction.
- **C) A structural lesion in the brain that causes periodic loss of consciousness.** Incorrect. A seizure is an electrical event, not a structural lesion. While structural lesions can cause seizures, the seizure itself is the abnormal electrical activity.
- **D) A psychological event caused by emotional stress manifesting as involuntary movements.** Incorrect. This description more closely matches psychogenic non-epileptic events (PNEE), which are seizure mimickers, not true seizures.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) A single provoked seizure due to alcohol withdrawal.** Incorrect. Provoked seizures (triggered by a reversible cause such as alcohol withdrawal) do not count toward an epilepsy diagnosis. Epilepsy requires unprovoked seizures.
- **B) Two unprovoked seizures occurring 12 hours apart.** Incorrect. The diagnostic criteria specify that the two unprovoked seizures must occur more than 24 hours apart. Two seizures within 12 hours do not satisfy this criterion.
- **C) Two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart.** Correct. One of the three ways to diagnose epilepsy is at least two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, indicating a persistent tendency of the brain to generate seizures.
- **D) A single febrile seizure in a 2-year-old child.** Incorrect. A febrile seizure is a provoked seizure (triggered by fever) and does not meet the criteria for epilepsy, although febrile seizures are a risk factor for later epilepsy.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Gradual onset, progressive, and permanent.** Incorrect. This describes a progressive neurologic condition such as a neurodegenerative disease. Seizures have sudden onset and are self-limited.
- **B) Stereotyped, transient, and episodic with clear onset and offset.** Correct. Seizures are characterized by being stereotyped (similar each time in a given patient), transient (lasting seconds to minutes), and episodic with clear onset and offset. These features help distinguish seizures from other neurologic conditions.
- **C) Variable presentation, prolonged, and continuous.** Incorrect. Seizures in a given patient tend to be stereotyped rather than variable. They are transient, not prolonged or continuous (with the exception of status epilepticus).
- **D) Random, chronic, and slowly evolving over weeks.** Incorrect. While seizures may occur unpredictably, each individual seizure is brief (seconds to minutes) and does not slowly evolve over weeks.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Brainstem.** Incorrect. The brainstem is not the origin of seizures. Brainstem pathology produces different clinical syndromes such as cranial nerve palsies and long tract signs.
- **B) Cerebellum.** Incorrect. Cerebellar pathology causes ataxia, dysmetria, and coordination problems, not seizures.
- **C) Cerebral cortex.** Correct. Seizures arise from the cerebral cortex. In the 3-step approach, the episodic time course plus cortical symptoms localizes the problem to the cortex. Subcortical structures, brainstem, cerebellum, and spinal cord do not generate seizures.
- **D) Spinal cord.** Incorrect. Spinal cord pathology causes myelopathic symptoms such as weakness and sensory level, not seizures.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Generalized tonic-clonic seizure.** Incorrect. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures involve both hemispheres from onset and produce bilateral stiffening and rhythmic jerking with loss of consciousness.
- **B) Focal seizure with preserved consciousness (focal aware).** Correct. A seizure beginning in one hemisphere where the patient remains fully aware and can remember the entire episode is classified as a focal seizure with preserved consciousness (formerly called a simple partial seizure).
- **C) Absence seizure.** Incorrect. Absence seizures are generalized seizures (involving both hemispheres at onset) characterized by behavioral arrest and staring, not focal onset.
- **D) Myoclonic seizure.** Incorrect. Myoclonic seizures are generalized seizures characterized by brief, irregular jerks and are not focal in origin.

### Q8. Answer: C
- **A) Brain tumor.** Incorrect. While brain tumors can cause focal seizures, they are not the most common cause of focal impaired consciousness seizures in adults.
- **B) Cortical stroke.** Incorrect. Stroke is a common cause of seizures in older adults but is not the most common cause of focal impaired consciousness seizures overall in the adult population.
- **C) Mesial temporal sclerosis.** Correct. Mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS) is the most common cause of focal impaired consciousness seizures in adults. It involves neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampus and is associated with characteristic MRI findings of hippocampal atrophy and bright signal.
- **D) Cortical dysplasia.** Incorrect. Cortical dysplasia is a structural cause of epilepsy but is not the most common cause of focal impaired consciousness seizures in adults.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) Vagus nerve stimulation.** Incorrect. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a palliative device-based therapy reserved for patients with drug-refractory epilepsy who have failed at least two medications.
- **B) Open surgical resection.** Incorrect. Surgery is considered for drug-refractory epilepsy (failure of two medications at therapeutic doses), not as initial treatment.
- **C) Anti-seizure medications.** Correct. Anti-seizure medications (ASMs) are the first-line treatment for epilepsy. Approximately two-thirds of patients become seizure-free with medications alone.
- **D) Deep brain stimulation.** Incorrect. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a device therapy used for drug-refractory epilepsy, not as a first-line treatment.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Schizophrenia.** Incorrect. While psychosis can occur in epilepsy (particularly post-ictal psychosis), schizophrenia is not the most common psychiatric co-morbidity.
- **B) Bipolar disorder.** Incorrect. Bipolar disorder is not identified as the most common psychiatric co-morbidity in epilepsy patients.
- **C) Depression.** Correct. Depression is the most common psychiatric co-morbidity in epilepsy, affecting 1 in 10 people with the condition. Screening for and treating depression is an important aspect of comprehensive epilepsy care.
- **D) Obsessive-compulsive disorder.** Incorrect. OCD is not among the most commonly listed psychiatric co-morbidities associated with epilepsy. Depression, anxiety, and ADHD are more frequently cited.
