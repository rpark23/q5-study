# Traumatic Brain Injury — Pre-Quiz

### Q1. What is the most common cause of traumatic brain injury in the United States?

- A) Motor vehicle accidents
- B) Falls
- C) Violence/assault
- D) Sports injuries

### Q2. Which population has been shown to have a 2-4x higher likelihood of TBI history, representing an important social determinant of health?

- A) College athletes
- B) Patients experiencing homelessness
- C) Healthcare workers
- D) Commercial truck drivers

### Q3. An epidural hematoma most commonly results from laceration of which vessel?

- A) Bridging veins
- B) Anterior cerebral artery
- C) Middle meningeal artery
- D) Superior sagittal sinus

### Q4. What is the classic CT appearance of a subdural hematoma?

- A) Convex (lens-shaped) hyperdensity
- B) Concave (crescent-shaped) hyperdensity
- C) Blood layering in the sulci
- D) Multiple punctate hemorrhages at the grey-white junction

### Q5. A patient develops cytotoxic cerebral edema after severe TBI. What is the underlying mechanism?

- A) Blood-brain barrier disruption allowing fluid into extracellular space
- B) Energy failure causing Na/K pump dysfunction, leading to intracellular water accumulation
- C) Obstruction of CSF drainage at the arachnoid granulations
- D) Venous sinus thrombosis causing backflow of blood

### Q6. Uncal herniation classically causes ipsilateral pupil dilation due to compression of which cranial nerve?

- A) CN II (optic nerve)
- B) CN III (oculomotor nerve)

### Q7. Which of the following is a clinical sign of a basilar skull fracture?

- B) Battle's sign (mastoid ecchymosis)

### Q8. A patient opens eyes to pain, makes incomprehensible sounds, and exhibits abnormal flexion to pain. What is their Glasgow Coma Scale score?

- A) 5

- A) Loss of consciousness is required for diagnosis

- C) Full physical and cognitive rest is the standard of care

### Q10. Which finding on examination of an infant is most concerning for abusive head trauma?

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Motor vehicle accidents.** Incorrect. MVAs account for approximately 15% of TBIs and are the second most common cause. While MVAs are the leading cause of TBI-related death in the 15-24 age group, they are not the most common overall mechanism of TBI.
- **B) Falls.** Correct. Falls account for approximately 50% of all TBIs, making them the most common cause by a wide margin. Falls disproportionately affect the extremes of age: adults over 75 and children ages 0-4. This is why fall prevention counseling is a critical public health intervention.
- **C) Violence/assault.** Incorrect. While violence and assault are recognized mechanisms of TBI, they account for a smaller proportion than falls or MVAs. However, violence-related TBI intersects significantly with social determinants of health.
- **D) Sports injuries.** Incorrect. Sports injuries are a recognized cause of TBI, particularly concussions in contact sports, but they represent a smaller fraction of total TBI cases compared to falls.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) College athletes.** Incorrect. While athletes are at risk for concussions, the lecture specifically highlighted homelessness and incarceration as key social determinants intersecting with TBI risk, not collegiate athletics.
- **B) Patients experiencing homelessness.** Correct. Patients who have experienced homelessness are 2-4x more likely to have a history of TBI. This represents an important intersection of social determinants of health with TBI, compounded by limited access to care and follow-up, and co-occurring mental health conditions.
- **C) Healthcare workers.** Incorrect. Healthcare workers are not specifically identified as a population with elevated TBI prevalence in the context of social determinants of health.
- **D) Commercial truck drivers.** Incorrect. While anyone involved in motor vehicle use has some TBI risk, commercial truck drivers were not highlighted as a specific vulnerable population in the context of social determinants and TBI.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Bridging veins.** Incorrect. Tearing of bridging veins (veins traveling from the brain through the dura to venous sinuses) causes subdural hematomas, not epidural hematomas. Bridging vein tears are more common in older adults with cerebral atrophy.
- **B) Anterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. The anterior cerebral artery is not typically the source of an epidural hematoma. Hemorrhage from major cerebral arteries would more likely cause intracerebral or subarachnoid hemorrhage.
- **C) Middle meningeal artery.** Correct. Epidural hematomas most commonly result from laceration of the middle meningeal artery, often associated with temporal bone fracture. This arterial bleeding creates a collection between the skull and dura that appears as a convex (lens-shaped) hyperdensity on CT.
- **D) Superior sagittal sinus.** Incorrect. The superior sagittal sinus is a venous structure. While dural venous sinus injury can cause hemorrhage, it is not the classic vessel associated with epidural hematoma formation.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Convex (lens-shaped) hyperdensity.** Incorrect. A convex or lens-shaped appearance on CT is the classic imaging finding of an epidural hematoma, not a subdural hematoma. Epidural hematomas are bounded by dural attachments to the skull, creating the lens shape.
- **B) Concave (crescent-shaped) hyperdensity.** Correct. Subdural hematomas appear as concave (crescent-shaped) hyperdensities on CT in the acute phase. The blood collects between the dura and arachnoid, spreading along the brain surface. Over time, the density changes: acute blood is hyperdense, at ~2 weeks it becomes isodense, and chronic subdurals become hypodense (CSF-like).
- **C) Blood layering in the sulci.** Incorrect. Blood in the sulci is the characteristic appearance of subarachnoid hemorrhage (traumatic or from aneurysm rupture), not a subdural hematoma.
- **D) Multiple punctate hemorrhages at the grey-white junction.** Incorrect. This describes the MRI appearance of diffuse axonal injury (DAI), where microhemorrhages are seen along white matter tracts at the grey-white junction, corpus callosum, and midbrain.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Blood-brain barrier disruption allowing fluid into extracellular space.** Incorrect. This describes the mechanism of vasogenic edema, not cytotoxic edema. Vasogenic edema is potentially reversible and predominantly affects white matter. It is seen around contusions, hematomas, tumors, and abscesses.
- **B) Energy failure causing Na/K pump dysfunction, leading to intracellular water accumulation.** Correct. Cytotoxic edema results from energy failure (e.g., from ischemia or severe trauma), which causes the Na/K-ATPase pump to fail. Sodium accumulates intracellularly, and water follows by osmosis, causing cell swelling. This is intracellular edema, affects both grey and white matter, and is irreversible because it represents cellular death.
- **C) Obstruction of CSF drainage at the arachnoid granulations.** Incorrect. Obstruction at the arachnoid granulations (e.g., from blood clogging them after hemorrhage) causes communicating hydrocephalus, not cytotoxic edema. These are distinct pathological processes.
- **D) Venous sinus thrombosis causing backflow of blood.** Incorrect. Venous sinus thrombosis can cause elevated intracranial pressure and venous infarction, but it is not the mechanism of cytotoxic edema in the context of TBI.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) CN II (optic nerve).** Incorrect. The optic nerve transmits visual information and is not the nerve compressed in uncal herniation. Optic nerve damage would cause visual field deficits or blindness, not pupil dilation.
- **B) CN III (oculomotor nerve).** Correct. In uncal herniation, the uncus (tip of the temporal lobe) is pushed over the tentorium cerebelli and compresses CN III. The parasympathetic fibers running along the outside of CN III are particularly vulnerable. These fibers normally constrict the pupil, so their compression causes ipsilateral pupil dilation (a fixed, dilated pupil). This is a critical clinical sign of impending herniation.
- **C) CN IV (trochlear nerve).** Incorrect. CN IV innervates the superior oblique muscle and controls specific eye movements. While it can be affected in trauma, it is not the nerve classically compressed in uncal herniation.
- **D) CN VII (facial nerve).** Incorrect. CN VII controls facial expression muscles and taste. It exits the brainstem at the cerebellopontine angle and is not the nerve compressed in the classic uncal herniation syndrome.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Papilledema on fundoscopic exam.** Incorrect. Papilledema (optic disc swelling) is a sign of elevated intracranial pressure, not specifically of a basilar skull fracture. While elevated ICP can occur with TBI, papilledema is not among the clinical signs used to diagnose basilar skull fractures.
- **B) Battle's sign (mastoid ecchymosis).** Correct. Battle's sign is bruising behind the ear over the mastoid process and is one of the classic clinical signs of a basilar skull fracture. Other signs include hemotympanum (blood behind the eardrum), raccoon eyes (bilateral periorbital ecchymosis with tarsal plate sparing), and CSF otorrhea or rhinorrhea. Basilar skull fractures can be imaging-negative and are often diagnosed clinically based on these findings.
- **C) Lhermitte's sign.** Incorrect. Lhermitte's sign is an electric shock sensation traveling down the spine with neck flexion, associated with cervical spinal cord pathology (e.g., multiple sclerosis or cervical spondylosis). It is not a sign of basilar skull fracture.
- **D) Babinski sign.** Incorrect. The Babinski sign (upgoing toe with plantar stimulation) indicates upper motor neuron dysfunction. While it may be present in severe TBI with corticospinal tract damage, it is not a specific clinical sign used to diagnose basilar skull fractures.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) 5.** Incorrect. A GCS of 5 would require lower individual component scores. With eye opening to pain (2), incomprehensible sounds (2), and abnormal flexion (3), the total is 7, not 5.
- **B) 7.** Correct. The GCS is calculated by adding the three component scores: Eye opening to pain = 2, Verbal response of incomprehensible sounds = 2, Motor response of abnormal flexion (decorticate posturing) = 3. Total = 2 + 2 + 3 = 7. This places the patient in the severe TBI category (GCS 3-8), which means they are comatose and require ICU-level care.
- **C) 9.** Incorrect. A GCS of 9 would classify the patient as moderate TBI (GCS 9-12). The correct calculation yields 7, placing the patient in the severe category.
- **D) 11.** Incorrect. A GCS of 11 would require higher component scores (e.g., eye opening to speech, confused verbal response, and motor localization to pain). The described clinical findings yield a total of 7.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Loss of consciousness is required for diagnosis.** Incorrect. Loss of consciousness is NOT required for a diagnosis of mild TBI (concussion). Signs of alteration in brain function such as confusion, amnesia, altered mental status, or any neurologic deficit are sufficient. Many patients with concussion never lose consciousness.
- **B) Most symptoms resolve within 7-10 days.** Correct. The majority of concussion symptoms resolve within 7-10 days. A minority of patients develop post-concussive syndrome with prolonged symptoms (headache, dizziness, sleep impairment, difficulty concentrating), but most of these resolve within approximately 3 months. Risk factors for prolonged symptoms include repeated concussions, female sex, and pre-morbid stress, anxiety, or depression.
- **C) Full physical and cognitive rest is the standard of care.** Incorrect. There is no evidence supporting full physical and cognitive rest after concussion. Current management recommendations include a period of acute rest followed by a gradual return to activities. Active rehabilitation and treating associated symptoms (anxiety, depression, headaches) are preferred.
- **D) Post-concussive syndrome affects the majority of patients.** Incorrect. Post-concussive syndrome, with prolonged symptoms beyond the typical recovery period, affects a minority of concussion patients. The majority recover fully within 7-10 days.

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) A single small bruise on the shin.** Incorrect. A single bruise on the shin is a very common and typically benign finding in mobile children who bump into objects during normal play. This would not raise concern for abusive head trauma by itself.
- **B) Retinal hemorrhages on fundoscopic exam.** Correct. Retinal hemorrhages are a key finding that raises high suspicion for abusive head trauma. In the context of a suspected TBI in an infant, retinal hemorrhages (found on fundoscopic exam) combined with subdural hemorrhages on brain imaging and suspicious cutaneous bruising form the classic triad of abusive head trauma. Skeletal survey may also reveal rib and long bone fractures.
- **C) An anterior fontanelle that is still open.** Incorrect. An open anterior fontanelle is a normal finding in infants up to approximately 18 months of age. This is an expected developmental feature and has no association with abusive head trauma.
- **D) A mild upper respiratory infection.** Incorrect. Upper respiratory infections are extremely common in infants and young children. This finding is unrelated to abusive head trauma and would not raise suspicion for abuse.
