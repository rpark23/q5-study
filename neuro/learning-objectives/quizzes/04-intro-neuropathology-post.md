# Introduction to Neuropathology — Post-Quiz

D) The mass is supratentorial and lumbar puncture only carries risk in infratentorial lesions

Which of the following processes and cell types does this finding represent?**

A) Reactive gliosis mediated by GFAP-positive astrocytes forming a glial scar

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) The mass lesion has already caused permanent brain damage and CSF analysis would be non-diagnostic.** Incorrect. While permanent damage may have occurred, this is not the reason to avoid lumbar puncture. The concern is not about diagnostic yield but about the immediate life-threatening risk of the procedure itself.
- **B) Removing CSF from below could create a pressure gradient favoring tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum, compressing the medulla.** Correct. In the setting of a mass lesion with raised intracranial pressure, removing CSF from the lumbar cistern can create a downward pressure gradient. This promotes tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum, which compresses the medullary respiratory centers and is the only lethal form of herniation.
- **C) Lumbar puncture would increase intracranial blood volume and worsen cerebral edema.** Incorrect. Lumbar puncture removes CSF; it does not increase intracranial blood volume. The danger is the creation of a pressure differential, not volume addition.
- **D) The mass is supratentorial and lumbar puncture only carries risk in infratentorial lesions.** Incorrect. Any mass lesion that raises intracranial pressure can predispose to tonsillar herniation after lumbar puncture, whether the lesion is supratentorial or infratentorial. Supratentorial masses can cause downward transtentorial and subsequent tonsillar herniation.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) The hippocampus receives its blood supply from end arteries with no collateral circulation.** Incorrect. While vascular anatomy contributes to regional vulnerability, the hippocampal CA1 sector's vulnerability is attributed to its intrinsic neuronal properties and metabolic characteristics, not solely to end-artery supply. The selective vulnerability hierarchy is a property of the neurons themselves.
- **B) The hippocampal CA1 sector sits at the top of the hierarchical vulnerability to hypoxia-ischemia, making it the first region to sustain irreversible injury.** Correct. In adults, the hippocampal CA1 (Sommer's sector) is the most vulnerable brain region to global hypoxia-ischemia. After 12 minutes of cardiac arrest, this region would be expected to suffer the most severe and selective damage, explaining the anterograde amnesia with relatively preserved motor and sensory function.
- **C) Hippocampal neurons are uniquely dependent on anaerobic metabolism and fail rapidly without glucose.** Incorrect. Hippocampal neurons, like all neurons, depend primarily on aerobic metabolism. Their vulnerability relates to their position in the hypoxia-ischemia hierarchy, not a unique dependence on anaerobic pathways.
- **D) The hippocampus is most susceptible because it has the lowest density of astrocytic support cells.** Incorrect. The selective vulnerability of hippocampal CA1 neurons is not attributed to a lack of astrocytic support. Selective vulnerability is a multifactorial property involving excitotoxicity, receptor density, and metabolic factors.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).** Incorrect. H&E is the standard histological stain that shows general tissue architecture. While it can reveal gross changes in white matter, it does not specifically stain myelin blue and is not the optimal stain for detecting subtle demyelination.
- **B) GFAP immunohistochemistry.** Incorrect. GFAP marks astrocytes and reactive gliosis. While gliosis often accompanies demyelination, GFAP staining would not show the loss-of-blue-staining pattern described; it would show increased brown immunoreactivity in reactive areas.
- **C) Luxol Fast Blue (LFB).** Correct. LFB specifically stains myelin blue. In demyelinating disease, affected white matter regions lose their blue staining, while areas with preserved myelin (including cortical myelinated fibers) retain the blue color. Loss of blue staining in the centrum semiovale with preserved cortical coloring is a classic LFB finding in white matter disease.
- **D) Silver stain (Bielschowsky).** Incorrect. Silver stains highlight neurofilaments and axons, which are argyrophilic (silver-loving). They are used to visualize neuronal processes and identify structures like neurofibrillary tangles, not to assess myelin integrity.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) GFAP, showing reactive astrogliosis along white matter tracts.** Incorrect. While GFAP-positive astrogliosis would be expected in areas of injury, the clinical scenario -- immediate unconsciousness after high-speed trauma without mass lesion -- is classic for diffuse axonal injury. GFAP would show a reactive pattern but would not produce the specific punctate labeling pattern described.
- **B) CD68, showing microglial activation in areas of Wallerian degeneration.** Incorrect. CD68-positive microglia/macrophages would be present in areas of secondary degeneration, but the widespread punctate pattern in white matter tracts immediately after trauma is most characteristic of Beta-APP staining at axonal shearing sites.
- **C) Beta-APP, showing disrupted axoplasmic flow at sites of axonal shearing.** Correct. Beta-APP is the most sensitive immunohistochemical marker for diffuse axonal injury (DAI). After axonal shearing, continued axoplasmic transport causes Beta-APP to accumulate at the damaged ends, producing punctate immunoreactivity throughout white matter tracts. DAI classically presents with immediate unconsciousness after high-speed trauma without mass lesion or fracture.
- **D) Synaptophysin, showing loss of presynaptic terminals in gray matter.** Incorrect. Synaptophysin marks presynaptic vesicle proteins and would be most relevant in gray matter synapse evaluation, not in detecting acute axonal shearing injuries in white matter tracts.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Chromatolysis indicating attempted axonal regeneration.** Incorrect. Chromatolysis features cell body enlargement and rounding, peripheral nuclear displacement, and dispersed Nissl substance. It reflects increased protein synthesis for axon repair. The neurons described here are shrunken, not enlarged.
- **B) Red dead neurons representing the earliest morphologic marker of irreversible neuronal injury.** Correct. The description -- shrunken neurons with pyknotic nuclei, absent nucleoli, and intense eosinophilia appearing within hours of ischemic injury in the hippocampal CA1 region -- is the classic presentation of red dead neurons (hypoxic-ischemic neuronal change). They appear 6-12 hours after irreversible injury and are the earliest morphologic sign of neuronal death.
- **C) Ferrugination showing mineral deposition in dead neurons.** Incorrect. Ferrugination (mineralization) produces purple-staining calcified neuronal remnants. It is a later process seen especially in neonatal brains and does not produce the eosinophilic (red) appearance described.
- **D) Neuronophagia with microglial engulfment of injured neurons.** Incorrect. Neuronophagia involves microglia clustering around and phagocytosing dead neurons. The description shows individual shrunken red neurons without mention of surrounding microglial cells, which is consistent with acute red dead neuron change rather than neuronophagia.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) Reactive gliosis with enlarged GFAP-positive astrocytes forming a dense glial scar.** Incorrect. While reactive gliosis is a nonspecific response to CNS injury, the specific glial change associated with hepatic encephalopathy and hyperammonemia is Alzheimer type II astrocytosis, not the formation of a dense gliotic scar.
- **B) Alzheimer type II astrocytes with swollen, pale nuclei in deep gray matter.** Correct. Hepatic encephalopathy with hyperammonemia produces a characteristic astrocytic change known as Alzheimer type II astrocytosis. These astrocytes have swollen, watery-appearing nuclei and are found predominantly in deep gray matter structures. This is a specific and well-recognized association.
- **C) Prominent neuronophagia with microglial nodule formation.** Incorrect. Neuronophagia and microglial nodules are characteristic of viral encephalitis, not hepatic encephalopathy. The pathology in hepatic encephalopathy is primarily astrocytic, not microglial.
- **D) Demyelination with loss of oligodendrocytes in periventricular white matter.** Incorrect. Demyelination is not a feature of hepatic encephalopathy. This pattern would be more consistent with multiple sclerosis or other primary demyelinating diseases.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) Ethanol, which selectively damages the cerebellar vermis.** Incorrect. Chronic ethanol toxicity causes degeneration of the superior cerebellar vermis, not bilateral putaminal necrosis. The clinical presentation (acute onset, blindness, basal ganglia necrosis) does not match chronic alcoholic cerebellar degeneration.
- **B) Carbon monoxide, which causes white matter microthromboses.** Incorrect. Carbon monoxide preferentially damages white matter through microvascular thrombosis, producing a "fleabitten" appearance. It does not characteristically cause bilateral putaminal necrosis and vision loss.
- **C) Methanol, whose toxic metabolite formate selectively damages the basal ganglia and retina.** Correct. Methanol is metabolized to formate, which is toxic to the basal ganglia (particularly the putamen) and retinal structures. The clinical triad of acute onset headache, abdominal pain, and bilateral vision loss with symmetric putaminal necrosis on imaging is classic for methanol poisoning. Homemade liquor distilled through contaminated equipment is a classic source.
- **D) Lead, which causes peripheral neuropathy and wrist drop through motor axon degeneration.** Incorrect. Lead toxicity primarily affects the peripheral nervous system (causing wrist drop from motor neuropathy) and can cause encephalopathy in children. It does not produce the selective bilateral putaminal necrosis and acute vision loss described.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) White matter has a higher density of neurons, making it more metabolically vulnerable to radiation.** Incorrect. White matter is composed primarily of myelinated axons and glial cells, not neurons. Neuronal cell bodies reside in gray matter. The vulnerability of white matter to radiation is related to oligodendrocyte biology, not neuronal density.
- **B) Myelin has a turnover time of approximately 88 days, making oligodendrocytes especially susceptible to radiation-induced injury during active synthesis.** Correct. Myelin is actively maintained and turned over with a cycle of approximately 88 days. Radiation preferentially injures dividing cells and cells engaged in active synthesis. Oligodendrocytes, which must continually produce myelin, are therefore particularly susceptible to radiation damage, leading to progressive demyelination and white matter necrosis.
- **C) Radiation selectively destroys astrocytes, which are concentrated in white matter.** Incorrect. While astrocytes are present in white matter, they are found throughout the CNS and are not the primary target of radiation injury. The white matter selectivity relates to oligodendrocyte vulnerability during active myelin synthesis.
- **D) White matter is more highly vascularized than gray matter, increasing radiation exposure.** Incorrect. Gray matter is actually more highly vascularized than white matter due to the metabolic demands of neuronal cell bodies. The preferential white matter damage is not due to increased vascular density.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Ethanol directly demyelinates cerebellar white matter tracts, producing ataxia from white matter loss.** Incorrect. Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration is primarily a gray matter disease affecting neuronal elements (Purkinje cells and granule cells), not a demyelinating white matter disease. The lecture classifies ethanol toxicity as a gray matter vulnerability example.
- **B) Chronic ethanol exposure selectively damages Purkinje cells and granule cells of the superior vermis, which control truncal and lower extremity coordination.** Correct. Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration selectively affects the superior cerebellar vermis, causing cell loss across all cerebellar folia layers. The vermis controls truncal stability and lower extremity coordination, which explains the clinical pattern of truncal instability, wide-based gait, and leg ataxia with relatively preserved upper extremity function.
- **C) Thiamine deficiency in alcoholism causes mammillary body necrosis, which secondarily produces cerebellar ataxia through circuit disconnection.** Incorrect. While thiamine deficiency (Wernicke encephalopathy) does occur in alcoholics and involves mammillary bodies, the clinical scenario describes isolated cerebellar vermis atrophy on MRI, which is the direct toxic effect of ethanol on cerebellar neurons, a distinct entity from Wernicke encephalopathy.
- **D) Ethanol toxicity primarily affects the deep cerebellar nuclei, which project to spinal motor neurons controlling gait.** Incorrect. Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration primarily affects the cerebellar cortex (Purkinje and granule cells) of the superior vermis, not the deep cerebellar nuclei. The atrophy is of the vermis itself as seen on MRI.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Tay-Sachs disease with neuronal storage of GM2 ganglioside.** Incorrect. Tay-Sachs disease is a gray matter (neuronal) storage disorder caused by hexosaminidase A deficiency. It causes neuronal distension with GM2 ganglioside accumulation, not white matter disease with multinucleated macrophages. The enzyme deficiency in this case (galactosylceramidase) is specific to Krabbe disease.
- **B) Metachromatic leukodystrophy with accumulation of sulfatides.** Incorrect. Metachromatic leukodystrophy is caused by arylsulfatase A deficiency leading to sulfatide accumulation. While it also causes white matter disease, the enzyme deficiency identified (galactosylceramidase) and the presence of globoid cells (multinucleated macrophages) are specific to Krabbe disease.
- **C) Krabbe disease (globoid cell leukodystrophy) with accumulation of toxic galactosylsphingosine.** Correct. Krabbe disease is caused by deficiency of galactosylceramidase, leading to accumulation of toxic galactosylsphingosine (psychosine). It presents in infants at 3-6 months with irritability, stiffness, and visual deterioration. The white matter shows characteristic globoid cells -- large multinucleated macrophages engorged with undigested material. The cortex is relatively spared as this is a leukodystrophy.
- **D) Multiple sclerosis with autoimmune destruction of oligodendrocytes.** Incorrect. Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease that typically presents in young adults, not infants. It would not be associated with galactosylceramidase deficiency or globoid cells. MS lesions show perivenous inflammation and demyelination with relative axonal preservation.

### Q11. Answer: B
- **A) Carbon monoxide binds to myelin basic protein, causing direct chemical demyelination.** Incorrect. Carbon monoxide's primary mechanism of toxicity is binding to hemoglobin (forming carboxyhemoglobin) and cytochrome oxidase, not direct binding to myelin basic protein. The white matter damage is vascular in mechanism, not through direct chemical demyelination.
- **B) Carbon monoxide causes microthromboses in the CNS microvasculature, and white matter is more vulnerable because gray matter is protected by anastomotic microvessels.** Correct. Carbon monoxide poisoning produces microthromboses throughout the CNS microvasculature. White matter is preferentially affected because gray matter has more robust anastomotic microvessel networks that provide collateral circulation. The "fleabitten" appearance on MRI reflects scattered microinfarcts throughout white matter. Delayed neurological deterioration weeks after the initial exposure is a well-recognized pattern.
- **C) Carbon monoxide selectively inhibits oligodendrocyte mitochondria, causing apoptosis of myelin-producing cells.** Incorrect. While carbon monoxide does inhibit cytochrome oxidase in mitochondria, the white matter selectivity in CO poisoning is explained by vascular anatomy (microthromboses in white matter lacking collaterals), not selective mitochondrial toxicity to oligodendrocytes specifically.
- **D) Carbon monoxide disrupts the blood-brain barrier exclusively in white matter regions, causing vasogenic edema.** Incorrect. Carbon monoxide does not selectively disrupt the blood-brain barrier in white matter. The mechanism is microvascular thrombosis with differential vulnerability based on the density of anastomotic microvessels between gray and white matter.

### Q12. Answer: B
- **A) Reactive gliosis mediated by GFAP-positive astrocytes forming a glial scar.** Incorrect. Reactive gliosis involves astrocyte proliferation and hypertrophy with GFAP upregulation. The cells described here are CD68-positive (a macrophage/microglial marker), small, and are actively engulfing neurons, which is not characteristic of astrocytes.
- **B) Neuronophagia carried out by activated microglia, with residual microglial nodules.** Correct. The description is classic for neuronophagia: activated microglia (CD68-positive, confirming their macrophage/microglial lineage) clustering around and engulfing degenerating neurons. Residual microglial nodules remaining after neurons have been cleared are a hallmark finding of viral encephalitis. Microglia are derived from blood monocytes during embryonic development and serve as the CNS tissue macrophages.
- **C) Demyelination caused by oligodendrocyte loss from direct viral cytolysis.** Incorrect. The pathology described is in the temporal cortex (gray matter) and involves neurons being engulfed by CD68-positive cells. Demyelination is a white matter process involving oligodendrocyte loss and would not present as cells surrounding and engulfing neurons.
- **D) Chromatolysis of motor neurons undergoing axonal regeneration after viral injury.** Incorrect. Chromatolysis is a regenerative change within individual neurons (cell body enlargement, nuclear displacement, Nissl dispersal). It does not involve clusters of cells surrounding neurons, and the CD68-positive staining identifies these cells as microglia/macrophages, not as neurons undergoing chromatolysis.
