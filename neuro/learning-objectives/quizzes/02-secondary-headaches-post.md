### Q1. A 45-year-old man with no significant past medical history presents with a 3-week history of progressively worsening headaches. He reports that the headaches are worse in the morning upon waking and when he strains during bowel movements. He denies any prior history of headaches. Using the SNOOP mnemonic, which combination of red flag features does this patient demonstrate?

- A) Systemic symptoms and Neurologic signs
- B) Onset: sudden thunderclap headache and Other associated conditions
- C) Other associated conditions (worse with Valsalva, awakens from sleep) and Prior headache history is different (new headache >40 years old)
- D) Neurologic symptoms and Prior headache history with medication overuse

### Q2. A 28-year-old woman presents with a 2-month history of headaches, transient episodes of bilateral vision blurring lasting seconds, and a whooshing sound in her ears. Her BMI is 38. On examination, you note bilateral optic disc swelling and she is unable to fully abduct either eye. Which cranial nerves are affected in this presentation?

- A) CN III and CN IV bilaterally
- B) CN II and CN VI bilaterally
- C) CN V and CN VII bilaterally
- D) CN IV and CN VIII bilaterally

### Q5. A 50-year-old man presents to the emergency department with the worst headache of his life that began 6 hours ago while lifting weights. Non-contrast CT head is read as normal. - C) Renal function panel and urinalysis

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C
- **A) Systemic symptoms and Neurologic signs.** Incorrect. The vignette does not describe systemic symptoms such as fever, rash, cancer, or immunocompromised state. It also does not describe focal neurologic signs, altered mental status, or papilledema on examination.
- **B) Onset: sudden thunderclap headache and Other associated conditions.** Incorrect. The headache is progressively worsening over 3 weeks, not a sudden thunderclap headache. The "O" for onset specifically refers to an abrupt, maximal-intensity headache, not a gradual worsening pattern.
- **C) Other associated conditions (worse with Valsalva, awakens from sleep) and Prior headache history is different (new headache >40 years old).** Correct. The headache worsening with Valsalva (straining) and awakening from sleep are features of increased ICP, falling under "Other associated conditions." A new headache in a patient over 40 with no prior headache history represents a change in prior headache pattern. Both are SNOOP red flags requiring further workup.
- **D) Neurologic symptoms and Prior headache history with medication overuse.** Incorrect. No focal neurologic symptoms are described in the vignette, and there is no mention of analgesic overuse. Medication overuse headache involves frequent use of acute headache medications.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) CN III and CN IV bilaterally.** Incorrect. CN III palsy would cause ptosis, eye deviation down-and-out, and mydriasis. CN IV palsy would cause difficulty looking down and inward. Neither matches this presentation of disc swelling and failure to abduct the eyes.
- **B) CN II and CN VI bilaterally.** Correct. This patient presents with classic idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH): young obese woman with headaches, transient visual obscurations, pulsatile tinnitus, papilledema (CN II involvement), and bilateral abduction deficits (CN VI palsies). CN VI has the longest intracranial course and is most susceptible to increased ICP. Papilledema represents optic disc swelling from transmitted increased ICP to the optic nerve sheath.
- **C) CN V and CN VII bilaterally.** Incorrect. CN V involvement would produce facial sensory loss or pain. CN VII involvement would produce facial weakness. Neither optic disc swelling nor abduction failure involves these nerves.
- **D) CN IV and CN VIII bilaterally.** Incorrect. CN IV palsy would cause vertical diplopia with head tilt, not horizontal abduction failure. CN VIII involvement would cause hearing loss or vertigo, not the pulsatile tinnitus or disc swelling described here.

### Q3. Answer: B
- **A) Perform LP immediately, as the clinical suspicion for infection is high.** Incorrect. Despite the urgency of treating CNS infection, this immunocompromised patient with altered mental status meets multiple criteria requiring neuroimaging before LP. Performing LP without imaging risks fatal brain herniation if a mass lesion or significant ICP elevation is present.
- **B) Start empiric antibiotics and obtain neuroimaging before LP.** Correct. This patient has two indications for imaging before LP: immunocompromised state (HIV with CD4 < 200) and altered mental status. Empiric antibiotics should not be delayed while waiting for imaging. Blood cultures and empiric treatment should be initiated immediately, and neuroimaging performed before LP to rule out mass lesion or abscess.
- **C) Obtain blood cultures and wait for results before deciding on LP.** Incorrect. Waiting for blood culture results (24-48 hours) would unacceptably delay the diagnosis and treatment of a potentially lethal CNS infection. The correct approach is to start empiric treatment, image, then perform LP.
- **D) Perform funduscopic exam and if no papilledema, proceed directly to LP without imaging.** Incorrect. While checking for papilledema is valuable, the absence of papilledema does not rule out elevated ICP or mass lesion. Papilledema is a late finding. In immunocompromised patients with altered mental status, neuroimaging is mandatory regardless of funduscopic findings.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Ruptured arteriovenous malformation in the frontal lobe.** Incorrect. While AVMs can cause SAH, the diffuse hyperdensity in the basal cisterns and Sylvian fissures is the classic pattern of aneurysmal SAH. AVM rupture more commonly causes intraparenchymal hemorrhage, though it can secondarily extend into the subarachnoid space.
- **B) Ruptured berry aneurysm at a Circle of Willis junction.** Correct. This is a classic presentation of SAH from a ruptured berry aneurysm: sudden-onset worst headache of life, rapid neurologic decline, meningismus, and CT showing hyperdense blood in the basal cisterns (where the Circle of Willis sits). Berry aneurysms at Circle of Willis junctions (anterior communicating artery, posterior communicating artery, MCA bifurcation, basilar tip) are the most common cause of nontraumatic SAH.
- **C) Hypertensive hemorrhage in the basal ganglia with intraventricular extension.** Incorrect. Hypertensive hemorrhage typically appears as intraparenchymal hemorrhage in the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, or cerebellum. While it can extend into the ventricles, the pattern described here (blood in subarachnoid cisterns and Sylvian fissures) is characteristic of aneurysmal SAH, not hypertensive hemorrhage.
- **D) Hemorrhagic conversion of an ischemic stroke in the MCA territory.** Incorrect. Hemorrhagic conversion of an ischemic stroke produces blood within brain parenchyma in a vascular territory distribution, not diffuse blood in the subarachnoid cisterns. This patient's CT pattern is classic for SAH.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) Discharge with outpatient neurology follow-up within one week.** Incorrect. Discharging this patient is dangerous. A normal CT does not exclude SAH because CT sensitivity is only 92% within 24 hours and declines further with time. A thunderclap headache must be assumed to be SAH until proven otherwise, and additional workup is mandatory.
- **B) Obtain MRI brain with gadolinium contrast to evaluate for structural lesion.** Incorrect. While MRI has some utility, the immediate concern in a thunderclap headache is SAH. The established diagnostic algorithm after a negative CT is lumbar puncture, not MRI. MRI with gadolinium is better suited for evaluating subacute or chronic headache etiologies.
- **C) Perform lumbar puncture and evaluate for xanthochromia.** Correct. The standard algorithm for thunderclap headache is CT head followed by LP if the CT is negative. CT misses approximately 8% of SAH within 24 hours, and sensitivity drops further over time. LP should be performed to look for xanthochromia (yellow CSF from hemoglobin breakdown), which confirms SAH. At 6 hours post-onset, xanthochromia should be detectable (it takes approximately 2 hours to develop).
- **D) Obtain CT angiogram of the head and neck and discharge if normal.** Incorrect. While CTA can identify aneurysms, a normal CTA does not exclude SAH from other causes, and SAH itself must first be confirmed or excluded. LP is the appropriate next step after negative CT to evaluate for blood products in the CSF.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) All intracranial aneurysms require immediate surgical clipping regardless of size.** Incorrect. Management of unruptured aneurysms is controversial and depends on size, location, patient factors, and family history. Not all aneurysms require intervention, and observation with serial imaging is appropriate for many small, unruptured aneurysms.
- **B) Endovascular coiling is contraindicated for anterior circulation aneurysms.** Incorrect. Endovascular coiling can be performed on aneurysms in both the anterior and posterior circulation. The choice between coiling and clipping depends on aneurysm location, neck width, and patient factors, not on which circulation is involved.
- **C) Unruptured aneurysms less than 8 mm in the anterior circulation may be observed with serial imaging.** Correct. Management of unruptured aneurysms remains controversial. The annual bleeding risk is 0.5-2%. For aneurysms less than 8 mm in the anterior circulation without family history of SAH, observation with serial imaging is a reasonable approach. Larger aneurysms, posterior circulation location, and family history may shift the decision toward treatment.
- **D) The annual bleeding risk of unruptured aneurysms exceeds 10%, mandating urgent intervention.** Incorrect. The annual bleeding risk of unruptured aneurysms is 0.5-2%, not greater than 10%. While this cumulative risk over years is significant, it does not mandate urgent intervention for all aneurysms, particularly small ones in the anterior circulation.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) CT head without contrast to rule out acute hemorrhage.** Incorrect. This presentation is classic for trigeminal neuralgia (brief lancinating pain in V2/V3 distribution triggered by innocuous stimuli), not an acute hemorrhagic event. CT is the first-line study for acute headache presentations, but MRI is the appropriate imaging modality for evaluating trigeminal neuralgia.
- **B) MRI brain to evaluate for vascular loop compression or structural lesion at the trigeminal nerve root entry zone.** Correct. MRI is the diagnostic study of choice for trigeminal neuralgia. It can identify a vascular loop compressing the trigeminal nerve at the root entry zone (the most common secondary cause), structural lesions such as tumors (e.g., meningioma, schwannoma), or demyelinating plaques from multiple sclerosis. Identifying the cause guides treatment selection.
- **C) Electromyography and nerve conduction studies of the facial nerve.** Incorrect. EMG/NCS of the facial nerve evaluates CN VII motor function, not CN V sensory pain. Trigeminal neuralgia is diagnosed clinically and with MRI, not electrodiagnostic studies.
- **D) Catheter angiography to assess the vertebrobasilar circulation.** Incorrect. Catheter angiography is invasive and reserved for specific vascular evaluations, such as characterizing aneurysms or planning endovascular intervention. MRI with dedicated sequences can noninvasively identify vascular compression of CN V without the risks of catheter angiography.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Stereotactic radiosurgery to ablate the trigeminal ganglion.** Incorrect. While stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife) is an option for some patients with trigeminal neuralgia, the first-line surgical treatment when a vascular loop has been identified on MRI is microvascular decompression, which addresses the underlying cause directly.
- **B) Microvascular decompression to separate the vascular loop from the CN V root entry zone.** Correct. When a vascular loop compressing CN V is identified on MRI and medical therapy has failed, microvascular decompression (MVD) is the most appropriate surgical treatment. MVD directly addresses the pathology by placing pledgets (Teflon pads) between the offending vessel and the trigeminal nerve root entry zone to relieve compression.
- **C) Percutaneous rhizotomy of the facial nerve.** Incorrect. The facial nerve (CN VII) is not involved in trigeminal neuralgia. Percutaneous procedures targeting the trigeminal nerve do exist (e.g., balloon compression, radiofrequency rhizotomy of CN V), but rhizotomy of CN VII would be targeting the wrong nerve entirely.
- **D) Endovascular coiling of the compressing vascular loop.** Incorrect. The compressing vessel is typically a normal artery (often the superior cerebellar artery). Coiling a normal, functioning artery would occlude it and cause a stroke. MVD separates the vessel from the nerve without disrupting blood flow.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) CN V, CN VI, and CN VII.** Incorrect. While CN V can be affected by large cerebellopontine angle (CPA) tumors, the primary nerves compressed by vestibular schwannomas are CN VII and CN VIII. CN VI is located more medially and is not typically affected by CPA masses.
- **B) CN III, CN IV, and CN VI.** Incorrect. These are the extraocular motor nerves located in the cavernous sinus and midbrain region, not at the cerebellopontine angle. They would not be affected by CPA tumors.
- **C) CN VII and CN VIII.** Correct. Vestibular schwannomas (acoustic neuromas) arise from the vestibular portion of CN VIII and compress CN VIII (causing hearing loss and vestibular dysfunction) and adjacent CN VII (causing facial weakness) at the cerebellopontine angle. Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are the hallmark of neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF-2), which follows autosomal dominant inheritance, consistent with the positive family history.
- **D) CN IX, CN X, and CN XI.** Incorrect. These lower cranial nerves exit through the jugular foramen, which is inferior to the cerebellopontine angle. They are more commonly affected by jugular foramen tumors (e.g., glomus jugulare), not vestibular schwannomas.

### Q10. Answer: D
- **A) Subarachnoid hemorrhage.** Incorrect. SAH would show xanthochromia or elevated RBC count on LP, not lymphocytic pleocytosis with low glucose. The CSF pattern here (lymphocytic predominance, elevated protein, low glucose) is inconsistent with hemorrhage.
- **B) Idiopathic intracranial hypertension.** Incorrect. IIH presents with elevated opening pressure (>250 mmH2O) but the CSF composition is normal (normal cell count, protein, and glucose). This patient has a normal opening pressure with abnormal CSF constituents, ruling out IIH.
- **C) Bacterial meningitis.** Incorrect. Bacterial meningitis typically shows neutrophilic predominance (not lymphocytic), very high protein, and very low glucose. While bacterial meningitis can have lymphocytic predominance early, the 90% lymphocytic pleocytosis with subacute (5-day) course is more consistent with a granulomatous or chronic infection.
- **D) Tuberculous or fungal meningitis.** Correct. The CSF profile shows the classic triad of TB/fungal meningitis: lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and low glucose (hypoglycorrhachia). The subacute presentation over days with low-grade fever and neck stiffness further supports this diagnosis. TB and fungal meningitis produce a granulomatous inflammatory response that impairs glucose transport across the blood-CSF barrier.

### Q11. Answer: C
- **A) Increase the dose of acetazolamide and reassess in 3 months.** Incorrect. This patient has progressive visual field loss despite 6 months of medical therapy. Continued vision loss indicates treatment failure, and waiting an additional 3 months risks permanent, irreversible optic nerve damage. Escalation to surgical management is warranted.
- **B) Switch to carbamazepine for headache management.** Incorrect. Carbamazepine is the first-line treatment for trigeminal neuralgia, not IIH. It has no role in reducing intracranial pressure or protecting the optic nerve from damage due to elevated ICP.
- **C) Refer for optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting.** Correct. When medical therapy (acetazolamide, topiramate, weight loss) fails to halt progressive visual loss in IIH, surgical intervention is indicated. Optic nerve sheath fenestration creates windows in the optic nerve sheath to relieve pressure on the optic nerve. CSF shunting (ventriculoperitoneal or lumboperitoneal) reduces ICP systemically. The choice depends on whether the primary concern is vision loss (fenestration) or intractable headache with vision loss (shunting).
- **D) Perform repeat lumbar puncture for therapeutic drainage every 2 weeks.** Incorrect. While therapeutic LP can provide temporary symptom relief, serial LPs are not a sustainable long-term management strategy. The CSF reaccumulates rapidly, the procedure carries risks with repetition (infection, post-LP headache), and it does not address the underlying pathophysiology. Definitive surgical intervention is more appropriate.

### Q12. Answer: B
- **A) Liver function tests and serum sodium level.** Incorrect. While monitoring liver function and sodium is important during carbamazepine therapy (it can cause hepatotoxicity and hyponatremia), these are routine monitoring tests, not the critical pharmacogenomic screening that must be done before initiating therapy in this patient population.
- **B) HLA-B1502 allele testing.** Correct. The HLA-B1502 allele, found most commonly in patients of Southeast Asian descent, is strongly associated with carbamazepine-induced Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), which are potentially fatal mucocutaneous reactions. Testing for this allele is mandatory before starting carbamazepine in at-risk populations. If positive, an alternative agent must be used.
- **C) Renal function panel and urinalysis.** Incorrect. Renal function monitoring is not the primary safety concern with carbamazepine initiation. Carbamazepine is hepatically metabolized, and renal toxicity is not a major adverse effect. The critical pre-treatment test in this patient is pharmacogenomic screening.
- **D) Thyroid-stimulating hormone and free T4 levels.** Incorrect. While carbamazepine can affect thyroid hormone levels through hepatic enzyme induction, thyroid screening is not the urgent pre-treatment test needed here. The life-threatening risk of SJS in patients with HLA-B1502 takes priority.
