## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Obtain imaging, localize the lesion, start treatment.** Incorrect. This skips the essential first step of clinical history and physical examination. Diagnostic testing should follow localization, not precede it.
- **B) Formulate key symptoms/signs from H&P, localize the lesion anatomically, formulate a diagnosis and differential diagnosis.** Correct. This is the 3-step approach taught in the lecture: (1) H&P to formulate key symptoms and signs, (2) anatomical localization, and (3) diagnosis and differential diagnosis. This systematic method allows neurologists to establish a diagnosis approximately 90% of the time.
- **C) Perform a lumbar puncture, analyze CSF, treat empirically.** Incorrect. Diagnostic procedures such as lumbar puncture come after localization and differential diagnosis, not as the initial approach. Treatment should be guided by a working diagnosis.
- **D) Screen for cognitive deficits, order an MRI, refer to neurosurgery.** Incorrect. This is a narrow approach limited to one aspect of the exam and premature imaging. The systematic approach begins with a complete history and physical examination before any testing.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) Progressive over months to years.** Incorrect. A chronic progressive time course is most suggestive of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer disease or Parkinson disease, not stroke.
- **B) Waxing and waning over days to weeks.** Incorrect. A waxing and waning pattern suggests inflammatory or demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis.
- **C) Acute onset over seconds to minutes.** Correct. Acute onset of neurologic symptoms over seconds to minutes is the hallmark time course for vascular events including ischemic stroke, hemorrhage, and trauma. The sudden interruption of blood supply leads to rapid onset of deficits.
- **D) Episodic over minutes to hours.** Incorrect. An episodic pattern suggests transient ischemic attacks (TIA), seizures, or migraines, which are self-limited events rather than completed strokes.

### Q3. Answer: D
- **A) Diplopia.** Incorrect. Diplopia (double vision) is one of the "D's" and localizes to the brainstem or posterior fossa, not the cortex. It results from dysfunction of cranial nerves III, IV, or VI or their nuclei.
- **B) Dysphagia.** Incorrect. Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) is a brainstem sign, one of the "D's." It localizes to the lower brainstem where cranial nerves IX and X control swallowing.
- **C) Dysmetria.** Incorrect. Dysmetria is a cerebellar sign indicating impaired coordination of limb movements. It is grouped with brainstem/posterior fossa signs, not cortical signs.
- **D) Aphasia.** Correct. Aphasia is a language deficit and one of the cortical "A's." Broca aphasia (expressive, non-fluent) localizes to the left inferior frontal gyrus, while Wernicke aphasia (receptive, fluent) localizes to the left posterior superior temporal gyrus. The cortical "A's" include aphasia, anopsia, agnosia, apraxia, and amnesia.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) Fasciculations and muscle atrophy.** Incorrect. Fasciculations and atrophy are hallmarks of lower motor neuron (LMN) lesions. They occur because of denervation of muscle fibers, which leads to spontaneous firing and eventual loss of muscle bulk.
- **B) Hyporeflexia and flaccidity.** Incorrect. Hyporeflexia and flaccidity are LMN signs. One caveat is that acute UMN injury (such as acute stroke or spinal shock) may transiently present with decreased reflexes and flaccidity before classic UMN signs develop.
- **C) Hyperreflexia, spasticity, and a positive Babinski sign.** Correct. UMN lesions affecting the corticospinal tract produce hyperreflexia (4+ reflexes), spasticity (velocity-dependent increased tone), and a Babinski sign (extensor plantar response). Other UMN signs include sustained clonus and a pyramidal pattern of weakness.
- **D) Fatiguable weakness that worsens with repetitive use.** Incorrect. Fatiguable weakness is characteristic of neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis, not UMN lesions.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) Right homonymous hemianopsia.** Incorrect. A right homonymous hemianopsia results from a lesion in the left visual fibers posterior to the optic chiasm (left optic tract, left lateral geniculate nucleus, or left optic radiations), not at the chiasm itself.
- **B) Left monocular vision loss.** Incorrect. Monocular vision loss results from a lesion anterior to the optic chiasm, affecting either the eye itself or the ipsilateral optic nerve. This is a pre-chiasmal lesion.
- **C) Bitemporal hemianopsia.** Correct. The optic chiasm is where nasal retinal fibers from each eye cross. A lesion compressing the chiasm (classically a pituitary adenoma) disrupts these crossing fibers, causing loss of the temporal visual fields in both eyes. This is a classic bitemporal hemianopsia.
- **D) Right superior quadrantanopsia.** Incorrect. A superior quadrantanopsia ("pie in the sky") results from a lesion in Meyer's loop in the temporal lobe, which carries inferior visual field fibers from the optic radiations. This is a post-chiasmal lesion.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) UMN facial weakness affects the entire ipsilateral face; LMN facial weakness spares the forehead.** Incorrect. This reverses the pattern. UMN lesions spare the forehead because the upper face receives bilateral cortical input, while LMN lesions affect the entire hemi-face.
- **B) UMN facial weakness spares the forehead (lower two-thirds weakness contralateral to the lesion); LMN facial weakness affects the entire ipsilateral hemi-face.** Correct. In UMN facial weakness, the forehead is spared because the upper division of the facial nerve nucleus receives bilateral cortical innervation. The lower face is weak contralateral to the brain lesion. In LMN facial weakness (e.g., Bell palsy), the entire ipsilateral hemi-face is weak because the peripheral nerve itself is damaged, including inability to raise the eyebrow or close the eye.
- **C) UMN facial weakness is always accompanied by hearing loss; LMN facial weakness is not.** Incorrect. UMN facial weakness is not typically associated with hearing loss. Interestingly, LMN facial weakness (Bell palsy) may be associated with hyperacusis due to involvement of the stapedial nerve branch of CN VII.
- **D) UMN facial weakness causes ipsilateral deficits; LMN facial weakness causes contralateral deficits.** Incorrect. The lateralization is reversed. UMN facial weakness causes contralateral lower face weakness (the corticobulbar tract crosses), while LMN facial weakness causes ipsilateral deficits because the peripheral nerve innervates the same-side face.

### Q7. Answer: D
- **A) Amnesia.** Incorrect. Amnesia (memory disturbance) is one of the cortical "A's." It localizes to the cerebral cortex, particularly the temporal lobes and hippocampal structures.
- **B) Apraxia.** Incorrect. Apraxia (inability to perform learned motor acts despite intact motor and sensory function) is a cortical sign, one of the "A's."
- **C) Agnosia.** Incorrect. Agnosia (failure to recognize stimuli, including anosognosia or neglect) is a cortical sign, one of the "A's," typically associated with parietal lobe lesions.
- **D) Dysconjugate gaze.** Correct. Dysconjugate gaze (eyes not moving together) is one of the brainstem "D's." It localizes to the brainstem where cranial nerve nuclei (III, IV, VI) and internuclear pathways (medial longitudinal fasciculus) coordinate conjugate eye movements. Other brainstem "D's" include diplopia, dysphagia, dysequilibrium, and dyscoordination.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Resting tremor and cogwheel rigidity.** Incorrect. Resting tremor and rigidity are hallmarks of basal ganglia (extrapyramidal) disease, particularly Parkinson disease. Cerebellar tremor is an intention tremor that worsens on approach to a target, not at rest.
- **B) Intention tremor, dysmetria, and dysdiadochokinesia with no sensory deficits.** Correct. The cerebellum modulates motor systems to produce smooth, coordinated movements. Cerebellar lesions cause intention tremor (worsening on approach to target), dysmetria (overshoot/undershoot), and dysdiadochokinesia (impaired rapid alternating movements). Importantly, cerebellar lesions produce pure motor symptoms with no sensory deficits, and tone is typically normal to decreased.
- **C) Hyperreflexia and spasticity.** Incorrect. Hyperreflexia and spasticity are UMN signs resulting from corticospinal tract damage. Cerebellar lesions typically produce normal to low tone and do not cause hyperreflexia.
- **D) Stocking-glove sensory loss with hyporeflexia.** Incorrect. A stocking-glove distribution of sensory loss with hyporeflexia is the classic pattern of peripheral polyneuropathy, a PNS disorder. Cerebellar lesions do not produce sensory deficits.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Proximal symmetric weakness with preserved reflexes.** Incorrect. This pattern describes a myopathy (muscle disease), where weakness is proximal (shoulder and hip girdle) and reflexes are preserved until late in the disease. There are no sensory deficits in myopathy.
- **B) Symmetric distal stocking-glove sensorimotor deficits with hyporeflexia.** Correct. Peripheral polyneuropathy is a length-dependent process where the longest nerves are affected first, producing symmetric distal sensory loss and weakness in a stocking-glove distribution. Reflexes are diminished or absent. Diabetic polyneuropathy is a classic example.
- **C) UMN signs combined with LMN signs and no sensory involvement.** Incorrect. The combination of UMN and LMN signs without sensory involvement is characteristic of motor neuron disease such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), where both the corticospinal tract and anterior horn cells are affected.
- **D) Pure motor fatiguable weakness with preserved reflexes.** Incorrect. Fatiguable weakness that worsens with repetitive use, with preserved reflexes and no sensory deficits, is characteristic of a neuromuscular junction disorder such as myasthenia gravis.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Ipsilateral loss of pain and temperature below the lesion.** Incorrect. The spinothalamic tract (carrying pain and temperature) crosses the midline in the spinal cord at the ventral commissure shortly after entering. Therefore, a hemisection disrupts the already-crossed fibers from the opposite side, producing contralateral loss, not ipsilateral loss.
- **B) Bilateral loss of pain and temperature at the level of the lesion only.** Incorrect. A hemisection would only disrupt one side of the cord, not both. The resulting deficit extends below the lesion, not just at the level.
- **C) Contralateral loss of pain and temperature beginning 2-3 segments below the lesion.** Correct. In Brown-Sequard syndrome, the spinothalamic tract fibers have already crossed from the opposite side. After entering the cord, pain and temperature fibers ascend 2-3 segments in Lissauer's tract before crossing. Therefore, the contralateral pain and temperature loss begins 2-3 segments below the level of the hemisection.
- **D) No pain or temperature loss because only motor tracts are affected.** Incorrect. A cord hemisection disrupts both motor and sensory tracts. The lateral corticospinal tract (motor) and dorsal columns (proprioception, vibration, light touch) are affected ipsilaterally, while the spinothalamic tract (pain and temperature) is affected contralaterally.
