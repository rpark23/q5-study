# Cerebrovascular Disease — Post-Quiz

### Q1. A 72-year-old man is brought to the emergency department after his wife found him slumped in his chair unable to speak. On examination he has right-sided face and arm weakness greater than leg, a right homonymous hemianopsia, and global aphasia. His eyes deviate to the left. Which vascular territory is most likely affected?

- A) Left anterior cerebral artery
- B) Left middle cerebral artery
- C) Left posterior cerebral artery
- D) Right middle cerebral artery

### Q2. A 65-year-old woman presents with sudden onset of isolated left leg weakness and sensory loss with minimal involvement of the arm and face. She also has urinary incontinence. MRI confirms an acute infarct along the medial surface of the right cerebral hemisphere. Which artery is occluded?

- A) Right middle cerebral artery
- B) Right posterior cerebral artery
- C) Right anterior cerebral artery
- D) Right posterior inferior cerebellar artery

### Q3. A 58-year-old man with a history of atrial fibrillation presents with sudden right homonymous hemianopsia with macular sparing. He can write his name but is unable to read what he wrote. MRI shows an acute left occipital lobe infarct extending into the splenium of the corpus callosum. Which artery is most likely occluded?

- A) Left middle cerebral artery
- B) Left anterior cerebral artery
- C) Left posterior cerebral artery
- D) Left anterior choroidal artery

### Q4. A 60-year-old man presents with acute onset of vertigo, nausea, hoarseness, and difficulty swallowing. Examination reveals left-sided Horner syndrome, loss of pain and temperature sensation on the left face and right body, left-sided limb ataxia, and leftward lateropulsion. Which vessel is most likely occluded?

- A) Left middle cerebral artery
- B) Left anterior inferior cerebellar artery
- C) Left posterior inferior cerebellar artery or vertebral artery
- D) Basilar artery

### Q5. A 45-year-old woman presents with acute right facial droop. On examination, she cannot raise her right eyebrow, cannot fully close her right eye, and has loss of taste on the anterior two-thirds of the right tongue. Forehead wrinkling is absent on the right. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Right MCA stroke causing UMN facial weakness
- B) Left MCA stroke causing UMN facial weakness
- C) Right CN VII (Bell's) palsy causing LMN facial weakness
- D) Left pontine stroke causing LMN facial weakness

### Q6. A 70-year-old man with poorly controlled hypertension and diabetes presents with sudden onset of right pure motor hemiparesis affecting the face, arm, and leg equally. Head CT is negative for hemorrhage. MRI shows a small (<15 mm) infarct in the left posterior limb of the internal capsule. Which type of ischemic stroke is this most consistent with?

- A) Embolic stroke from atrial fibrillation
- B) Thrombotic stroke of the left MCA trunk
- C) Lacunar infarct from small vessel disease
- D) Watershed infarct from hypotension

### Q7. A 52-year-old woman presents with the sudden onset of the "worst headache of her life" followed by loss of consciousness. She is found to have nuchal rigidity. A head CT shows diffuse subarachnoid blood around the basal cisterns. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

- A) Hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage
- B) Ruptured berry aneurysm
- C) Cerebral amyloid angiopathy
- D) Arteriovenous malformation

### Q8. A 55-year-old Black American man with a history of uncontrolled hypertension and diabetes presents to the emergency department with acute left hemiplegia. He lives in a historically redlined neighborhood with limited access to primary care and a food desert. He has not seen a doctor in 3 years. Approximately what percentage of the disparity in stroke incidence among Black Americans is attributed to social determinants of health?

- A) 10%
- B) 30%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

### Q9. A 68-year-old man presents to the emergency department with acute right hemiparesis and aphasia. Symptom onset was 2 hours ago. Head CT shows no hemorrhage. CT angiography reveals a left MCA occlusion. IV alteplase is administered. Which of the following additional interventions has an extended treatment window of up to 24 hours?

- A) Intra-arterial tPA only
- B) Mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion
- C) IV heparin bolus
- D) Carotid endarterectomy

### Q10. An 82-year-old man with dementia and a history of recurrent lobar hemorrhages is found to have multiple microbleeds at the gray-white junction on gradient-echo MRI. His hemorrhages are supratentorial and superficial. Neuropathologic examination of cortical vessels would most likely reveal which of the following?

- A) Fibrinoid necrosis from chronic hypertension
- B) Amyloid-beta deposition in vessel walls
- C) Granulomatous vasculitis with giant cells
- D) Atherosclerotic plaque with luminal stenosis

### Q11. A 75-year-old woman undergoes cardiac surgery complicated by prolonged hypotension. Postoperatively, she has bilateral arm weakness with relative sparing of the face and legs. MRI shows bilateral sickle-shaped infarcts along the cerebral convexity at the junction of the ACA and MCA territories. Which type of infarct does this represent?

- A) Embolic infarct of the MCA territory
- B) Lacunar infarct of the internal capsule
- C) Border zone (watershed) infarct
- D) Venous infarct from sagittal sinus thrombosis

### Q12. A 22-year-old man presents with new-onset seizures. MRI reveals a tangle of abnormal vessels in the left frontal lobe with evidence of prior hemorrhage and surrounding gliosis. Angiography confirms arteriovenous shunting. Which of the following best describes this lesion?

- A) Berry aneurysm
- B) Cavernous malformation
- C) Arteriovenous malformation (AVM)
- D) Capillary telangiectasia

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Left anterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. An ACA stroke would produce contralateral leg weakness greater than arm and face, often with urinary incontinence. This patient has arm and face predominant weakness with aphasia, which is not the ACA distribution.
- **B) Left middle cerebral artery.** Correct. This presentation is classic for a left MCA stroke: contralateral (right) face and arm weakness greater than leg, homonymous hemianopsia, global aphasia (left hemisphere is dominant for language in most people), and gaze deviation toward the lesion (eyes "look toward the stroke"). The MCA supplies the lateral cortex including motor areas for face and arm and language centers.
- **C) Left posterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. A left PCA stroke would cause a right homonymous hemianopsia and possibly alexia without agraphia, but it would not produce the prominent face/arm hemiparesis or global aphasia seen here. The PCA supplies the occipital lobe and medial temporal lobe, not the motor cortex.
- **D) Right middle cerebral artery.** Incorrect. A right MCA stroke would cause left-sided weakness and neglect rather than aphasia. This patient's deficits are right-sided, and global aphasia indicates a left (dominant) hemisphere lesion.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) Right middle cerebral artery.** Incorrect. An MCA stroke produces face and arm weakness greater than leg, the opposite of this patient's presentation. The MCA supplies the lateral cortex, which contains the face and upper extremity regions of the motor homunculus.
- **B) Right posterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. A PCA stroke produces contralateral homonymous hemianopsia and possibly memory deficits, not isolated leg weakness. The PCA supplies the visual cortex and medial temporal lobe.
- **C) Right anterior cerebral artery.** Correct. The ACA supplies the medial surface of the cerebral hemisphere, which contains the leg representation on the motor and sensory homunculus. A right ACA stroke produces left leg weakness and sensory loss with relative sparing of the arm and face, often accompanied by urinary incontinence from involvement of the medial frontal micturition centers.
- **D) Right posterior inferior cerebellar artery.** Incorrect. PICA occlusion causes lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome with vertigo, dysphagia, Horner syndrome, and crossed sensory findings. It does not cause isolated leg weakness.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Left middle cerebral artery.** Incorrect. An MCA stroke produces contralateral face/arm weakness, sensory loss, and (if left-sided) aphasia -- not isolated hemianopsia with alexia without agraphia. The MCA does not supply the occipital lobe.
- **B) Left anterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. An ACA stroke produces contralateral leg weakness, not visual field defects. The ACA supplies the medial frontal and parietal cortex, not the occipital visual cortex.
- **C) Left posterior cerebral artery.** Correct. The left PCA supplies the left occipital lobe (primary visual cortex), producing a right homonymous hemianopsia. The classic syndrome of alexia without agraphia occurs when a left PCA stroke destroys the left visual cortex and the splenium of the corpus callosum, disconnecting the intact right visual cortex from the left-hemisphere language areas. The patient can write (left language areas intact) but cannot read (visual input cannot reach language centers).
- **D) Left anterior choroidal artery.** Incorrect. The anterior choroidal artery supplies parts of the internal capsule, optic tract, and lateral geniculate body. While its occlusion can cause a hemianopsia, it would not produce the alexia without agraphia syndrome seen with PCA territory infarction.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) Left middle cerebral artery.** Incorrect. MCA stroke produces contralateral hemiparesis, sensory loss, and cortical signs (aphasia or neglect). It does not cause vertigo, dysphagia, Horner syndrome, or crossed sensory findings, which are brainstem localizing signs.
- **B) Left anterior inferior cerebellar artery.** Incorrect. AICA occlusion produces a lateral pontine syndrome with ipsilateral facial weakness, hearing loss, and cerebellar signs. It does not typically produce the specific constellation of dysphagia, hoarseness, and Horner syndrome seen in lateral medullary syndrome.
- **C) Left posterior inferior cerebellar artery or vertebral artery.** Correct. This is the classic lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome caused by occlusion of the PICA or vertebral artery. The hallmark features include ipsilateral Horner syndrome (descending sympathetics), ipsilateral facial pain/temperature loss (spinal trigeminal nucleus), contralateral body pain/temperature loss (spinothalamic tract), ipsilateral ataxia (inferior cerebellar peduncle), and dysphagia/hoarseness (nucleus ambiguus, CN IX/X).
- **D) Basilar artery.** Incorrect. Basilar artery occlusion typically produces bilateral brainstem signs and can cause locked-in syndrome or coma. The unilateral, lateralized findings in this patient are more consistent with a lateral medullary lesion.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) Right MCA stroke causing UMN facial weakness.** Incorrect. UMN facial weakness spares the forehead (due to bilateral upper motor neuron innervation of the upper face). This patient has complete right-sided facial weakness including the forehead, indicating a lower motor neuron lesion. Additionally, taste loss is a peripheral nerve finding, not a cortical finding.
- **B) Left MCA stroke causing UMN facial weakness.** Incorrect. A left MCA stroke would cause right lower face weakness with forehead sparing (UMN pattern). This patient's involvement of the forehead and loss of taste localize the lesion to the peripheral nerve, not the cortex.
- **C) Right CN VII (Bell's) palsy causing LMN facial weakness.** Correct. The entire right side of the face is weak, including the forehead (cannot raise eyebrow, cannot close eye), and there is loss of taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. This is a classic LMN (peripheral) CN VII palsy pattern. Bell's palsy is the most common cause of LMN facial weakness and presents with ipsilateral complete facial weakness plus possible taste loss and hyperacusis.
- **D) Left pontine stroke causing LMN facial weakness.** Incorrect. While a pontine lesion at the CN VII nucleus could produce LMN-type facial weakness, it would typically be accompanied by other brainstem signs (e.g., lateral gaze palsy from CN VI involvement, contralateral hemiparesis from corticospinal tract involvement). An isolated facial palsy with taste loss in an otherwise well patient is most consistent with Bell's palsy.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Embolic stroke from atrial fibrillation.** Incorrect. Cardioembolic strokes typically occlude larger cortical vessels (most commonly MCA), producing cortical signs such as aphasia, neglect, or visual field cuts. A small subcortical infarct with pure motor hemiparesis is not the typical embolic pattern.
- **B) Thrombotic stroke of the left MCA trunk.** Incorrect. Thrombotic occlusion of the MCA trunk would produce a large territory infarct with cortical signs (aphasia, neglect, hemianopsia) in addition to hemiparesis. A small capsular infarct does not result from large vessel thrombosis.
- **C) Lacunar infarct from small vessel disease.** Correct. A small (<15 mm) infarct in the posterior limb of the internal capsule producing pure motor hemiparesis is the classic lacunar stroke syndrome. Lacunar infarcts result from lipohyalinosis or microatheroma of small penetrating arteries (lenticulostriate arteries) due to chronic hypertension and diabetes. Pure motor hemiparesis (face = arm = leg) is one of the five classical lacunar syndromes.
- **D) Watershed infarct from hypotension.** Incorrect. Watershed infarcts occur at the border zones between major arterial territories (e.g., ACA-MCA junction) after hypotensive episodes. They produce bilateral or proximal limb weakness, not a unilateral pure motor deficit from a small deep infarct.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Hypertensive intracerebral hemorrhage.** Incorrect. Hypertensive hemorrhage produces bleeding into deep brain structures (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum), which may rupture into the ventricles. It does not typically present as diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage in the basal cisterns.
- **B) Ruptured berry aneurysm.** Correct. Berry (saccular) aneurysms are the most frequent cause of clinically significant subarachnoid hemorrhage. They occur at branch points of the circle of Willis, with 90% in the anterior circulation. The classic presentation is sudden-onset "worst headache of life" with possible loss of consciousness, nuchal rigidity, and CT showing blood in the basal cisterns. About 20-30% of patients have multiple aneurysms.
- **C) Cerebral amyloid angiopathy.** Incorrect. CAA causes lobar (supratentorial, superficial) intracerebral hemorrhages, not diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage. It is seen in older adults and is associated with Alzheimer disease and dementia.
- **D) Arteriovenous malformation.** Incorrect. While AVMs can cause subarachnoid or intracerebral hemorrhage, they are a much less common cause of SAH than ruptured berry aneurysms. AVMs typically present in younger patients (age 10-30) with seizures or hemorrhage, and the hemorrhage pattern is usually more focal.

### Q8. Answer: C
- **A) 10%.** Incorrect. The contribution of social determinants of health to stroke disparity is far greater than 10%. SDOH encompasses where people live, learn, work, and play, and its impact on stroke risk in Black Americans is substantial.
- **B) 30%.** Incorrect. While significant, 30% underestimates the contribution of social determinants. Research shows SDOH accounts for a majority of the stroke incidence disparity affecting Black Americans.
- **C) 60%.** Correct. Approximately 60% of the disparity in stroke incidence among Black Americans is related to social determinants of health. These include structural factors such as historical redlining, limited healthcare access, food deserts, lower income, and reduced educational opportunities -- all rooted in a legacy of slavery, Jim Crow laws, and structural racism. Evidence shows zip code is a greater risk factor than genetic code.
- **D) 90%.** Incorrect. While SDOH is the dominant contributor, attributing 90% overstates the evidence. Multiple factors including both social determinants and traditional risk factor prevalence contribute to stroke disparities.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Intra-arterial tPA only.** Incorrect. While intra-arterial thrombolysis can be part of endovascular treatment, the extended 24-hour window (DAWN trial) specifically applies to mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion, not to intra-arterial tPA alone.
- **B) Mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion.** Correct. The DAWN trial demonstrated that mechanical thrombectomy can be performed up to 24 hours after symptom onset in select patients with large vessel occlusion and favorable imaging profiles. A catheter is inserted through the femoral artery and threaded to the intracranial vessels for mechanical clot retrieval. This dramatically extended the treatment window beyond the 4.5-hour IV tPA limit.
- **C) IV heparin bolus.** Incorrect. Heparin is NOT used for acute ischemic stroke treatment. No heparin is given for acute stroke; antiplatelet therapy is initiated within 48 hours (waiting 24 hours if thrombolytics were administered).
- **D) Carotid endarterectomy.** Incorrect. Carotid endarterectomy is a secondary prevention procedure for patients with symptomatic internal carotid stenosis greater than 70%. It is not an acute stroke intervention and is not performed in the emergency setting.

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) Fibrinoid necrosis from chronic hypertension.** Incorrect. Fibrinoid necrosis affects small penetrating arteries and causes deep hypertensive hemorrhages in the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, and cerebellum. This patient's hemorrhages are superficial and lobar, which is characteristic of CAA, not hypertensive vasculopathy.
- **B) Amyloid-beta deposition in vessel walls.** Correct. This is cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), the second most common cause of primary intracerebral hemorrhage. CAA is characterized by deposition of amyloid-beta in the walls of cortical and leptomeningeal vessels. It is associated with Alzheimer disease and dementia, causes supratentorial lobar hemorrhages, and shows multiple microbleeds at the gray-white junction on gradient-echo MRI. It is associated with Alzheimer disease but NOT systemic amyloidosis.
- **C) Granulomatous vasculitis with giant cells.** Incorrect. Granulomatous vasculitis (such as primary angiitis of the CNS) is a rare inflammatory condition. It does not produce the pattern of recurrent lobar hemorrhages with microbleeds characteristic of CAA.
- **D) Atherosclerotic plaque with luminal stenosis.** Incorrect. Atherosclerosis affects large- and medium-sized arteries (carotid, vertebral, basilar) and causes thrombotic or embolic infarcts, not recurrent superficial lobar hemorrhages. The pattern described is classic for CAA.

### Q11. Answer: C
- **A) Embolic infarct of the MCA territory.** Incorrect. Embolic infarcts typically affect a single vascular territory (most commonly MCA) and produce a wedge-shaped infarct. This patient's bilateral, sickle-shaped infarcts at the ACA-MCA junction suggest a hemodynamic mechanism, not embolism.
- **B) Lacunar infarct of the internal capsule.** Incorrect. Lacunar infarcts are small (<15 mm), deep subcortical lesions. This patient has bilateral cortical infarcts at arterial border zones, which is a completely different mechanism and location.
- **C) Border zone (watershed) infarct.** Correct. Border zone (watershed) infarcts occur at the most distal reaches of arterial blood supply, in the regions between major vascular territories. After prolonged hypotension (as during cardiac surgery), these areas are most vulnerable to ischemia. The ACA-MCA border zone along the cerebral convexity produces the characteristic sickle-shaped band of necrosis. Bilateral arm weakness with sparing of face and legs reflects the location of the upper extremity on the homunculus at the ACA-MCA watershed zone.
- **D) Venous infarct from sagittal sinus thrombosis.** Incorrect. Sagittal sinus thrombosis produces hemorrhagic venous infarcts, often bilateral, but is typically seen in young women and is associated with risk factors such as oral contraceptives, pregnancy, or hypercoagulable states. The clinical context of intraoperative hypotension and the characteristic border zone pattern point to watershed infarction.

### Q12. Answer: C
- **A) Berry aneurysm.** Incorrect. Berry aneurysms are saccular outpouchings at arterial branch points in the circle of Willis. They present with subarachnoid hemorrhage (thunderclap headache), not seizures with a parenchymal tangle of vessels. Berry aneurysms are focal dilatations, not tangles of abnormal vessels with arteriovenous shunting.
- **B) Cavernous malformation.** Incorrect. Cavernous malformations (cavernous hemangiomas) consist of closely packed, thin-walled vascular channels with no intervening neural tissue and are elastica-stain negative. Critically, they do not demonstrate arteriovenous shunting on angiography and are often angiographically occult. The presence of arteriovenous shunting on angiography distinguishes AVMs from cavernous malformations.
- **C) Arteriovenous malformation (AVM).** Correct. AVMs are tortuous vascular masses composed of "arterialized" veins (vessels with abnormal or incomplete elastica) that demonstrate arteriovenous shunting. They are most commonly found in the MCA territory, usually recognized at age 10-30, and present with seizures or hemorrhage. Males are affected twice as often as females. Microscopy shows enlarged vessels separated by gliotic tissue with evidence of prior hemorrhage. AVMs are amenable to surgical resection.
- **D) Capillary telangiectasia.** Incorrect. Capillary telangiectasias are small collections of dilated capillaries that have no clinical importance. They do not cause seizures, hemorrhage, or demonstrate the large arteriovenous shunting seen on angiography in this case.
