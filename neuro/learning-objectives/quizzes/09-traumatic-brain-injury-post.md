# Traumatic Brain Injury — Post-Quiz

### Q1. Which statement about TBI epidemiology is correct?

- A) A direct blow to the head is required to sustain a TBI
- B) The most common cause of TBI-related death is motor vehicle accidents
- C) Adults over 75 have the highest age-specific incidence of TBI
- D) Female patients have a higher rate of fatal TBI than male patients

### Q2. Approximately what percentage of incarcerated persons have a history of TBI?

- A) 10%
- B) 25%
- C) 50%
- D) 75%

### Q3. A patient presents after head trauma with loss of consciousness lasting 8 hours. MRI shows microhemorrhages at the grey-white junction and corpus callosum. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Epidural hematoma
- B) Subdural hematoma
- C) Diffuse axonal injury
- D) Cerebral contusion

### Q4. A 25-year-old is hit in the temporal region, briefly loses consciousness, has a lucid interval, then rapidly deteriorates. CT shows a convex hyperdensity. What is the most likely diagnosis and underlying vessel?

- A) Subdural hematoma from bridging vein tear
- B) Epidural hematoma from middle meningeal artery laceration
- C) Subarachnoid hemorrhage from aneurysm rupture
- D) Cerebral contusion from deep penetrating vessel damage

### Q5. Which feature distinguishes vasogenic edema from cytotoxic edema?

- A) Vasogenic edema affects grey and white matter equally
- B) Vasogenic edema is intracellular
- C) Vasogenic edema is potentially reversible
- D) Vasogenic edema results from Na/K pump failure

### Q6. According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, what are the three components within the fixed cranial vault?

- A) Grey matter, white matter, and CSF
- B) Brain tissue, CSF, and blood
- C) Neurons, glia, and extracellular fluid
- D) Cortex, meninges, and venous sinuses

### Q7. A trauma patient presents with bilateral periorbital ecchymosis and clear fluid draining from the nose. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Nasal bone fracture with epistaxis
- B) Orbital blowout fracture
- C) Basilar skull fracture
- D) Le Fort III facial fracture

### Q8. A patient with severe TBI has a GCS of 6. Which of the following is an appropriate management strategy for elevated ICP?

- A) Administer normal saline at maintenance rate
- B) Place an external ventricular drain to monitor ICP and drain CSF
- C) Prescribe strict bed rest only with no monitoring
- D) Discharge home with follow-up in 2 weeks

### Q9. Which combination of findings in an infant should raise the highest suspicion for abusive head trauma?

- A) Anterior fontanelle bulging and fever
- B) Subdural hemorrhages, retinal hemorrhages, and rib fractures
- C) A single skull fracture and a forehead bruise from a witnessed fall
- D) Bilateral otitis media and irritability

### Q10. A patient asks about safe firearm storage. Which counseling recommendation is most consistent with current guidelines for suicide prevention?

- A) Keep the firearm loaded in a bedside drawer for quick access
- B) Store the firearm in a high cabinet out of children's reach
- C) Store the firearm unloaded in a lockbox with ammunition locked separately
- D) Firearm storage practices have no impact on suicide risk

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C
- **A) A direct blow to the head is required to sustain a TBI.** Incorrect. A direct head impact is NOT required to sustain a TBI. Acceleration/deceleration forces without direct impact (such as whiplash in an MVA), blast waves, and penetrating injuries can all cause TBI. The key criterion is an alteration in brain function, regardless of the specific mechanism.
- **B) The most common cause of TBI-related death is motor vehicle accidents.** Incorrect. Suicide accounts for approximately 35% of TBI-related deaths, making it the leading cause. Falls account for ~30% and MVAs for ~17%. Suicide as a cause of TBI-related death is highest in adults over 75.
- **C) Adults over 75 have the highest age-specific incidence of TBI.** Correct. Adults over 75 have the highest age-specific incidence at approximately 17 per 1,000 people, followed closely by children ages 0-4 (~16/1,000) and young adults 15-24 (~10/1,000). TBI risk is highest at the extremes of age.
- **D) Female patients have a higher rate of fatal TBI than male patients.** Incorrect. Males have a higher rate of both sustaining TBI (male:female ratio ~1.5:1) and fatal TBI (ratio closer to 3:1). The higher male predominance in fatal TBI is driven in part by higher rates of MVAs and violence.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) 10%.** Incorrect. This significantly underestimates the prevalence of TBI history among incarcerated persons. The actual figure is much higher, reflecting the important intersection of TBI with social determinants of health.
- **B) 25%.** Incorrect. While this is a substantial proportion, it still underestimates the true prevalence. Approximately half of incarcerated persons have a TBI history, and having a TBI history confers a ~2.5x increased risk of incarceration.
- **C) 50%.** Correct. Approximately 50% of incarcerated persons have a history of TBI, and having a history of TBI is associated with a ~2.5x risk of incarceration. This highlights the important bidirectional relationship between TBI and incarceration as a social determinant of health. Contributing factors include limited access to care and the intersection with mental health conditions.
- **D) 75%.** Incorrect. While the prevalence is strikingly high, 75% overestimates the figure. The approximately 50% prevalence is itself remarkable and underscores the need for TBI screening and support services in correctional settings.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Epidural hematoma.** Incorrect. An epidural hematoma results from arterial bleeding (usually middle meningeal artery) between the skull and dura and appears as a convex hyperdensity on CT. It would not present as microhemorrhages at the grey-white junction on MRI.
- **B) Subdural hematoma.** Incorrect. A subdural hematoma from bridging vein tears appears as a crescent-shaped collection between the dura and arachnoid on CT. It would not show the pattern of microhemorrhages at the grey-white junction and corpus callosum described here.
- **C) Diffuse axonal injury.** Correct. The combination of prolonged loss of consciousness (>6 hours) and MRI showing microhemorrhages at the grey-white junction and corpus callosum is characteristic of diffuse axonal injury (DAI). DAI results from axonal shearing due to acceleration/deceleration forces. MRI with GRE/SWI sequences reveals lesions along white matter tracts. Notably, DAI can be imaging-negative up to 50% of the time, so MRI may underestimate the extent of injury.
- **D) Cerebral contusion.** Incorrect. Cerebral contusions are bruises of brain tissue that appear as hyperdense areas on CT, most commonly in the frontal lobes and temporal tips (coup and contrecoup pattern). They do not present as diffuse microhemorrhages along white matter tracts.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Subdural hematoma from bridging vein tear.** Incorrect. A subdural hematoma appears as a concave (crescent-shaped) hyperdensity on CT, not convex. Additionally, subdurals are more common in elderly patients with cerebral atrophy and typically do not present with the classic lucid interval pattern.
- **B) Epidural hematoma from middle meningeal artery laceration.** Correct. This is the classic presentation of an epidural hematoma: a young patient with temporal region trauma, brief loss of consciousness, a lucid interval during which they appear to recover, followed by rapid deterioration as the arterial hematoma expands. CT shows a convex (lens-shaped) hyperdensity between the skull and dura. This is a neurologic emergency requiring neurosurgical drainage (burr hole). Epidural hematomas are most common in patients in their 20s and rare in patients over 50-60.
- **C) Subarachnoid hemorrhage from aneurysm rupture.** Incorrect. SAH from aneurysm rupture classically presents with sudden "thunderclap" headache, not with a lucid interval pattern after trauma. Traumatic SAH does occur but appears as blood in the sulci, not as a convex collection on CT.
- **D) Cerebral contusion from deep penetrating vessel damage.** Incorrect. Cerebral contusions appear as hyperdense areas within brain parenchyma (commonly frontal and temporal regions), not as a convex extra-axial collection. They also do not typically present with the lucid interval pattern.

### Q5. Answer: C
- **A) Vasogenic edema affects grey and white matter equally.** Incorrect. Vasogenic edema predominantly affects white matter more than grey matter. In contrast, cytotoxic edema affects both grey and white matter because it involves intracellular swelling regardless of cell type.
- **B) Vasogenic edema is intracellular.** Incorrect. Vasogenic edema is extracellular. It results from blood-brain barrier disruption that allows excess fluid into the extracellular space. Cytotoxic edema is the intracellular type, resulting from cellular swelling due to Na/K pump failure.
- **C) Vasogenic edema is potentially reversible.** Correct. Vasogenic edema is potentially reversible because the cells have not necessarily died -- excess fluid is in the extracellular space due to blood-brain barrier disruption. If the underlying cause is treated (e.g., removal of a hematoma or tumor), the edema can resolve. In contrast, cytotoxic edema is irreversible because it represents cellular death. However, sustained vasogenic edema with elevated pressure can eventually lead to cytotoxic edema.
- **D) Vasogenic edema results from Na/K pump failure.** Incorrect. Na/K-ATPase pump failure is the mechanism of cytotoxic edema, not vasogenic edema. In cytotoxic edema, energy failure causes the pump to stop working, sodium accumulates intracellularly, and water follows by osmosis. Vasogenic edema results from blood-brain barrier disruption.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) Grey matter, white matter, and CSF.** Incorrect. While grey and white matter are components of brain tissue, this answer incorrectly separates them as distinct compartments and omits blood, which is one of the three components of the Monro-Kellie doctrine.
- **B) Brain tissue, CSF, and blood.** Correct. The Monro-Kellie doctrine states that the cranium has a constant volume containing three components: brain tissue (~80%), CSF (~10%, with ~500 mL/day produced and reabsorbed), and blood (~10%). If any one component increases in volume, ICP rises because the total volume cannot change. This is why hemorrhage, edema, or hydrocephalus leads to elevated ICP.
- **C) Neurons, glia, and extracellular fluid.** Incorrect. While neurons and glia are cellular components of the brain, this does not represent the Monro-Kellie doctrine. The doctrine describes macroscopic compartments (brain tissue, CSF, blood), not cellular-level components.
- **D) Cortex, meninges, and venous sinuses.** Incorrect. This lists anatomical structures rather than the three volume components of the Monro-Kellie doctrine. The meninges and venous sinuses are part of the cranial anatomy but are not the compartments described by this principle.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) Nasal bone fracture with epistaxis.** Incorrect. While a nasal fracture can cause bleeding from the nose, the combination of bilateral periorbital ecchymosis (raccoon eyes) and clear nasal drainage (suggesting CSF rhinorrhea) points to a basilar skull fracture rather than an isolated nasal fracture. Epistaxis produces blood, not clear fluid.
- **B) Orbital blowout fracture.** Incorrect. An orbital blowout fracture typically presents with periorbital swelling and restricted eye movements (especially upward gaze due to inferior rectus entrapment), not the specific pattern of bilateral periorbital ecchymosis with clear nasal drainage.
- **C) Basilar skull fracture.** Correct. This patient has two classic signs of basilar skull fracture: raccoon eyes (bilateral periorbital ecchymosis) and CSF rhinorrhea (clear fluid draining from the nose indicating a CSF leak). Other signs include Battle's sign (mastoid ecchymosis) and hemotympanum (blood behind the eardrum). Basilar skull fractures can be imaging-negative and are often diagnosed clinically. CSF leaks are important to identify for infection prevention.
- **D) Le Fort III facial fracture.** Incorrect. While Le Fort III fractures involve craniofacial dissociation and can have periorbital findings, the specific combination of raccoon eyes and clear CSF rhinorrhea in a trauma patient is most classically associated with a basilar skull fracture.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Administer normal saline at maintenance rate.** Incorrect. For elevated ICP in severe TBI, hypertonic fluids (hypertonic saline 3% or 23%, or mannitol) are used to draw fluid out of the brain via osmotic effects. Normal saline at maintenance rate does not actively treat elevated ICP.
- **B) Place an external ventricular drain to monitor ICP and drain CSF.** Correct. An EVD (External Ventricular Drain) is placed into the lateral ventricle and serves a dual purpose: it allows continuous monitoring of ICP and enables therapeutic drainage of CSF to reduce intracranial pressure. Other interventions for elevated ICP include hypertonic fluids, hematoma evacuation, and hemicraniectomy (removal of part of the skull to allow swelling).
- **C) Prescribe strict bed rest only with no monitoring.** Incorrect. A GCS of 6 represents severe TBI (GCS 3-8), which requires hospital admission to a Neuro-ICU with close monitoring of blood pressure, ICP, and temperature. Neuroprotective measures and active management of elevated ICP are essential. Bed rest alone without monitoring is dangerously inadequate.
- **D) Discharge home with follow-up in 2 weeks.** Incorrect. A GCS of 6 means the patient is comatose. Discharge home would be medically negligent. Severe TBI requires ICU admission, ICP monitoring, seizure prevention, infection prevention, and potentially neurosurgical intervention.

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) Anterior fontanelle bulging and fever.** Incorrect. A bulging fontanelle with fever in an infant is more suggestive of meningitis or another infectious process, not abusive head trauma. While increased ICP from abuse can cause a bulging fontanelle, the combination with fever points toward infection as the primary differential.
- **B) Subdural hemorrhages, retinal hemorrhages, and rib fractures.** Correct. This combination is highly suspicious for abusive head trauma. Subdural hemorrhages on brain imaging, retinal hemorrhages on fundoscopic exam, and fractures found on skeletal survey (especially rib and long bone fractures) together form the classic constellation of findings in abusive head trauma. Additional red flags include story inconsistencies and suspicious cutaneous bruising. Abusive head trauma is the leading cause of death in US children under 5.
- **C) A single skull fracture and a forehead bruise from a witnessed fall.** Incorrect. A single linear skull fracture with a consistent mechanism (witnessed fall) and an appropriate bruise location is more consistent with accidental trauma. While any injury in a child warrants careful evaluation, a consistent story and mechanism make abuse less likely.
- **D) Bilateral otitis media and irritability.** Incorrect. Bilateral ear infections causing irritability are an extremely common pediatric presentation and are not associated with abusive head trauma. This would be managed with appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Keep the firearm loaded in a bedside drawer for quick access.** Incorrect. This is the highest-risk storage scenario. Easy access to a loaded firearm significantly increases the risk of impulsive self-harm, particularly during a crisis. Cranial gunshot wounds are over 90% fatal, making prevention of access during crises critical.
- **B) Store the firearm in a high cabinet out of children's reach.** Incorrect. While keeping firearms away from children is important, simply placing a firearm in a high location without it being locked and unloaded does not provide adequate safety, especially for suicide prevention in adults who can access high shelves.
- **C) Store the firearm unloaded in a lockbox with ammunition locked separately.** Correct. If firearms are kept in the home, current guidelines recommend storing them unloaded, in a lockbox, with ammunition locked separately. The safest option is to store firearms outside the home entirely. Secure firearm storage is specifically highlighted as a suicide prevention strategy, as it creates barriers during impulsive crises. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available 24/7 by call or text.
- **D) Firearm storage practices have no impact on suicide risk.** Incorrect. This is factually incorrect. Firearms are involved in approximately 53% of suicides, and secure storage is an evidence-based suicide prevention strategy. Creating barriers to firearm access during crises reduces impulsive self-harm.
