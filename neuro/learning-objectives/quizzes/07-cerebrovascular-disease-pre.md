# Cerebrovascular Disease — Pre-Quiz

### Q1. What is the ranking of stroke as a cause of death in the United States (as of 2022)?

- A) 2nd leading cause
- B) 3rd leading cause
- C) 5th leading cause
- D) 10th leading cause

### Q2. Which artery is the first branch of the internal carotid artery?

- A) Middle cerebral artery
- B) Anterior cerebral artery
- C) Ophthalmic artery
- D) Posterior communicating artery

### Q3. Which vascular territory supplies the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe?

- A) Middle cerebral artery (MCA)
- B) Anterior cerebral artery (ACA)
- C) Posterior cerebral artery (PCA)
- D) Superior cerebellar artery (SCA)

### Q4. The cortical "A's" (aphasia, anopsia, agnosia, apraxia) localize a stroke to which circulation?

- A) Posterior circulation
- B) Anterior circulation
- C) Spinal circulation
- D) Venous circulation

### Q5. Which stroke syndrome presents with contralateral leg weakness greater than arm or face weakness?

- A) MCA stroke
- B) ACA stroke
- C) PCA stroke
- D) Lateral medullary syndrome

### Q6. In upper motor neuron (UMN) facial weakness, which part of the face is characteristically spared?

- A) Lower lip
- B) Chin
- C) Forehead
- D) Cheek

### Q7. What percentage of all strokes are ischemic?

- A) 20%
- B) 50%
- C) 65%
- D) 80%

### Q8. Which is the most important modifiable risk factor for stroke?

- A) Diabetes mellitus
- B) Hypertension
- C) Atrial fibrillation
- D) Smoking

### Q9. What is the maximum time window for administration of IV tPA in acute ischemic stroke?

- A) 1 hour
- B) 3 hours
- C) 4.5 hours
- D) 24 hours

### Q10. "Red dead" hypereosinophilic neurons with pyknotic nuclei become visible on microscopy at what time point after an irreversible ischemic insult?

- A) 1-2 hours
- B) 6-12 hours
- C) 48-72 hours
- D) 7 days

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C
- **A) 2nd leading cause.** Incorrect. Heart disease and cancer rank higher than stroke. The 2nd leading cause of death is cancer, not stroke.
- **B) 3rd leading cause.** Incorrect. Although stroke is a major killer, it ranks below heart disease, cancer, and other causes. Third place is occupied by other conditions.
- **C) 5th leading cause.** Correct. In 2022, stroke was the 5th leading cause of death in the United States. Despite declining death rates from cerebrovascular disease (one of the top 10 public health achievements), stroke remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality.
- **D) 10th leading cause.** Incorrect. Stroke ranks significantly higher than 10th. It is the most common neurological disease and a major contributor to both death and disability.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) Middle cerebral artery.** Incorrect. The MCA is the larger terminal branch of the ICA but is not its first branch. The MCA continues as the main trunk after the ICA bifurcates.
- **B) Anterior cerebral artery.** Incorrect. The ACA is the other terminal branch of the ICA at its bifurcation, not the first branch given off along its course.
- **C) Ophthalmic artery.** Correct. The ophthalmic artery is the first branch of the internal carotid artery. It supplies the eye and orbit, which is why ischemia in the ICA territory can cause amaurosis fugax (transient monocular vision loss).
- **D) Posterior communicating artery.** Incorrect. The PComm arises from the ICA but is not its first branch. It connects the anterior and posterior circulations as part of the Circle of Willis.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Middle cerebral artery (MCA).** Incorrect. The MCA supplies the lateral neocortex including the face and arm areas of motor cortex and language centers. It does not supply the occipital lobe.
- **B) Anterior cerebral artery (ACA).** Incorrect. The ACA supplies the medial aspects of the cerebral hemispheres, including the leg area of the motor cortex. It does not extend to the occipital lobe.
- **C) Posterior cerebral artery (PCA).** Correct. The PCA supplies the occipital cortex (primary visual cortex), the mesial temporal lobes (including hippocampus), and most of the thalamus. PCA stroke classically causes contralateral homonymous hemianopsia.
- **D) Superior cerebellar artery (SCA).** Incorrect. The SCA supplies the superior surface of the cerebellum and parts of the brainstem. It is part of the posterior circulation but does not supply the visual cortex.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Posterior circulation.** Incorrect. The posterior circulation produces the "D's" (diplopia, dysphagia, dysequilibrium, dyscoordination), not the cortical "A's." Posterior circulation strokes affect the brainstem and cerebellum.
- **B) Anterior circulation.** Correct. The cortical "A's" -- aphasia, anopsia, agnosia, apraxia, amnesia, alexia, agraphia, and acalculia -- indicate cortical dysfunction in the anterior circulation (MCA and ACA territories). These higher cortical functions are localized to cerebral cortex supplied by the anterior circulation.
- **C) Spinal circulation.** Incorrect. Spinal cord strokes produce motor and sensory deficits in the limbs and trunk, not cortical signs like aphasia or agnosia. The cortical "A's" require cerebral cortex involvement.
- **D) Venous circulation.** Incorrect. Venous sinus thrombosis can present with various neurologic signs, but the cortical "A's" as a localizing principle specifically indicate anterior arterial circulation territory involvement.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) MCA stroke.** Incorrect. MCA stroke produces contralateral face and arm weakness greater than leg weakness, the opposite pattern. This is because the lateral cortex supplied by the MCA includes the face and upper extremity regions of the motor homunculus.
- **B) ACA stroke.** Correct. The ACA supplies the medial surface of the cerebral hemisphere, which includes the leg area of the motor and sensory cortex on the homunculus. ACA stroke therefore produces contralateral leg weakness and sensory loss greater than arm or face involvement.
- **C) PCA stroke.** Incorrect. PCA strokes primarily produce visual field deficits (contralateral homonymous hemianopsia) and memory impairment, not prominent motor weakness. The PCA does not supply motor cortex.
- **D) Lateral medullary syndrome.** Incorrect. Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome produces ipsilateral facial pain/temperature loss, Horner syndrome, ataxia, and contralateral body pain/temperature loss. It does not produce a leg-predominant weakness pattern.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Lower lip.** Incorrect. The lower lip is part of the lower face, which is affected in UMN facial weakness. UMN lesions cause weakness of the contralateral lower face because the lower face has only contralateral UMN innervation.
- **B) Chin.** Incorrect. The chin is part of the lower face and is weak in UMN facial palsy. The chin does not have the bilateral upper motor neuron innervation that protects the forehead.
- **C) Forehead.** Correct. In UMN (central) facial weakness, the forehead is spared because the upper face (frontalis and orbicularis oculi) receives bilateral upper motor neuron innervation. This means a unilateral cortical or corticobulbar tract lesion still allows the intact contralateral pathway to activate the upper face muscles.
- **D) Cheek.** Incorrect. The cheek is part of the lower face musculature and is affected in UMN facial weakness. Only the upper face (forehead and eye closure) is spared due to bilateral innervation.

### Q7. Answer: D
- **A) 20%.** Incorrect. Approximately 20% of strokes are hemorrhagic, not ischemic. This figure represents the minority of strokes caused by bleeding.
- **B) 50%.** Incorrect. The split between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke is not equal. Ischemic stroke is far more common than hemorrhagic stroke.
- **C) 65%.** Incorrect. While ischemic stroke is the majority, the figure is higher than 65%. Approximately 80% of all strokes are ischemic.
- **D) 80%.** Correct. Approximately 80% of all strokes are ischemic, resulting from arterial occlusion and reduced oxygen delivery to the brain. The remaining 20% are hemorrhagic (intracerebral hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage).

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Diabetes mellitus.** Incorrect. Diabetes is an important modifiable risk factor for stroke, but it is not the single most important one. Blood glucose control is part of secondary prevention but is not the top priority.
- **B) Hypertension.** Correct. Hypertension is the most important modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. It contributes to atherosclerosis, small vessel disease (lacunar infarcts), and intracerebral hemorrhage from microaneurysm rupture.
- **C) Atrial fibrillation.** Incorrect. Atrial fibrillation is an important modifiable risk factor that predisposes to cardioembolic stroke, but hypertension has a greater overall population-attributable risk.
- **D) Smoking.** Incorrect. Smoking is a significant modifiable risk factor for stroke and is a target for secondary prevention (smoking cessation). However, hypertension remains the most impactful modifiable factor.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) 1 hour.** Incorrect. While earlier treatment yields better outcomes ("time is brain"), the approved window for IV tPA extends well beyond 1 hour. Treatment is not restricted to such a narrow time frame.
- **B) 3 hours.** Incorrect. The original FDA approval for IV tPA was within 3 hours, but subsequent trials (ECASS III) extended the window. The current guideline allows administration up to 4.5 hours.
- **C) 4.5 hours.** Correct. IV tPA (alteplase) or tenecteplase can be administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset (or last known normal) if there are no contraindications. Contraindications include hemorrhage on CT, elevated INR, recent surgery, and bleeding disorders.
- **D) 24 hours.** Incorrect. The 24-hour window applies to mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion (per the DAWN trial), not to IV thrombolysis. IV tPA given beyond 4.5 hours carries unacceptable hemorrhagic risk.

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) 1-2 hours.** Incorrect. At 1-2 hours, gross and microscopic changes are generally not yet detectable. Grossly, no abnormalities are visible until around 12 hours after the insult.
- **B) 6-12 hours.** Correct. "Red dead" hypereosinophilic neurons with pyknotic nuclei in selectively vulnerable regions (such as hippocampal CA1) become evident 6-12 hours after an irreversible hypoxic-ischemic insult. These neurons are shrunken, deeply eosinophilic, and represent early evidence of neuronal necrosis.
- **C) 48-72 hours.** Incorrect. By 48-72 hours, the inflammatory response is already well underway with neutrophilic infiltration. Red dead neurons appear much earlier in the timeline.
- **D) 7 days.** Incorrect. By 7 days, macrophages have arrived to begin phagocytosis of necrotic debris. Red dead neurons are an acute finding that appears within the first 12 hours.
