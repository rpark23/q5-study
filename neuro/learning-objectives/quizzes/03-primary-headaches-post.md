# Primary Headaches — Post-Quiz

**1. A 28-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with a sudden-onset "thunderclap" headache that she describes as the worst headache of her life. She has no prior headache history. Her mother died of a ruptured cerebral aneurysm. Vital signs reveal a blood pressure of 160/95 mmHg. Which SNOOP features are present in this case that should prompt urgent evaluation?**

A) Systemic symptoms only
B) Onset (sudden, worst headache), prior headache history (new), and systemic symptoms (family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage)
C) Neurologic symptoms and other associated conditions only
D) Prior headache history is different and neurologic symptoms only

**2. A 35-year-old woman presents with recurrent headaches over the past year. She reports at least 8 episodes of throbbing, unilateral headaches lasting 12-24 hours. During attacks, she becomes nauseous and retreats to a dark, quiet room. She avoids exercise during episodes because it worsens the pain. Physical examination is normal. Which of the following criteria does this patient fulfill for migraine without aura?**

A) Criteria A, B, and C only — she lacks the required photophobia and phonophobia
B) Criteria A through E — she has at least 5 attacks, duration 4-72 hours, unilateral and pulsating pain aggravated by activity, and nausea with photophobia and phonophobia
C) She does not meet criteria because the headaches are not bilateral
D) She does not meet criteria because nausea alone is insufficient for criterion D

**3. A 22-year-old man experiences a 20-minute episode of shimmering zigzag lines in his right visual field that gradually expand, followed by a severe left-sided pulsating headache with nausea. Which of the following statements about this patient's aura is most accurate?**

A) The aura likely represents a transient ischemic attack because it involves visual field loss
B) Motor weakness should be expected as a component of typical migraine aura
C) The visual aura is consistent with the most common aura type, which accounts for 99% of migraine auras and should develop over at least 4 minutes and resolve within 60 minutes
D) The aura is atypical because true migraine auras last at least 2 hours

The pain does not worsen with walking or climbing stairs. Which primary headache diagnosis is most consistent with this presentation?**

A) Migraine without aura

**12. A 33-year-old woman with chronic migraine (18 headache days per month) has failed trials of propranolol, topiramate, and amitriptyline over the past year. Which of the following correctly describes the mechanism by which CGRP contributes to migraine pathophysiology?**

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Systemic symptoms only.** Incorrect. While the patient does have systemic risk factors (family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage), this answer fails to capture the critical "onset" features (sudden, worst headache of life) and the fact that she has no prior headache history, which represents a change in pattern under the "P" category.
- **B) Onset (sudden, worst headache), prior headache history (new), and systemic symptoms (family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage).** Correct. This patient has multiple SNOOP red flags: the "O" for onset (first or worst, sudden-onset headache), the "P" for prior headache history (no prior history means this is a new headache type), and the "S" for systemic features (family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage). These features together mandate urgent workup including neuroimaging and likely lumbar puncture.
- **C) Neurologic symptoms and other associated conditions only.** Incorrect. The vignette does not describe focal neurologic deficits, altered mental status, seizure, papilledema, or stiff neck. There is also no mention of head trauma, positional worsening, or association with Valsalva.
- **D) Prior headache history is different and neurologic symptoms only.** Incorrect. While the lack of prior headache history is indeed a red flag, there are no neurologic symptoms described. This answer also omits the critical onset features and the family history of subarachnoid hemorrhage.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) Criteria A, B, and C only — she lacks the required photophobia and phonophobia.** Incorrect. Criterion D requires EITHER photophobia AND phonophobia, OR nausea with or without vomiting. The patient retreats to a dark, quiet room (implying photophobia and phonophobia) and has nausea, so she fulfills criterion D by both pathways.
- **B) Criteria A through E — she has at least 5 attacks, duration 4-72 hours, unilateral and pulsating pain aggravated by activity, and nausea with photophobia and phonophobia.** Correct. She has had 8 episodes (criterion A: at least 5), lasting 12-24 hours (criterion B: 4-72 hours), with unilateral and pulsating quality aggravated by activity (criterion C: at least 2 of the listed features), with nausea and she seeks dark/quiet rooms indicating photophobia and phonophobia (criterion D), and normal examination (criterion E: not attributed to another disorder).
- **C) She does not meet criteria because the headaches are not bilateral.** Incorrect. Migraine headaches are characteristically unilateral. The diagnostic criteria for criterion C list unilateral location as one of the qualifying features. Bilateral headaches are more typical of tension-type headache.
- **D) She does not meet criteria because nausea alone is insufficient for criterion D.** Incorrect. Criterion D is satisfied by EITHER photophobia AND phonophobia OR nausea with or without vomiting. Nausea alone is sufficient to meet criterion D. Additionally, this patient also demonstrates photophobia and phonophobia by retreating to a dark, quiet room.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) The aura likely represents a transient ischemic attack because it involves visual field loss.** Incorrect. The gradual onset over minutes, the characteristic zigzag/shimmering quality, and resolution followed by a typical migraine headache are classic for migraine aura. TIAs typically present with sudden onset of negative neurological symptoms and do not have a gradually expanding, shimmering quality.
- **B) Motor weakness should be expected as a component of typical migraine aura.** Incorrect. Motor weakness is explicitly NOT considered part of typical migraine aura. Migraine with motor weakness is classified separately as hemiplegic migraine and has distinct diagnostic criteria and genetic associations.
- **C) The visual aura is consistent with the most common aura type, which accounts for 99% of migraine auras and should develop over at least 4 minutes and resolve within 60 minutes.** Correct. Visual phenomena (zigzag lines, fortification spectra, scotomata) account for 99% of migraine auras. Auras characteristically develop gradually over at least 4 minutes and are reversible within 60 minutes. This patient's 20-minute shimmering zigzag episode fits this profile precisely.
- **D) The aura is atypical because true migraine auras last at least 2 hours.** Incorrect. Migraine auras resolve within 60 minutes by definition. An aura lasting more than 60 minutes should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses such as TIA or persistent aura without infarction.

### Q4. Answer: C
- **A) Migraine without aura.** Incorrect. Migraine is typically unilateral, pulsating, moderate-to-severe in intensity, aggravated by physical activity, and associated with nausea and/or photophobia and phonophobia. This patient has bilateral, band-like, mild-to-moderate pain without migrainous features and no worsening with activity.
- **B) Cluster headache.** Incorrect. Cluster headache is always unilateral, excruciating, located behind the eye, lasts 15-180 minutes per attack, and is associated with autonomic features (tearing, conjunctival injection, Horner's syndrome). This patient's presentation does not match any of these features.
- **C) Tension-type headache.** Correct. This presentation is classic for tension-type headache: bilateral location, band-like pressure quality, mild-to-moderate intensity that waxes and wanes, gradual worsening throughout the day, absence of nausea/vomiting/photophobia/phonophobia, and no aggravation by routine physical activity.
- **D) Migraine with aura.** Incorrect. There is no aura described (no visual, sensory, or language disturbances preceding the headache). Additionally, the headache characteristics themselves are inconsistent with migraine, being bilateral, non-pulsating, and not worsened by activity.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Bilateral headache, nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia.** Incorrect. This combination describes migraine. Cluster headache is always unilateral, and the associated symptoms are autonomic (tearing, injection, rhinorrhea, Horner's) rather than the photo/phonophobia and nausea seen in migraine. Cluster patients are agitated and pace, whereas migraine patients prefer stillness.
- **B) Ipsilateral tearing, conjunctival injection, rhinorrhea, and Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis).** Correct. Cluster headache is characterized by unilateral, excruciating retro-orbital pain with ipsilateral autonomic features including lacrimation, scleral injection, rhinorrhea, and Horner's syndrome. The Horner's syndrome results from neurogenic inflammation of the cavernous sinus injuring sympathetic fibers running along the internal carotid artery.
- **C) Bilateral band-like pressure with pericranial muscle tenderness.** Incorrect. This describes tension-type headache. Cluster headache pain is strictly unilateral, explosive, and deep rather than bilateral and pressure-like.
- **D) Unilateral headache with contralateral tearing and bilateral mydriasis.** Incorrect. In cluster headache, autonomic features are always ipsilateral to the headache, not contralateral. Additionally, Horner's syndrome produces ipsilateral miosis (not mydriasis), due to disruption of sympathetic innervation to the pupillary dilator muscle.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) History of peptic ulcer disease.** Incorrect. Peptic ulcer disease is a concern when prescribing NSAIDs due to their gastrointestinal side effects, not beta-blockers. Propranolol's main side effects include fatigue and depression.
- **B) Asthma.** Correct. Beta-blockers such as propranolol are contraindicated in patients with asthma because they block beta-2 adrenergic receptors in bronchial smooth muscle, which can precipitate life-threatening bronchospasm. This is the most critical contraindication to screen for before initiating propranolol for migraine prophylaxis.
- **C) Chronic kidney disease.** Incorrect. While medication dosing may require adjustment in renal disease, chronic kidney disease is not a primary listed contraindication for propranolol in migraine prophylaxis. Asthma is the specific contraindication highlighted for beta-blockers.
- **D) Hypothyroidism.** Incorrect. Hypothyroidism is not a major contraindication to beta-blocker use. Beta-blockers are actually used in the management of hyperthyroidism to control sympathetic symptoms such as tachycardia and tremor.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Valproate causes significant weight loss that may impair fertility.** Incorrect. Valproate causes weight gain, not weight loss (weight loss is associated with topiramate). Additionally, the most critical concern in this patient is teratogenicity, not weight change. Valproate is also associated with hyperandrogenism.
- **B) Valproate is teratogenic and can cause neural tube defects; it is contraindicated in women of childbearing potential who are not using reliable contraception.** Correct. Valproate is a known teratogen associated with neural tube defects, cognitive impairment, and other birth defects. Most migraine prophylactic medications are Class C or D, and valproate is one of the most dangerous in pregnancy. In a sexually active woman using unreliable contraception, this risk is the most critical consideration before prescribing.
- **C) Valproate causes irreversible renal toxicity requiring regular monitoring.** Incorrect. Valproate is associated with liver toxicity (hepatotoxicity), not renal toxicity. Liver function tests should be monitored, but the primary concern in this specific patient scenario is teratogenicity given her childbearing potential and inconsistent contraception use.
- **D) Valproate is only effective in men and has no proven benefit in women.** Incorrect. Valproate is effective for migraine prophylaxis regardless of sex. However, its teratogenic potential makes it a particularly concerning choice in women of childbearing age who are not using reliable contraception.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Left CN III (oculomotor nerve) palsy disrupting the efferent limb.** Incorrect. If the left CN III were damaged, the left pupil would fail to constrict when light is shone in either eye. The finding here is that neither pupil constricts when light is in the right eye, but both constrict when light is in the left eye, pointing to an afferent (not efferent) problem on the right side.
- **B) Right optic nerve (CN II) lesion disrupting the afferent limb of the pupillary light reflex — a relative afferent pupillary defect.** Correct. The pupillary light reflex has an afferent limb (CN II) and an efferent limb (CN III bilaterally). When the right CN II is damaged, light shone in the right eye does not generate an afferent signal, so neither pupil constricts. When light is shone in the left eye, the intact left CN II transmits the signal, and both CN III efferents cause bilateral constriction. This pattern defines a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD).
- **C) Bilateral Horner's syndrome disrupting sympathetic input.** Incorrect. Horner's syndrome affects sympathetic innervation and produces miosis, ptosis, and anhidrosis. It does not abolish the pupillary light reflex, which is a parasympathetic pathway. The light reflex would remain intact in Horner's syndrome.
- **D) Left CN II lesion disrupting the afferent limb.** Incorrect. If the left CN II were damaged, light shone in the left eye would fail to produce constriction in either pupil. The scenario describes the opposite: the left eye produces a normal bilateral response, and the right eye does not, pointing to a right-sided afferent lesion.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) Hypothalamus to lateral brainstem to C3-C4 spinal cord to superior cervical ganglion to the eye.** Incorrect. The first-order sympathetic neuron descends from the hypothalamus through the brainstem to the ciliospinal center at T1-T2, not C3-C4. The cervical spinal cord is not where the preganglionic sympathetic neurons exit.
- **B) Edinger-Westphal nucleus to CN III to ciliary ganglion to pupillary sphincter.** Incorrect. This describes the parasympathetic pathway for pupillary constriction, not the sympathetic pathway for dilation. The Edinger-Westphal nucleus is the parasympathetic nucleus of CN III. Damage to this pathway causes mydriasis, not the miosis seen in Horner's syndrome.
- **C) Hypothalamus descending through brainstem to lateral spinal cord at T1-T2, exiting via white rami communicantes to the paravertebral sympathetic chain, ascending to the superior cervical ganglion along the ICA to the pupillary dilator, superior tarsal muscle, and sweat glands.** Correct. This accurately traces the three-neuron sympathetic pathway: first-order (hypothalamus to T1-T2 ciliospinal center), second-order (exits via white rami communicantes to the paravertebral sympathetic chain and ascends to the superior cervical ganglion), and third-order (postganglionic fibers travel along the ICA to innervate the pupillary dilator muscle, superior tarsal muscle of Muller, and sweat glands). A Pancoast tumor at the lung apex compresses the second-order neuron.
- **D) Trigeminal ganglion to spinal nucleus of V to VPM thalamus to somatosensory cortex.** Incorrect. This describes the trigeminothalamic sensory pathway for facial pain and temperature, not the sympathetic pathway. It has no role in pupillary dilation or the features of Horner's syndrome.

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) Oral sumatriptan and a darkened room.** Incorrect. While triptans can be used for cluster headache, the oral route is too slow for the short-duration attacks of cluster headache (15-180 minutes). Subcutaneous or intranasal formulations are preferred. Additionally, retreating to a dark room is characteristic of migraine behavior; cluster headache patients are restless and agitated. The first-line abortive is high-flow oxygen.
- **B) High-flow 100% oxygen at least 6 L/min via non-rebreather mask for 20 minutes.** Correct. High-flow 100% FiO2 oxygen delivered at a minimum of 6 L/min via non-rebreather mask for 20 minutes is the first-line abortive treatment for cluster headache. It is effective, rapid, and has virtually no side effects. Other abortive options include subcutaneous triptans, octreotide, and intranasal DHE.
- **C) IV morphine and ketorolac.** Incorrect. Opioids should be avoided in the management of primary headache disorders. They are habit-forming, may worsen sensitization, and are not first-line treatment for cluster headache. The American Headache Society's Choosing Wisely recommendations advise against prescribing opioids as first-line treatment for recurrent headache disorders.
- **D) Oral prednisone 60 mg as a single dose.** Incorrect. While prednisone is used in cluster headache management, it is a prophylactic medication given as a 21-day taper to prevent future attacks during a cluster period, not as an acute abortive treatment for an ongoing attack. The acute attack requires high-flow oxygen or triptans.

### Q11. Answer: B
- **A) Chronic migraine; increase the dose and frequency of her current analgesics.** Incorrect. While this patient may have an underlying chronic migraine, increasing the frequency of analgesic use would perpetuate and worsen the medication overuse headache cycle. Medication overuse headache is defined as headache occurring 15 or more days per month due to overuse of abortive medications.
- **B) Medication overuse headache; discontinue the overused abortive medications and initiate appropriate prophylactic therapy.** Correct. This patient's daily analgesic use with headaches 20 or more days per month is classic for medication overuse headache (defined as headache occurring at least 15 days/month due to overuse of abortive medications). Management requires discontinuation of the overused medications and initiation of prophylactic therapy. The headache usually resolves when the overuse stops.
- **C) New daily persistent headache; obtain emergent neuroimaging.** Incorrect. New daily persistent headache has a distinct, memorable onset with daily headaches from the start. This patient has a longstanding episodic migraine history that has gradually transformed into chronic daily headache in the setting of analgesic overuse, which is the hallmark of medication overuse headache.
- **D) Tension-type headache; add butalbital to her regimen.** Incorrect. This does not match tension-type headache, and butalbital is specifically listed as a medication to avoid for headache management. The American Headache Society's Choosing Wisely recommendations advise against prescribing butalbital as first-line treatment. Butalbital is habit-forming and can itself contribute to medication overuse headache.

### Q12. Answer: B
- **A) CGRP directly inhibits serotonin reuptake in the dorsal raphe nucleus, causing vasoconstriction.** Incorrect. CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) does not inhibit serotonin reuptake. CGRP is a vasodilatory neuropeptide released from trigeminal sensory afferents that mediates neurogenic inflammation and trigeminovascular pain transmission, not serotonin reuptake inhibition.
- **B) CGRP is released from trigeminal sensory afferents during migraine, mediating trigeminovascular pain transmission and neurogenic inflammation with decreased pain thresholds and increased pain magnitude.** Correct. During migraine, activation of the trigeminothalamic pathway leads to release of neuropeptides including substance P, CGRP, and neurokinin A from trigeminal sensory afferents. CGRP specifically mediates trigeminovascular pain transmission and causes sensitization characterized by decreased pain thresholds and increased pain magnitude. This mechanism is the basis for both CGRP monoclonal antibodies (fremanezumab, erenumab, galcanezumab) used for prophylaxis and CGRP receptor antagonists (ubrogepant, rimegepant) used for acute treatment.
- **C) CGRP is a parasympathetic neurotransmitter that causes pupillary constriction and lacrimation.** Incorrect. CGRP is not a parasympathetic neurotransmitter. Pupillary constriction is mediated by the parasympathetic pathway via CN III and acetylcholine at the ciliary ganglion. CGRP is a neuropeptide involved in pain signaling and neurogenic inflammation in the trigeminovascular system.
- **D) CGRP acts exclusively at the thalamic level to modulate cortical spreading depression.** Incorrect. CGRP acts primarily at the trigeminovascular level, not exclusively at the thalamus. While cortical spreading depression is one proposed mechanism in migraine pathogenesis, CGRP's primary role is in mediating neurogenic inflammation and pain transmission at the level of the trigeminal sensory afferents and meningeal blood vessels.
