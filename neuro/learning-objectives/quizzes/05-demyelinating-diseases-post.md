# CNS Demyelinating Diseases — Post-Quiz

### Q1. A 35-year-old woman presents with progressive numbness in her left hand that developed over the past 24 hours. On exam, she has decreased sensation to light touch and pinprick over the left forearm and hand. MRI of the brain shows a single ovoid white matter lesion adjacent to the left lateral ventricle. Biopsy of this lesion would most likely show which of the following?

- A) Loss of axons with secondary myelin degeneration
- B) Macrophages stripping myelin from relatively preserved axons
- C) Demyelination with loss of aquaporin-4 expressing astrocytes
- D) Diffuse cortical neuronal loss with reactive gliosis

### Q2. A 28-year-old woman presents with her second episode of right arm weakness over the past year. CSF analysis shows oligoclonal bands present in CSF but absent in serum. Which of the following best explains the origin of these oligoclonal bands?

- A) Leakage of serum immunoglobulins across a disrupted blood-brain barrier
- B) Antibody production by clonally expanded B cell populations within the CNS
- C) Degranulation of mast cells in the meninges releasing preformed IgE
- D) Passive transfer of maternal antibodies across the choroid plexus

### Q3. A 30-year-old woman is diagnosed with relapsing-remitting MS. She asks about her prognosis. Her neurologist explains that without treatment, approximately what percentage of RRMS patients historically progressed to secondary progressive MS (SPMS) within 20 years?

- A) 20%
- B) 40%
- C) 60%
- D) 80%

### Q4. A 25-year-old woman presents with 2 days of progressive painless vision loss in the right eye. On exam, the right pupil constricts less when light is shone directly into it compared to when light is shone in the left eye (swinging flashlight test positive). Left eye exam is normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Retinal detachment
- B) Optic neuritis
- C) Oculomotor nerve palsy
- D) Central retinal artery occlusion

### Q5. A 32-year-old man presents with bilateral lower extremity weakness, urinary retention, and a sensory level at T8. MRI of the spine shows a T2-hyperintense lesion spanning 1 vertebral body in the thoracic cord. Which additional finding would help meet the McDonald criteria for dissemination in space?

- A) A second spinal cord lesion at a different thoracic level
- B) A T2/FLAIR lesion in the periventricular white matter on brain MRI
- C) Elevated protein in the CSF
- D) Bilateral lower extremity hyperreflexia on exam

### Q6. A 40-year-old woman with known RRMS presents with a new episode of right-sided weakness and diplopia that began 36 hours ago. MRI shows several new gadolinium-enhancing brain lesions. What is the most appropriate acute treatment?

- A) Oral prednisone taper over 3 weeks
- B) IV methylprednisolone (Solumedrol) for 5 days
- C) Initiation of interferon beta injections
- D) Plasmapheresis as first-line therapy

### Q7. A 29-year-old woman with RRMS controlled on natalizumab for 3 years presents to discuss family planning. She and her partner want to conceive in the next 6 months. Which of the following statements about MS and pregnancy is most accurate?

- A) MS relapse risk increases during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters of pregnancy
- B) Teriflunomide is the preferred MS therapy during pregnancy due to its safety profile
- C) Relapse risk decreases during pregnancy but increases 3-4 months postpartum
- D) Women with MS should be counseled against pregnancy due to high risk of fetal complications

### Q8. A 38-year-old woman with a history of MS undergoes brain MRI for routine surveillance. The radiologist reports "multiple ovoid T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions oriented perpendicular to the lateral ventricles." Which of the following terms best describes this classic imaging pattern?

- A) Lhermitte phenomenon
- B) Dawson's fingers
- C) Ring enhancement
- D) Leptomeningeal enhancement

### Q9. A neurologist is deciding between two disease-modifying therapies for a patient with aggressive RRMS. She considers ocrelizumab. What is the primary mechanism of action of this drug?

- A) Sequestration of lymphocytes in lymph nodes via sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulation
- B) Blockade of alpha-4 integrins to prevent leukocyte migration into the CNS
- C) B-cell depletion via anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody activity
- D) Reduction of MHC class II expression on antigen-presenting cells

### Q10. A 42-year-old woman of Afro-Caribbean descent presents with severe bilateral vision loss and paraplegia. MRI shows longitudinally extensive T2-hyperintense lesions spanning 5 vertebral bodies in the spinal cord, and the brain MRI is largely unremarkable. Serum testing reveals aquaporin-4 IgG antibodies. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS)
- B) Primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS)
- C) Neuromyelitis optica (NMO)
- D) Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)

### Q11. A 6-year-old boy develops fever, headache, and vomiting. Three weeks later, he becomes increasingly irritable and lethargic, with bilateral weakness and ataxia. MRI of the brain shows diffuse, poorly demarcated, large T2-hyperintense lesions in the cerebral white matter and thalami bilaterally. Which of the following features best distinguishes this condition from multiple sclerosis?

- A) Presence of white matter lesions on MRI
- B) Monophasic course following a febrile illness with encephalopathy
- C) Response to IV corticosteroid therapy
- D) Perivascular lymphocytic inflammation on biopsy

### Q12. A neurologist is evaluating two patients in clinic. Patient A is a 32-year-old woman with MS who has unilateral optic neuritis with good recovery and periventricular white matter lesions with positive oligoclonal bands. Patient B is a 45-year-old woman with NMO who has severe bilateral optic neuritis with residual deficits and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis. Which of the following laboratory and imaging differences further supports the distinction between these two diagnoses?

- A) Patient A would have positive aquaporin-4 antibodies; Patient B would have positive oligoclonal bands
- B) Patient A would have spinal cord lesions spanning greater than 3 vertebral bodies; Patient B would have short-segment spinal cord lesions
- C) Patient A would have negative aquaporin-4 antibodies and positive oligoclonal bands; Patient B would have positive aquaporin-4 antibodies and typically absent oligoclonal bands
- D) Patient A would have a normal brain MRI; Patient B would have periventricular and juxtacortical lesions

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) Loss of axons with secondary myelin degeneration.** Incorrect. This describes secondary demyelination (Wallerian degeneration), which occurs when myelin is lost because the axon it surrounds has been destroyed by infarct, trauma, or degenerative processes. The clinical scenario and MRI findings are more consistent with a primary demyelinating disease such as MS.
- **B) Macrophages stripping myelin from relatively preserved axons.** Correct. This describes primary demyelination, the hallmark pathology of MS. In acute MS lesions, activated macrophages phagocytose myelin from axons that remain relatively intact. The periventricular ovoid white matter lesion in a young woman with subacute neurologic symptoms is a classic presentation. Additional findings would include perivascular lymphocytic inflammation and activated microglia.
- **C) Demyelination with loss of aquaporin-4 expressing astrocytes.** Incorrect. Loss of aquaporin-4 expressing astrocytes with complement deposition is the pathological hallmark of neuromyelitis optica (NMO), not MS. NMO is caused by antibodies directed against aquaporin-4 on astrocyte end-feet, and typically presents with severe optic neuritis or longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis rather than isolated periventricular brain lesions.
- **D) Diffuse cortical neuronal loss with reactive gliosis.** Incorrect. Diffuse cortical neuronal loss is characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Alzheimer's disease) rather than demyelinating diseases. MS primarily targets white matter myelin, not cortical neurons, though cortical lesions can occur.

### Q2. Answer: B
- **A) Leakage of serum immunoglobulins across a disrupted blood-brain barrier.** Incorrect. If oligoclonal bands were due to serum leakage, the same bands would appear in both CSF and serum. The defining feature of positive oligoclonal bands in MS is their presence in CSF but absence in serum, indicating intrathecal (within the CNS) antibody production.
- **B) Antibody production by clonally expanded B cell populations within the CNS.** Correct. Oligoclonal bands represent limited classes of immunoglobulins produced by clonally expanded B cells and plasma cells residing within the CNS. They are found in the CSF but not in serum, indicating intrathecal synthesis. They are present in 90-95% of MS patients and support the diagnosis.
- **C) Degranulation of mast cells in the meninges releasing preformed IgE.** Incorrect. Mast cell degranulation and IgE release are features of type I hypersensitivity (allergic) reactions. Oligoclonal bands in MS are IgG antibodies produced by B cells, not IgE from mast cells. This mechanism is not relevant to MS pathophysiology.
- **D) Passive transfer of maternal antibodies across the choroid plexus.** Incorrect. Passive transfer of maternal antibodies occurs transplacentally during fetal development, not through the choroid plexus in adults. Oligoclonal bands in MS reflect active, local immune responses by CNS-resident B cell clones, not passive antibody transfer.

### Q3. Answer: D
- **A) 20%.** Incorrect. 20% significantly underestimates the historical progression rate. This figure more closely reflects the conversion rate in treated patients on modern disease-modifying therapies (15-20% within approximately 20 years), not untreated patients.
- **B) 40%.** Incorrect. While substantial, 40% underestimates the historical natural history of untreated RRMS. Before the advent of effective disease-modifying therapies, the majority of RRMS patients eventually progressed to SPMS.
- **C) 60%.** Incorrect. Although closer, 60% still underestimates the historical progression rate. The historical data indicate a higher proportion of untreated RRMS patients progressed to SPMS within two decades.
- **D) 80%.** Correct. Historically, approximately 80% of untreated RRMS patients progressed to SPMS within 20 years. SPMS is characterized by continued functional decline between flares with fewer distinct relapses. This statistic underscores the importance of early initiation of disease-modifying therapy, which has reduced the 20-year conversion rate to approximately 15-20%.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) Retinal detachment.** Incorrect. Retinal detachment typically presents with floaters, flashes of light, and a curtain-like visual field loss. It does not produce a relative afferent pupillary defect (rAPD), which is a hallmark of optic nerve pathology, not retinal pathology (unless the detachment is extensive).
- **B) Optic neuritis.** Correct. This presentation is classic for optic neuritis: a young woman with subacute unilateral vision loss and a relative afferent pupillary defect (rAPD, positive swinging flashlight test). Optic neuritis is one of the most common presenting symptoms of MS. The rAPD indicates decreased afferent input from the affected optic nerve. Additional findings may include pain with eye movement, red desaturation, and optic nerve pallor.
- **C) Oculomotor nerve palsy.** Incorrect. An oculomotor (CN III) nerve palsy presents with ptosis, a "down and out" eye position, and potentially a dilated pupil. It does not cause the pattern of unilateral vision loss with an rAPD seen in this patient, as CN III is an efferent (motor) nerve, not an afferent (sensory) pathway.
- **D) Central retinal artery occlusion.** Incorrect. Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) causes sudden, painless monocular vision loss and is typically seen in older patients with vascular risk factors. Fundoscopic exam classically shows a pale retina with a cherry-red spot at the macula. The subacute onset over 2 days in a young woman is more consistent with optic neuritis.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) A second spinal cord lesion at a different thoracic level.** Incorrect. Two spinal cord lesions are both within the same CNS region (spinal cord). Dissemination in space requires lesions in at least 2 of 4 distinct regions: periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord. A second spinal cord lesion does not satisfy a second region.
- **B) A T2/FLAIR lesion in the periventricular white matter on brain MRI.** Correct. The patient already has evidence of spinal cord involvement (one of the four McDonald regions). A periventricular white matter lesion on brain MRI would satisfy a second region, thereby meeting the dissemination in space criterion. The four regions are: periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial (cerebellum/brainstem), and spinal cord.
- **C) Elevated protein in the CSF.** Incorrect. Elevated CSF protein is a nonspecific finding that can occur in many conditions (infection, inflammation, obstruction). It does not contribute to demonstrating dissemination in space per the McDonald criteria. Oligoclonal bands in the CSF are more specific for MS but address a different diagnostic criterion.
- **D) Bilateral lower extremity hyperreflexia on exam.** Incorrect. Hyperreflexia is an upper motor neuron sign that can be explained by the known thoracic spinal cord lesion itself. It does not represent involvement of a second distinct CNS region and therefore does not contribute to dissemination in space.

### Q6. Answer: B
- **A) Oral prednisone taper over 3 weeks.** Incorrect. Oral corticosteroids alone are not the standard of care for acute MS flares. High-dose IV methylprednisolone is preferred because it achieves higher CNS concentrations more rapidly and is more effective at reducing inflammation and hastening recovery from acute relapses.
- **B) IV methylprednisolone (Solumedrol) for 5 days.** Correct. IV methylprednisolone for 5 days is the standard acute treatment for MS relapses. It hastens recovery from the acute flare by reducing inflammation, though it does not modify the overall disease course. This patient has new gadolinium-enhancing lesions indicating active blood-brain barrier breakdown and acute inflammation.
- **C) Initiation of interferon beta injections.** Incorrect. Interferon beta is a disease-modifying therapy (DMARD) used for long-term prevention of relapses, not for treating an acute flare. DMARDs take weeks to months to exert their effects. This patient needs acute treatment for her current relapse; interferon beta may be considered as part of her chronic regimen.
- **D) Plasmapheresis as first-line therapy.** Incorrect. Plasmapheresis (plasma exchange) is a second-line treatment reserved for severe MS relapses that do not respond to IV corticosteroids. It is not used as first-line therapy. The standard initial approach is a 5-day course of IV methylprednisolone.

### Q7. Answer: C
- **A) MS relapse risk increases during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters of pregnancy.** Incorrect. The opposite is true. Disease activity in MS decreases during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters of pregnancy, likely due to hormonal and immunological changes that shift toward an anti-inflammatory state. The period of increased relapse risk is postpartum.
- **B) Teriflunomide is the preferred MS therapy during pregnancy due to its safety profile.** Incorrect. Teriflunomide is definitively teratogenic and is contraindicated in pregnancy. It requires an active washout procedure before conception can be attempted. No MS therapy has been definitively proven safe during pregnancy, though glatiramer acetate and beta interferons have not been shown to be harmful.
- **C) Relapse risk decreases during pregnancy but increases 3-4 months postpartum.** Correct. MS relapse risk decreases during pregnancy, particularly in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, due to hormonal and immune modulation. However, relapse risk increases significantly 3-4 months postpartum. Breastfeeding may lower postpartum relapse risk somewhat. Most women with well-controlled MS can safely discontinue medications for pregnancy with careful monitoring.
- **D) Women with MS should be counseled against pregnancy due to high risk of fetal complications.** Incorrect. MS itself does not preclude pregnancy. Most women with well-controlled MS can safely have children. The key is shared decision-making regarding medication management before, during, and after pregnancy. Discontinuation of certain medications may be needed, but pregnancy is not contraindicated.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Lhermitte phenomenon.** Incorrect. Lhermitte phenomenon (or sign) is a clinical symptom, not an imaging finding. It refers to an electric shock-like sensation radiating down the spine with neck flexion, caused by demyelination of the posterior columns of the cervical spinal cord.
- **B) Dawson's fingers.** Correct. Dawson's fingers is the term for the classic MRI appearance of MS lesions that are ovoid, T2/FLAIR hyperintense, and oriented perpendicular to the lateral ventricles. They reflect perivenular demyelination extending radially outward from the ventricles along small penetrating veins. This is a highly characteristic (though not pathognomonic) imaging sign of MS.
- **C) Ring enhancement.** Incorrect. Ring enhancement refers to a pattern of contrast enhancement where the periphery of a lesion enhances but the center does not. While some active MS lesions can show incomplete ring enhancement, the described pattern of ovoid lesions perpendicular to the ventricles specifically refers to Dawson's fingers.
- **D) Leptomeningeal enhancement.** Incorrect. Leptomeningeal enhancement refers to contrast enhancement of the pia and arachnoid mater and is more commonly associated with meningitis or leptomeningeal carcinomatosis. The described pattern of periventricular ovoid lesions is Dawson's fingers, not leptomeningeal disease.

### Q9. Answer: C
- **A) Sequestration of lymphocytes in lymph nodes via sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulation.** Incorrect. This describes the mechanism of fingolimod and other sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulators. These drugs prevent lymphocytes from exiting lymph nodes, thereby reducing the number of circulating lymphocytes available to enter the CNS.
- **B) Blockade of alpha-4 integrins to prevent leukocyte migration into the CNS.** Incorrect. This describes the mechanism of natalizumab, which blocks alpha-4 integrins on leukocytes. Alpha-4 integrins are required for leukocytes to adhere to and cross the blood-brain barrier endothelium. Natalizumab is a different monoclonal antibody than ocrelizumab.
- **C) B-cell depletion via anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody activity.** Correct. Ocrelizumab is a humanized anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that depletes B cells. CD20 is a surface marker expressed on pre-B cells and mature B cells. By depleting B cells, ocrelizumab reduces their roles as antigen-presenting cells, producers of proinflammatory cytokines, and sources of antibodies in MS pathogenesis. It is approved for both RRMS and PPMS.
- **D) Reduction of MHC class II expression on antigen-presenting cells.** Incorrect. This describes part of the mechanism of interferon beta, which decreases MHC class II expression and thereby reduces antigen presentation to T cells. Ocrelizumab works through an entirely different mechanism by targeting and depleting CD20-positive B cells.

### Q10. Answer: C
- **A) Relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS).** Incorrect. RRMS typically presents with unilateral optic neuritis (not bilateral) with good recovery, short-segment spinal cord lesions (less than 3 vertebral bodies), and characteristic periventricular brain lesions on MRI. This patient has bilateral vision loss, a longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesion spanning 5 vertebral bodies, a normal brain MRI, and positive aquaporin-4 antibodies -- all features that distinguish NMO from MS.
- **B) Primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS).** Incorrect. PPMS presents with progressive disability from onset without distinct relapses and does not feature aquaporin-4 antibodies. The acute, severe presentation with bilateral optic neuritis and extensive myelitis with positive AQP4-IgG is characteristic of NMO, not PPMS.
- **C) Neuromyelitis optica (NMO).** Correct. This is a classic NMO presentation: a woman of Afro-Caribbean descent (a population with higher NMO prevalence) with severe bilateral optic neuritis, longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (greater than 3 vertebral bodies), a largely normal brain MRI, and positive aquaporin-4 (AQP4) IgG antibodies. NMO is caused by antibodies to aquaporin-4 on astrocyte end-feet, causing complement-mediated damage. Treatment differs from MS and includes rituximab, eculizumab, and inebilizumab.
- **D) Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM).** Incorrect. ADEM is a monophasic, post-infectious demyelinating condition that primarily affects children. It typically presents with encephalopathy (altered mental status), multifocal neurologic deficits following a febrile illness, and diffuse poorly demarcated brain lesions on MRI. The absence of encephalopathy, the positive AQP4 antibodies, and the adult presentation make NMO the correct diagnosis.

### Q11. Answer: B
- **A) Presence of white matter lesions on MRI.** Incorrect. Both ADEM and MS show white matter lesions on MRI, so this finding alone does not distinguish them. The key difference is the pattern: ADEM shows diffuse, poorly demarcated, large lesions that can involve grey matter (thalamus, basal ganglia), while MS shows multiple discrete ovoid periventricular and juxtacortical lesions.
- **B) Monophasic course following a febrile illness with encephalopathy.** Correct. ADEM is an autoimmune monophasic demyelinating condition triggered by an antecedent infection (approximately 75% of cases), presenting 2-60 days after a febrile illness. The hallmark distinguishing feature from MS is encephalopathy (altered mental status beyond what fever alone would cause), including irritability, lethargy, and potentially coma. MS is a chronic relapsing disease that does not present with encephalopathy or follow a post-infectious monophasic course.
- **C) Response to IV corticosteroid therapy.** Incorrect. Both ADEM and MS acute flares are treated with corticosteroids. Response to steroids does not distinguish the two conditions. ADEM may additionally require IVIG or plasmapheresis if the patient deteriorates on steroids.
- **D) Perivascular lymphocytic inflammation on biopsy.** Incorrect. Both ADEM and MS show perivascular inflammation pathologically. While ADEM demonstrates small foci of perivascular demyelination and MS shows larger plaques, both share perivascular lymphocytic infiltration as a pathological feature. This alone would not reliably distinguish the two conditions.

### Q12. Answer: C
- **A) Patient A would have positive aquaporin-4 antibodies; Patient B would have positive oligoclonal bands.** Incorrect. This reverses the expected findings. MS (Patient A) is typically AQP4-IgG negative and oligoclonal band positive. NMO (Patient B) is AQP4-IgG positive and typically oligoclonal band negative (or absent). Aquaporin-4 antibodies are the defining serological marker of NMO, not MS.
- **B) Patient A would have spinal cord lesions spanning greater than 3 vertebral bodies; Patient B would have short-segment spinal cord lesions.** Incorrect. This reverses the expected imaging findings. MS spinal cord lesions are typically short (less than 3 vertebral bodies, often 1 vertebral body or less). NMO characteristically produces longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis spanning greater than 3 vertebral bodies.
- **C) Patient A would have negative aquaporin-4 antibodies and positive oligoclonal bands; Patient B would have positive aquaporin-4 antibodies and typically absent oligoclonal bands.** Correct. This accurately captures the key laboratory distinctions. MS is characterized by positive CSF oligoclonal bands (90-95% of cases) and negative AQP4-IgG. NMO is a definitive autoimmune disease driven by antibodies to aquaporin-4 on astrocyte end-feet (positive AQP4-IgG) and typically has absent oligoclonal bands. This distinction has critical treatment implications, as some MS DMARDs can worsen NMO.
- **D) Patient A would have a normal brain MRI; Patient B would have periventricular and juxtacortical lesions.** Incorrect. This reverses the expected imaging findings. MS (Patient A) characteristically shows periventricular and juxtacortical white matter lesions on brain MRI, including Dawson's fingers. NMO (Patient B) often has a normal or near-normal brain MRI, though lesions can occur in specific locations such as the area postrema, thalami, and brainstem.
