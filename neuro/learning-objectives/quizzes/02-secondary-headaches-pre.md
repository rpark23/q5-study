### Q1. Which of the following best describes a secondary headache?

- A) A headache that occurs after an initial primary headache resolves
- B) A headache caused by an underlying structural or pathological process
- C) A headache that is less severe than a primary headache
- D) A headache that does not respond to over-the-counter analgesics

### Q2. In the SNOOP mnemonic for headache red flags, what does the "O" for Onset specifically refer to?

- A) Headaches that occur only in the occipital region
- B) Gradual onset headache worsening over several weeks
- C) First or worst headache with sudden ("thunderclap") onset

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B
- **A) A headache that occurs after an initial primary headache resolves.** Incorrect. The term "secondary" does not refer to temporal sequence. Secondary headaches are defined by their etiology, not by when they occur relative to another headache.
- **B) A headache caused by an underlying structural or pathological process.** Correct. Secondary headaches (approximately 10% of all headaches) are attributable to an identifiable underlying cause such as tumor, infection, hemorrhage, or vascular malformation. This distinguishes them from primary headaches where no structural cause exists.
- **C) A headache that is less severe than a primary headache.** Incorrect. Severity does not distinguish primary from secondary headaches. Many primary headaches (e.g., cluster headache) are excruciating, while some secondary headaches can be mild.
- **D) A headache that does not respond to over-the-counter analgesics.** Incorrect. Treatment response does not define the classification. Both primary and secondary headaches may or may not respond to analgesics.

### Q2. Answer: C
- **A) Headaches that occur only in the occipital region.** Incorrect. The "O" in SNOOP does not refer to headache location. Location alone is not a red flag feature in this mnemonic.
- **B) Gradual onset headache worsening over several weeks.** Incorrect. This progressive pattern would fall more under "P" (prior headache history is different with worsening pattern). The "O" for Onset specifically emphasizes sudden onset.
- **C) First or worst headache with sudden ("thunderclap") onset.** Correct. The first "O" in SNOOP stands for Onset and refers to a first or worst headache, particularly a thunderclap headache that reaches maximum intensity within seconds to minutes. This pattern suggests SAH until proven otherwise.
- **D) Onset of headache after consumption of alcohol.** Incorrect. While substance-related headaches are a category of secondary headache, the SNOOP "O" for Onset specifically highlights sudden, severe onset rather than substance triggers.

### Q3. Answer: C
- **A) Unilateral miosis with ptosis.** Incorrect. Unilateral miosis with ptosis describes Horner syndrome, which results from disruption of the sympathetic chain, not from increased ICP.
- **B) Headache that improves when lying flat.** Incorrect. Increased ICP causes headaches that worsen with lying down, bending over, and Valsalva maneuvers. Headache that improves with lying flat is more suggestive of intracranial hypotension (e.g., post-LP headache).
- **C) Bilateral CN VI palsies with papilledema.** Correct. Increased ICP classically produces papilledema (optic disc swelling from transmitted pressure) and CN VI palsies because the abducens nerve has the longest intracranial course and is most vulnerable to stretch injury from elevated pressure.
- **D) Ipsilateral facial droop with forehead sparing.** Incorrect. Contralateral lower face weakness with forehead sparing is characteristic of an upper motor neuron lesion of CN VII, not a sign of increased ICP.

### Q4. Answer: B
- **A) It exits the skull through the narrowest foramen.** Incorrect. CN VI exits through Dorello's canal, but this is not the primary reason it is susceptible to ICP-related injury. The key factor is the length of its intracranial trajectory.
- **B) It has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve.** Correct. CN VI (abducens nerve) has the longest intracranial course from its origin at the pontomedullary junction to the lateral rectus muscle. This extended path makes it most susceptible to stretch injury when intracranial pressure rises, producing horizontal diplopia.
- **C) It is the only unmyelinated cranial nerve.** Incorrect. This is not an accurate anatomical fact. The susceptibility of CN VI to injury from increased ICP is due to its long intracranial course, not its myelination status.
- **D) It passes through the cavernous sinus bilaterally.** Incorrect. While CN VI does pass through the cavernous sinus, all cranial nerves pass through only on their respective sides. The distinguishing feature is its long intracranial course, not its cavernous sinus passage.

### Q5. Answer: B
- **A) Electroencephalogram (EEG).** Incorrect. While EEG may be useful in evaluating seizures, it is not a required study before LP. The critical concern before LP is ruling out mass lesions that could lead to brain herniation.
- **B) Neuroimaging (CT or MRI).** Correct. Patients with altered mental status, recent seizure, focal neurologic deficits (including papilledema), or immunocompromised state must undergo neuroimaging before LP. This is to rule out mass lesions or elevated ICP that could cause fatal brain herniation if CSF is removed.
- **C) Complete blood count with differential.** Incorrect. While checking platelet count and coagulation status is important (platelets < 50,000 or INR > 1.4 are contraindications), the specific requirement for a patient with altered mental status is neuroimaging, not CBC.
- **D) Ophthalmology consultation for funduscopic exam.** Incorrect. While detecting papilledema is valuable, a formal ophthalmology consultation is not required before LP. Neuroimaging is the mandatory prerequisite in patients with altered mental status.

### Q6. Answer: C
- **A) Elevated protein level.** Incorrect. Elevated CSF protein is a nonspecific finding that can occur in many conditions including infection, inflammation, and Guillain-Barre syndrome. It is not the hallmark finding for SAH on LP.
- **B) Lymphocytic pleocytosis.** Incorrect. Lymphocytic pleocytosis suggests viral meningitis or other inflammatory conditions, not subarachnoid hemorrhage.
- **C) Xanthochromia.** Correct. Xanthochromia is the yellow discoloration of CSF caused by breakdown of hemoglobin from red blood cells in the subarachnoid space. It is the diagnostic finding for SAH on LP and distinguishes true SAH from a traumatic tap. It may take up to 2 hours after symptom onset to develop.
- **D) Decreased glucose level.** Incorrect. Low CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia) is characteristic of bacterial meningitis, tuberculosis, or fungal infection, not SAH.

### Q7. Answer: B
- **A) Placing a clip across the neck of the aneurysm to exclude it from circulation.** Incorrect. This describes surgical clipping, which is an open neurosurgical procedure, not endovascular coiling. Clipping requires a craniotomy and direct visualization.
- **B) Inserting metal coils into the aneurysm sac via catheter to promote thrombosis.** Correct. Endovascular coiling is performed by Neurointerventional Radiology. A catheter is advanced into the aneurysm, and metal coils are deployed within the sac to promote thrombosis and exclude the aneurysm from circulation. Stenting may be used as an adjunct.
- **C) Injecting a sclerosing agent into the vessel wall to shrink the aneurysm.** Incorrect. Sclerotherapy is used in peripheral vascular procedures (e.g., varicose veins), not in the treatment of cerebral aneurysms.
- **D) Bypassing the aneurysm with a vein graft to divert blood flow.** Incorrect. Vein graft bypass is not the standard endovascular coiling technique. While bypass procedures exist in cerebrovascular surgery, coiling works by filling the aneurysm sac with thrombogenic material.

### Q8. Answer: B
- **A) Constant dull aching pain across the entire face bilaterally.** Incorrect. Trigeminal neuralgia is characterized by brief, paroxysmal (not constant) pain that is unilateral (not bilateral). Constant bilateral facial pain would suggest a different diagnosis.
- **B) Brief lancinating pain triggered by non-noxious stimuli in CN V distribution.** Correct. Trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux) produces recurrent, unilateral, brief electric shock-like pains in the distribution of CN V, most often V2 and V3. These episodes are triggered by normally innocuous stimuli such as eating, cold air, touch, or wind.
- **C) Throbbing pain with photophobia and phonophobia lasting 4-72 hours.** Incorrect. This describes a migraine headache, which is a primary headache disorder. Migraines are longer in duration and have different associated features than trigeminal neuralgia.
- **D) Deep boring periorbital pain with ipsilateral tearing and rhinorrhea.** Incorrect. This describes a cluster headache, another primary headache disorder characterized by severe unilateral periorbital pain with autonomic features (lacrimation, rhinorrhea, conjunctival injection).

### Q9. Answer: B
- **A) External carotid artery bifurcation.** Incorrect. The external carotid artery is not part of the Circle of Willis. The Circle of Willis is formed by the internal carotid arteries and the vertebrobasilar system at the base of the brain.
- **B) Anterior communicating artery junction.** Correct. The anterior communicating artery is one of the most common sites for berry aneurysms. Other common Circle of Willis aneurysm locations include the posterior communicating artery, MCA bifurcation, basilar tip, and ophthalmic artery origin.
- **C) Vertebral artery origin at the subclavian artery.** Incorrect. The origin of the vertebral artery from the subclavian artery is extracranial and is not part of the Circle of Willis. While vertebral artery pathology exists, this is not a typical aneurysm location within the circle.
- **D) Superior sagittal sinus confluence.** Incorrect. The superior sagittal sinus is a venous structure, not an arterial one. The Circle of Willis is an arterial anastomotic ring, and aneurysms form at arterial branch points.

### Q10. Answer: B
- **A) MRI brain with gadolinium contrast.** Incorrect. While MRI provides superior anatomical detail for many conditions, it is not the first-line study for acute thunderclap headache. MRI is more time-consuming and is preferred for subacute or chronic presentations. In the acute setting, speed is critical.
- **B) Non-contrast CT head.** Correct. Non-contrast CT head is the first-line imaging study for acute thunderclap headache because it rapidly detects acute blood (which appears hyperdense/bright). CT detects SAH in 92% of cases within 24 hours of symptom onset. If negative but suspicion remains high, LP should follow.
- **C) MR angiography of the head and neck.** Incorrect. MRA is used to identify vascular abnormalities (aneurysms, dissection) but is not the initial screening study for acute headache. It may be obtained after SAH is confirmed to identify the source of bleeding.
- **D) Cerebral catheter angiography.** Incorrect. Conventional catheter angiography is the gold standard for vascular anatomy but is invasive and not appropriate as a first-line screening tool. It is reserved for cases where noninvasive imaging is inconclusive or when endovascular treatment is planned.
