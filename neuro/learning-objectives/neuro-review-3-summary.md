## 1. Altered Mental Status & Coma

### Definitions
- **Consciousness** has two components: **arousal** (are you awake?) and **awareness** (do you know what's going on around you?). AMS exists on a spectrum from mild confusion to deep coma.
- **Coma** = absence of BOTH arousal AND awareness. The patient cannot be woken up and has no purposeful interaction with the environment.
- **GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale)** = E4 + V5 + M6 (max 15). A standardized way to score level of consciousness:
  - **E (Eye opening):** 4 = spontaneous, 3 = to voice, 2 = to pain, 1 = none
  - **V (Verbal):** 5 = oriented, 4 = confused, 3 = inappropriate words, 2 = incomprehensible sounds, 1 = none
  - **M (Motor):** 6 = obeys commands, 5 = localizes pain, 4 = withdraws, 3 = flexor posturing, 2 = extensor posturing, 1 = none

### Simon's Rule of 4

**Four things to do first:**
1. **ABCs, vitals** - secure the airway, check breathing and circulation
2. **Give thiamine FIRST, then D50 (dextrose), then naloxone**
   - Why thiamine first? Glucose without thiamine can precipitate or worsen **Wernicke encephalopathy** (thiamine is a cofactor for glucose metabolism in the brain). If you give glucose to a thiamine-depleted patient, you burn through the last of their thiamine.
   - D50 treats hypoglycemia (a common reversible cause of AMS)
   - Naloxone reverses opioid overdose
3. **Blood & urine tests** - look for metabolic causes (glucose, electrolytes, toxicology, etc.)

**Four things to examine:**
1. **Pupils** - absent pupillary reflex means the brainstem is not working. A fixed dilated pupil suggests CN III compression (e.g., uncal herniation). Pinpoint pupils suggest opioid overdose or pontine lesion.
2. **Doll's eyes (oculocephalic reflex) / cold calorics (vestibulo-ocular reflex)** - these test brainstem pathways connecting CN VIII to CN III/VI. If eyes don't move appropriately, the brainstem is damaged. Normal doll's eyes = eyes move opposite to head turning (like a doll's eyes staying fixed on you). In a comatose patient, absent doll's eyes = brainstem dysfunction.
3. **Response to pain:**
   - **Flexor posturing (decorticate)** = arms flex in, legs extend. Means the lesion is ABOVE the midbrain (cortex disconnected from brainstem, but brainstem still partially works). Think: "COR" = arms toward the CORE.
   - **Extensor posturing (decerebrate)** = arms and legs both extend. Means the lesion is at the level of the pons/medulla (brainstem itself is damaged). This is worse than flexor posturing.
4. **Meningeal signs** - nuchal rigidity, Kernig's sign, Brudzinski's sign → think meningitis or subarachnoid hemorrhage

### Two Localizations of Coma
Only two places in the brain where damage causes coma:
1. **ARAS (ascending reticular activating system)** - a network in the upper brainstem (midbrain/pons) that keeps you awake. A small focal lesion here can cause coma because this is the "on switch" for consciousness.
2. **Diffuse bilateral cerebral hemispheres / bilateral thalamus** - you need BOTH hemispheres to be diffusely damaged (or both thalami) to lose consciousness from cortical disease. A unilateral hemisphere lesion alone will NOT cause coma (the other side can maintain arousal).

### Three Etiologies of Coma
1. **Non-structural (metabolic)** - there is no physical lesion on imaging. The brain is globally dysfunctional due to a metabolic cause (e.g., EtOH intoxication, drug overdose, hepatic encephalopathy, uremia, hypoglycemia). Key clue: **cranial nerves are intact and there is no motor asymmetry** (because there's no focal lesion).
2. **Structural** - there IS a physical lesion (e.g., stroke, hemorrhage, tumor, abscess). Expect focal neurologic findings like asymmetric pupils, hemiparesis, or gaze deviation.
3. **Electrical** - seizure or status epilepticus. The brain is electrically "short-circuiting." Post-ictal state can also look like coma.

### Coma Mimics
These conditions can look like coma but are NOT true coma. You must distinguish them:

| Mimic | What's happening | Key distinguishing feature |
|-------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **Persistent vegetative state (PVS)** | Diffuse cortical damage destroyed awareness, but the brainstem is intact so arousal is preserved. Patient opens eyes, has sleep/wake cycles, may turn head or move limbs reflexively, but has NO purposeful interaction with the environment. | Eyes open + sleep/wake cycles but no awareness. Can persist for years. |
| **Locked-in state** | Patient is FULLY CONSCIOUS and can feel everything, but is completely paralyzed due to a bilateral pontine lesion. The pons carries all motor output from brain to body. The midbrain is spared, so vertical eye movements still work. | Can communicate with vertical eye movements or blinks. Terrifying condition - the mind is trapped in a paralyzed body. |
| **Complete peripheral nerve paralysis** | Brain is completely normal but all peripheral nerves are paralyzed (e.g., severe GBS). Patient appears comatose but is fully aware. | Peripheral cause; brain imaging and EEG normal. |
| **Post-ictal state** | After a seizure, the brain needs time to "reboot." Patient is transiently unresponsive. | History of seizure; recovers over minutes to hours. |
| **Abulia / akinetic mutism** | Large frontal lobe lesion causes extreme loss of motivation/initiative. Patient can be awake but barely moves or speaks - not because they can't, but because they lack the drive to. | Frontal lobe pathology; may respond with minimal effort when strongly stimulated. |
| **Psychogenic unresponsiveness** | Psychiatric cause (e.g., catatonia, conversion disorder). No structural brain problem. | Normal neurologic exam when tested carefully; EEG shows normal waking activity. |
| **Minimally conscious state** | A step above PVS - patient shows reproducible evidence of awareness (e.g., tracks objects with eyes, reaches for things) but cannot reliably communicate. | Inconsistent but reproducible purposeful behaviors. |
| **Brain death** | Irreversible loss of ALL brain function - cortex, subcortex, AND brainstem. No pupillary reflexes, no corneal reflexes, no respiratory drive. This is legal death. | Must confirm with apnea test and/or ancillary tests (EEG showing no activity, cerebral blood flow study showing no flow). |

## 2. Pediatric Neurology: Neurocutaneous Disorders

These are genetic disorders that affect both the nervous system and the skin (neuro + cutaneous). The skin findings are visible clues to underlying neurologic disease.

| Disorder | Genetics | Skin Findings | Neuro Findings | Other |
|----------|---------|---------------|---------------|-------|
| **TSC** (Tuberous Sclerosis Complex) | AD, TSC1/TSC2 genes (tumor suppressors) | **Ash-leaf spots** (hypopigmented macules, seen with Wood's lamp), **shagreen patch** (leathery skin plaque on lower back), facial angiofibromas | **Cortical tubers** (malformed brain tissue → seizures), **subependymal giant cell astrocytomas** (SEGA) | Renal angiomyolipomas, cardiac rhabdomyomas |
| **Sturge-Weber** | Sporadic (somatic GNAQ mutation) | **Port-wine birthmark** (facial, V1 distribution - NOT a "port wine birthmark" = NOT TSC) | **Leptomeningeal angioma** (abnormal blood vessels on brain surface → seizures), intellectual disability | **Glaucoma** (ipsilateral) |
| **NF1** | AD, NF1 gene (chr 17) | **Cafe-au-lait spots** (>6), **axillary/inguinal freckling** (Crowe sign) | **Neurofibromas** (benign nerve tumors), **optic gliomas**, learning disabilities | **Lisch nodules** (iris hamartomas - pathognomonic) |
| **NF2** | AD, NF2 gene (chr 22) | Fewer skin findings than NF1 | **Bilateral vestibular schwannomas** (acoustic neuromas) - this is the defining feature | Meningiomas, ependymomas |

Key distinction: NF1 = cafe-au-lait spots + neurofibromas; NF2 = **bilateral** acoustic neuromas (unilateral would not be NF2).

## 3. Movement Disorders

### Pyramidal vs Extrapyramidal - The Big Picture

The motor system has two parallel systems that work together:

**Pyramidal system (voluntary motor)** = the direct pathway from motor cortex → corticospinal tract → spinal cord → muscle. This is the system you use when you consciously decide to move. When it's damaged, you get **weakness** (the signal can't get through) plus **upper motor neuron signs** (hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski) because the inhibitory modulation from cortex is lost.

**Extrapyramidal system (involuntary motor modulation)** = the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem nuclei that fine-tune and modulate movement. This system doesn't generate movement itself - it adjusts the quality, speed, and smoothness of movement. When it's damaged, you DON'T get weakness (the pyramidal system still works), but you get **abnormal movements** - either too much movement (chorea, tremor, ballism) or too little movement (bradykinesia, rigidity).

| Feature | Pyramidal | Extrapyramidal |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| What it controls | Voluntary movement execution | Movement modulation/fine-tuning |
| Damage causes | Weakness or paralysis | Abnormal involuntary movements OR slowness |
| Strength on exam | Reduced | Usually preserved |
| Reflexes | Hyperreflexia, Babinski | Usually normal |
| Tone | Spasticity (velocity-dependent) | Rigidity (velocity-independent) |
| Anatomy | Motor cortex → corticospinal/corticobulbar tracts | Basal ganglia, cerebellum, brainstem nuclei |

### Two Types of Increased Tone

This distinction comes up frequently:
- **Spasticity** (pyramidal) = velocity-DEPENDENT stiffness. The faster you try to move the limb, the more resistance you feel. You may feel a sudden "catch" (like a clasp knife). This is because the pyramidal tract normally inhibits spinal reflexes; when it's damaged, reflexes are overactive.
- **Lead-pipe rigidity** (extrapyramidal) = velocity-INDEPENDENT stiffness. The resistance is constant no matter how fast or slow you move the limb. It feels like bending a lead pipe. This is a basal ganglia problem.
- **Cogwheel rigidity** = lead-pipe rigidity + superimposed tremor. You feel a ratchety, stop-and-go resistance. Classic for Parkinson's disease.

### Key Buzzwords and Their Localizations

| Term | What it looks like | Where the lesion is | Classic disease |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Chorea** | Flowing, random, dance-like movements that seem to move from one body part to another | Caudate nucleus (basal ganglia) | **Huntington's disease** |
| **Hemiballismus** | Fast, violent, large-amplitude flinging movements of one side of the body | **Subthalamic nucleus** (contralateral) | Often from a small stroke in the STN |
| **Athetosis** | Slow, sinuous, writhing movements, especially of fingers and hands | Basal ganglia | Often seen with chorea (choreoathetosis) |
| **Dystonia** | Sustained muscle contractions causing twisting, repetitive movements or abnormal postures | Basal ganglia | Cervical dystonia (torticollis), writer's cramp |
| **Ataxia** | Incoordination - clumsy, unsteady movements and gait | **Cerebellum** | Alcohol, stroke, MS |
| **Dysmetria** | Overshooting or undershooting a target (finger-to-nose test) | Cerebellum | Cerebellar stroke/tumor |
| **Dysdiadochokinesia** | Inability to perform rapid alternating movements smoothly | Cerebellum | — |
| **Truncal titubation** | Rhythmic swaying of the trunk | Midline (vermis) cerebellum | — |

### High-Yield Movement Disorders

**Essential Tremor:**
- The most common movement disorder overall
- An **action/kinetic tremor** - occurs during movement (e.g., drinking from a cup, writing) and with sustained posture (holding arms outstretched). Absent at rest.
- Often bilateral, involves hands > head > voice
- May improve with alcohol (classic history: "my tremor gets better when I drink")
- Treatment: **propranolol** (beta-blocker, best evidence) or **primidone** (anticonvulsant)
- NOT Parkinson's (which is a resting tremor)

**Cerebellar Disease:**
- Think of the cerebellum as the "quality control center" for movement. It ensures movements are smooth, coordinated, and accurate.
- When damaged, everything becomes clumsy: **dysmetria** (can't point accurately), **dysdiadochokinesia** (can't do rapid alternating movements), **ataxia** (unsteady gait), **dysarthria** (slurred, scanning speech), **truncal titubation** (trunk sways), **intention tremor** (gets worse as you approach the target)
- Common causes: alcohol (chronic cerebellar degeneration), stroke, MS, tumor

**Huntington's Disease:**
- **Autosomal dominant**, caused by **CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion** on **chromosome 4** (huntingtin gene)
- Normal: <36 repeats; Disease: >40 repeats. More repeats = earlier onset (anticipation)
- Classic triad: **chorea** (involuntary dance-like movements) + **neuropsychiatric symptoms** (depression, irritability, personality change - often precede motor symptoms) + **cognitive decline** (progresses to dementia)
- Caudate nucleus atrophy visible on imaging
- Fatal, typically within 15-20 years of onset. No disease-modifying treatment.

### Tremor Types - How to Tell Them Apart

| Tremor | When it happens | What it looks like | Disease | How to test |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|---------|------------|
| **Resting** | At rest; IMPROVES with intentional movement | "Pill rolling" - thumb rolls over fingers rhythmically | **Parkinson's disease** | Have patient rest hands in lap; tremor appears. Ask them to reach for something; tremor diminishes. |
| **Kinetic/Postural** | During movement or while holding a posture | Shaking hands while writing, drinking, or holding arms outstretched | **Essential tremor** | Ask patient to write, pour water, or hold arms out in front. |
| **Intention** | Worsens as limb APPROACHES the target | Increasing oscillation during finger-to-nose test | **Cerebellar disease** | Finger-to-nose test: tremor absent at start, maximal at the nose. |
| **Dystonic** | In a body part that is also in a dystonic posture | Tremor in a twisted/contracted limb or neck | **Dystonia** | Tremor present in the affected dystonic limb. |

All tremors are **extrapyramidal signs** - they arise from dysfunction in the basal ganglia or cerebellum, not the pyramidal (corticospinal) tract.

## 4. Neurodegenerative Disease & Dementia

### MCI vs Dementia - The Key Distinction

Both MCI and dementia represent cognitive decline beyond normal aging. The critical dividing line is **functional independence:**
- **MCI (Mild Cognitive Impairment):** patient has noticeable cognitive difficulties (e.g., forgetting appointments, slower thinking), BUT they can still **live independently** - they manage their finances, medications, cooking, driving, etc., even if it's harder than before.
- **Dementia:** cognitive decline has progressed to the point where the patient **cannot independently perform instrumental activities of daily living (iADLs)** - managing finances, driving, cooking, shopping, medications. Eventually they lose basic ADLs too (bathing, dressing, eating).
- MCI can progress to dementia, but not always. It's a risk state, not a guaranteed trajectory.
- Neither term specifies an etiology - both can be caused by Alzheimer's, vascular disease, etc.

### Cognitive Domain Localization

Each type of cognitive function maps to a specific brain region. Knowing this helps you predict which dementia a patient has based on their symptoms:

| Domain | What it means | Localization | If impaired, think... |
|--------|-------------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Episodic memory** | Ability to form and recall new memories of events/experiences (short-term memory) | **Hippocampus** (medial temporal lobe) | Alzheimer's disease (earliest symptom) |
| **Language production** | Ability to generate fluent speech and write | **Left frontal lobe (Broca's area)** | Broca's aphasia: understands but can't speak fluently ("broken Broca's") |
| **Language comprehension** | Ability to understand spoken and written language | **Left temporal lobe (Wernicke's area)** | Wernicke's aphasia: speaks fluently but makes no sense ("Wernicke's is wordy but wrong") |
| **Visuospatial** | Ability to perceive spatial relationships, navigate, recognize faces, copy drawings | **Right > left parietal lobes** (with occipital contributions) | Lewy body dementia; also right parietal strokes |
| **Executive function** | Planning, reasoning, sequencing, self-control, judgment, abstract thinking | **Frontal lobes** | Frontotemporal dementia (behavioral variant) |

### Three Major Dementias - Compared

| Feature | Alzheimer's Disease (AD) | Lewy Body Dementia (LBD) | Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) |
|---------|---|---|---|
| **Underlying pathology** | **Tau tangles** (intracellular) + **amyloid-beta plaques** (extracellular) | **Alpha-synuclein** aggregates forming **Lewy bodies** inside neurons | **Tau** or **TDP-43** protein accumulations |
| **Primary cognitive domain** | **Short-term memory** (hippocampal) | **Visuospatial** (parietal) | **Executive function** (frontal) |
| **Hallmark symptoms** | Repetitive questions, getting lost, forgetting recent events. Memory loss is the earliest and most prominent feature. | The "4 F's": **Fluctuations** (good days/bad days, waxing/waning alertness), **Visual hallucinations** (vivid, detailed - e.g., seeing people/animals), **REM sleep behavior disorder** (acting out dreams), **Parkinsonism** (rigidity, bradykinesia) | **Behavioral disinhibition** (saying/doing inappropriate things), apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviors. Personality changes often noticed by family before cognitive testing is abnormal. |
| **Imaging** | **Hippocampal atrophy** on MRI | Often normal early on | **Frontal and/or temporal lobe atrophy** |
| **Treatment** | **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) - boost acetylcholine; **Memantine** (NMDA receptor antagonist) - reduces glutamate excitotoxicity; +/- **aducanumab/lecanemab** (anti-amyloid antibodies - controversial, modest benefit) | Cholinesterase inhibitors may help; AVOID antipsychotics (severe sensitivity in LBD) | No specific treatment |
| **Reversible causes to rule out** | Always check **vitamin B12** and **thyroid function** (TSH) in any patient presenting with cognitive decline - these are treatable! | — | **C9orf72** genetic testing if family history of ALS + dementia |

### Prion Disease (Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease)
- Caused by **misfolded prion proteins** (PrPSc) that cause normal proteins to misfold in a chain reaction, leading to rapid neuronal death
- What makes it unique: **rapidly progressive dementia** - decline over **weeks to months** (most other dementias progress over years)
- **Myoclonus** (sudden involuntary muscle jerks) is very characteristic
- MRI shows **cortical ribboning** (bright signal along the cortex on DWI/FLAIR - the cortex looks like it's "lighting up")
- CSF markers: **14-3-3 protein** (released from dying neurons), **RT-QuIC** (real-time quaking-induced conversion - highly sensitive and specific test for prion protein)
- Always fatal, usually within 6-12 months of symptom onset
- No treatment

## 5. Parkinson's Disease

### Cardinal Features: **TRAP**

Parkinson's disease is the classic example of an extrapyramidal (basal ganglia) disorder. The mnemonic is **TRAP**:

- **T = Tremor** - specifically a **resting tremor** that looks like "pill rolling" (thumb and index finger rubbing together). The tremor goes AWAY when the patient reaches for something (this is the opposite of essential tremor). Often starts unilaterally.
- **R = Rigidity** - **lead-pipe rigidity** (constant resistance to passive movement, velocity-independent). When tremor is superimposed, it becomes **cogwheel rigidity** (ratchety, stop-and-go resistance).
- **A = Akinesia/Bradykinesia** - slowness of movement. The patient has trouble initiating movements, movements are small and slow. You'll see small handwriting (micrographia), shuffling gait with small steps, reduced arm swing, masked facies (reduced facial expression), soft voice (hypophonia).
- **P = Postural instability** - impaired balance reflexes. The patient tends to fall, especially backward. Tested with the "pull test" (pull patient backward by shoulders and see if they can recover their balance). This is typically a later feature.

### Parkinsonism vs Parkinson's Disease
- **Parkinsonism** = the clinical syndrome - any combination of TRAP features from ANY cause. Many things can cause parkinsonism:
  - Drugs (antipsychotics, metoclopramide - they block dopamine receptors)
  - Vascular parkinsonism (multiple small strokes in basal ganglia)
  - Parkinson-plus syndromes (MSA, PSP, CBD, LBD)
  - Toxins (MPTP, manganese)
- **Parkinson's disease (PD)** = the specific idiopathic neurodegenerative disease caused by:
  - **Loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta** (the dopamine factory in the midbrain)
  - **Lewy bodies** (alpha-synuclein protein aggregates) found in surviving neurons
  - Since the substantia nigra normally sends dopamine to the striatum (caudate + putamen) via the nigrostriatal pathway, loss of these neurons → dopamine depletion in the basal ganglia → impaired movement modulation

### Non-Motor Symptoms (important!)
PD is not just a movement disorder. Non-motor symptoms can precede motor symptoms by years:
- **Anosmia** (loss of smell) - often the earliest symptom
- **Constipation** (GI dysmotility)
- **REM sleep behavior disorder** (acting out dreams)
- **Depression and anxiety**
- **Cognitive decline** (can progress to PD dementia in later stages)
- **Autonomic dysfunction** (orthostatic hypotension)

### Treatment

All PD treatments are **symptomatic** - they replace or augment dopamine signaling but do NOT slow or stop neurodegeneration. The underlying disease keeps progressing.

| Drug Class | Examples | How it works | Key Side Effects | Notes |
|------------|---------|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Levodopa** | Carbidopa-levodopa (Sinemet) | Levodopa is the precursor to dopamine. It crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the brain. Carbidopa prevents conversion outside the brain (reduces nausea). | **Dyskinesias** with chronic use (involuntary writhing movements - too much dopamine). Also "wearing off" (effect doesn't last as long) and "on-off" fluctuations. | Most effective PD drug. Mainstay of treatment. |
| **DA agonists** | Ropinirole, pramipexole | Directly stimulate dopamine receptors (bypass the need for dopamine production) | **Psychiatric:** impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping), hallucinations, mood changes | Sometimes used first in younger patients to delay levodopa dyskinesias |
| **MAO-B inhibitors** | Selegiline, rasagiline (names end in "-galine") | Block monoamine oxidase B, the enzyme that breaks down dopamine in the brain → more dopamine available | Generally well-tolerated | Mild benefit; sometimes used as early monotherapy or adjunct |
| **COMT inhibitors** | Entacapone, tolcapone (names end in "-capone") | Block catechol-O-methyltransferase, another enzyme that degrades dopamine and levodopa → prolongs levodopa's effect | **Tolcapone: liver toxicity** (requires LFT monitoring). Entacapone is safer. | Always used WITH levodopa (extends its duration) |

### Surgical Treatment
- **Deep brain stimulation (DBS):** electrodes implanted in the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)** or **globus pallidus internus (GPi)**. High-frequency electrical stimulation modulates the overactive inhibitory output from these nuclei. Helps with tremor, rigidity, dyskinesias, and motor fluctuations. Reserved for patients with good levodopa response but troublesome fluctuations/dyskinesias.
- **Pallidotomy** (surgical destruction of part of GPi) - rarely performed now that DBS is available.

### Why Does DBS Work? (Basal Ganglia Circuitry)
In PD, loss of dopamine in the striatum leads to overactivity of the indirect pathway → excessive inhibition of the thalamus → reduced excitatory input to the motor cortex → bradykinesia/akinesia. DBS of the STN or GPi disrupts this overactive inhibitory output, "releasing" the thalamus to excite the cortex again.
