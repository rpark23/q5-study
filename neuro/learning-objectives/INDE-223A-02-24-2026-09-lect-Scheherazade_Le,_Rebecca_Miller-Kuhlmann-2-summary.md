## 1. Pathogenesis of Neural Tube Defects and Prevention

### Neural Tube Development
The entire CNS develops from the neural tube. The notochord induces the midline ectoderm (outermost embryonic layer) to become the neural plate, which folds and closes to form the neural tube. **Neurulation (complete closure) is finished by week 4 of gestation** — before most people know they are pregnant.

- Closure begins at the neck and proceeds cephalad (toward the head) and caudad (toward the tail)
- The rostral tube becomes the cerebral hemispheres and brainstem; the caudal tube becomes the spinal cord

**Neural tube defects (NTDs)** occur when the neural folds fail to close or reopen, causing variable abnormalities of neural tissue, soft tissue, and overlying bone.

### NTDs: The Two Main Types

| Defect | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Anencephaly** | Failed anterior (cephalad) closure | Absent brain + skull; incompatible with survival |
| **Spina bifida** (spinal dysraphism) | Failed posterior (caudad) closure | Involves spinal cord; causes lower extremity and sphincter deficits |

### Spina Bifida Subtypes

| Subtype | What herniates | Neurologic deficit? |
|---|---|---|
| **Meningocele** | Meninges only (fluid-filled sac) | No |
| **Myelomeningocele (MMC)** | Spinal cord + meninges in a sac; CSF continuous with skin | Yes; most common lumbosacral |
| **Spina bifida aperta** | Open form; no overlying skin | Yes |
| **Congenital dermal sinus** | Ectodermal tract from skin to CNS persists | Risk of recurrent meningitis |
| **Spina bifida occulta** | Bony defect only; no cord or meninges involved | Usually none; may have overlying skin finding in ~10% |

**Hydrocephalus** occurs in ~90% of patients with MMC. MMC is surgically closed within the first 48 hours of life to prevent infection; shunt placement follows immediately to divert CSF flow.

### Prevention
- **Folic acid 0.4 mg daily** periconceptionally prevents 50-70% of NTDs
- Because the neural tube closes by week 4 (before most pregnancies are recognized), folate should be prescribed to all people of childbearing potential who are sexually active
- Higher doses are needed if on teratogenic medications (e.g., valproic acid, carbamazepine, phenobarbital)
- **Risk factors:** prior NTD with same partner, antiseizure medications, inadequate folate intake, gestational diabetes

### Prenatal Screening
- **Elevated maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)** correlates with open NTDs (closed lesions usually do not elevate AFP)
- Ultrasound can identify anencephaly and spina bifida

## 2. Prenatal and Newborn Screening

### Why It Matters
Pediatric neurologic care begins before birth. Prenatal and newborn screening can prevent or mitigate neurologic disease.

### Newborn Screening for Inborn Errors of Metabolism
- ~89 treatable disorders can affect the CNS during infancy or childhood
- These are single gene disorders causing toxic accumulation or energy deficiency
- Included on California Newborn Screening (blood and urine tests)
- **3 main categories:**
  1. Excessive accumulation of a toxic compound (e.g., organic acid disorders, urea cycle defects)
  2. Direct effects on energy metabolism (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase complex deficiency)
  3. Defects in catabolism in cell organelles (e.g., lysosomal storage disorders)

- **Presentations:** intellectual disability, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, behavioral disturbances, or nonspecific symptoms (poor feeding, lethargy, seizures)
- **Treatment:** dietary restrictions, enzyme replacement, vitamins/cofactors, bone marrow transplant, gene therapy
- **Caveat:** newborn screening can miss milder presentations that emerge later in life; consider inborn errors in patients with recurrent encephalopathy, ataxia, or metabolic acidosis triggered by illness or diet changes

## 3. General Approach to the Pediatric Neurology Patient

### Key Principle
Children are not just small adults. The nervous system is still developing, so symptoms and signs manifest differently depending on age and developmental stage. Much of the exam can be conducted by **watching the child talk, walk, and play**.

### Three-Step Diagnostic Approach

1. **Formulate key symptoms and signs** — establish a time course
   - Static deficits suggest trauma, hypoxic-ischemic injury, or congenital malformation
   - Progressive/insidious course suggests metabolic or neurodegenerative disease
   - Episodic course (minutes-hours) suggests seizures, TIA, or migraine

2. **Localize the lesion** — CNS vs. PNS; focal vs. generalized

3. **Etiological diagnosis** — use the mnemonic **I MIND THE VIP** to consider the differential

### Pediatric-Specific History Items (beyond standard adult history)
- **Pregnancy:** maternal age, toxin/drug/alcohol/folate exposure, prior miscarriages or unexplained child loss
- **Birth history:** gestational age, birth weight, method of delivery, Apgar scores, neonatal complications
- **Developmental history:** milestone achievement and timing; developmental **regression** is a red flag requiring expedited workup
- **Family history:** 3-generation pedigree, consanguinity
- **Social history:** poverty, nutrition, lead exposure, stress, access to prenatal care

### Pediatric Neurologic Exam

**Observation (performed first):**
- Head symmetry, dysmorphic features, head control, posture
- Skin exam: neurocutaneous stigmata
- Limb symmetry, spontaneous movement, tone, gait
- Level of alertness, eye contact, tracking, smile
- Language: what sounds/words does the child make?
- Odd behaviors: repetitive, self-stimulatory, self-injurious

**Hands-on exam (in a non-threatening environment):**
- Use games, toys, and distractions; have caregiver present
- Newborn/pediatric reflexes (e.g., Moro, stepping, fencer's posture)
- Drawing, copying figures, hopping, skipping
- **Head circumference (OFC = occipitofrontal circumference):** considered a vital sign in pediatrics; excessive head growth is a major sign of elevated ICP up to age 3
- **Fontanelle palpation:** bulging in a quiet infant = elevated ICP; sunken = dehydration/malnutrition
- Height and weight curves

| Finding | Definition | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Microcephaly** | Head >2 SD below mean | Infection (CMV, Zika), toxin exposure, chromosomal abnormalities, malnutrition |
| **Macrocephaly** | Head >2 SD above mean | Hydrocephalus, megalencephaly, space-occupying lesion, familial trait |

## 4. How Neurologic Signs Manifest Differently in Children

| Sign/Symptom | Pediatric Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Altered mental status** | Irritability, inconsolability, behavior change — not verbal complaint |
| **Elevated ICP** | Bulging fontanelle (infants <3 years), macrocephaly, setting sun sign (impaired upward gaze from midbrain compression) |
| **Developmental delay/regression** | Failure to meet milestones or loss of previously achieved skills |
| **Seizures** | Common presentation of neurologic disease; **infantile spasms** = sudden trunk flexion + limb stiffening |
| **Spasticity** | May appear as fisting (cortical thumb) or scissoring gait |
| **Hypotonia** | Frog-leg posture, head lag; can be CNS **or** PNS in origin |
| **Cerebral palsy** | Non-progressive motor deficit (hemiparesis, spasticity, dystonia); handedness before age 2 is abnormal and suggests weakness |

**Distinguishing CNS vs. PNS hypotonia:**
- CNS: decreased consciousness, seizures, hyperreflexia, spasticity
- PNS: alert infant, hyporeflexia, fasciculations, muscle atrophy, joint deformities

## 5. Developmental Milestones: Denver Developmental Screening Test II (DDST-II)

- Used from **birth to 6 years** of age
- Screens 4 domains: **gross motor, fine motor, language, personal-social**
- Identifies **developmental delay** (quotient <70% within a domain) and **global developmental delay** (>2 SD below in 2+ domains)
- **Regression** = loss of previously acquired skills → red flag requiring expedited workup, especially for treatable inborn errors of metabolism

### Example: Expected Milestones at 4-5 Years

| Domain | Expected Milestone |
|---|---|
| Cognitive/Language | Names 6 colors; speech fully understandable; draws recognizable person |
| Gross motor | Hops; balances on one foot x5 seconds; jumps forward 10 times |
| Fine motor | Copies simple figures |
| Social | Plays with other kids; dramatic/pretend play |
| Self-mastery | Uses knife for simple food prep; helps set table |

**Language delay** in particular predicts future intellectual disability.

**Intellectual disability** = significant limitations in intellectual functioning AND adaptive behavior, originating before age 18, applied to children ≥5 years. Severity:
- Mild: IQ 55-70
- Moderate: IQ 40-55
- Severe: IQ 25-40
- Profound: IQ <25

## 6. Neurocutaneous Disorders (Phakomatoses): TSC vs. NF-1 vs. NF-2

The skin and nervous system both derive from the **ectoderm**, so skin findings can be a key diagnostic clue for neurologic disease. Phakomatoses are multisystem genetic disorders primarily affecting the skin and CNS.

---

### Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC)

- **Incidence:** 1/6,000 births; inherited or sporadic
- **Genetics:** Autosomal dominant; mutations in **TSC1** (chromosome 9q34, encodes hamartin) or **TSC2** (chromosome 16p13, encodes tuberin) → dysregulation of the **mTOR pathway** → benign overgrowths (hamartomas)

**Neurologic manifestations:**
- **Epilepsy** in 80-90%; onset often in year 1 of life; 1/3 begin with infantile spasms
- **Intellectual disability** in 60%
- **Cortical tubers:** focal areas of dysplastic cortex with abnormal glutamate/GABA receptor expression → seizures
- **Subependymal nodules** ("candle wax drippings" lining the ventricles) → can enlarge into **subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs)** → obstruct the foramen of Monro → obstructive hydrocephalus → elevated ICP

**Skin findings:**
- Adenoma sebaceum (facial angiofibromas — not acne)
- Hypopigmented macules (ash-leaf spots — best seen under Wood's lamp)
- Shagreen patch (raised sandpaper-like lesion on lower back)
- Periungual fibromas
- Dental pits

**Other organs:** cardiac rhabdomyomas (30%, often spontaneously regress), renal angiomyolipomas (60%), retinal hamartomas (50-87%)

**Treatment:**
- Epilepsy: anti-seizure medications (ASMs) +/- epilepsy surgery
- SEGAs: **mTOR inhibitors** (e.g., everolimus/rapamycin) to shrink lesions; also used for refractory seizures, skin, renal, and pulmonary lesions
- Neurosurgical resection if indicated
- Supportive: speech/language therapy, genetic counseling, surveillance

---

### Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF-1) = von Recklinghausen Syndrome

- **Incidence:** 1/3,000-4,000 births — **most common** neurocutaneous disorder
- **Genetics:** Autosomal dominant; mutation in **NF1** gene on chromosome 17q11.2 (encodes neurofibromin); 50% sporadic; neurofibromin normally downregulates the **RAS-MAPK** pathway

**Key features:**
- **Skin (hyperpigmented):** cafe au lait spots (hallmark), axillary/inguinal freckling in 90%
- **Eyes:** optic pathway gliomas (WHO I pilocytic astrocytoma) in 15-20% of children; Lisch nodules (melanocytic iris hamartomas)
- **Brain:** NF-1 spots (asymptomatic T2/FLAIR white matter hyperintensities in 60-80% of children, in brainstem, basal ganglia, thalami, cerebellum); neurocognitive/behavioral symptoms in 30-70%
- **PNS:** neurofibromas (benign nerve sheath tumors, cutaneous or subcutaneous)

---

### Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF-2)

- **Incidence:** 1/33,000-40,000 births — much less common than NF-1 or TSC
- **Genetics:** Autosomal dominant; mutation in **NF2** gene on chromosome 22q12 (encodes merlin, a tumor suppressor); >50% sporadic

**Key features:**
- **Hallmark:** bilateral vestibular schwannomas (acoustic neuromas) in 80% — think "NF-2 = 2 schwannomas"
- **Brain:** multiple meningiomas
- **Eyes:** cataracts in 60-80%
- **Skin:** less prominent than NF-1 or TSC; multiple benign nerve sheath tumors

---

### Comparison Table: TSC vs. NF-1 vs. NF-2

| Feature | TSC | NF-1 | NF-2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Incidence** | 1/6,000 | 1/3,000-4,000 | 1/33,000-40,000 |
| **Gene/chromosome** | TSC1 (9q) or TSC2 (16p) | NF1 (17q) | NF2 (22q) |
| **Protein** | Hamartin / Tuberin | Neurofibromin | Merlin |
| **Pathway** | mTOR | RAS-MAPK | RAS-MAPK |
| **Inheritance** | Autosomal dominant | Autosomal dominant (50% sporadic) | Autosomal dominant (>50% sporadic) |
| **Skin** | Ash-leaf spots (hypopigmented), angiofibromas, Shagreen patch | Cafe au lait spots (hyperpigmented), freckling, neurofibromas | Less prominent; nerve sheath tumors |
| **Hallmark brain finding** | Cortical tubers; subependymal nodules/SEGAs | Optic pathway glioma; NF-1 spots | Bilateral vestibular schwannomas |
| **Epilepsy** | 80-90% | Less common | Less common |
| **Eyes** | Retinal hamartomas | Lisch nodules; optic glioma | Cataracts (60-80%) |
