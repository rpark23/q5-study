## Learning Objectives

### 1. Pathophysiology of arterial and venous thrombosis, including Virchow's Triad

- **Virchow's Triad** describes three precipitating factors for thrombus formation:
  - **Hypercoagulability**: intrinsic alterations in the nature of blood itself (e.g., inherited thrombophilias like antithrombin deficiency)
  - **Vascular Endothelial Damage**: inflammation near blood vessels (e.g., vasculitis, trauma)
  - **Blood Flow Stasis**: decreased blood flow (e.g., immobility)
- **Arterial thrombus** characteristics:
  - High flow, high shear forces environment
  - Platelet-rich and fibrin-poor ("white clot")
  - Pathophysiology involves atherosclerotic damage, inflammation, ruptured plaque, and high shear forces promoting von Willebrand factor unfolding and platelet adhesion
  - Clinical manifestations: stroke, myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease
  - Treatment: antiplatelet agents
- **Venous thrombus** characteristics:
  - Low flow, low shear forces environment
  - Fibrin-rich and platelet-poor ("red clot" due to trapped RBCs)
  - Pathophysiology involves disorder of plasma coagulation with endothelial activation leading to inflammation and coagulation pathway activation
  - Mechanisms include inappropriate coagulation activation, gain of coagulation factor function, inadequate thrombin control, or suppressed fibrinolysis
  - Clinical manifestations: deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE)
  - Treatment: anticoagulation
- **Immunothrombosis**: thrombosis evolved as part of the host's innate defense mechanism against infection, with fibrin clots helping to localize pathogens and limit host damage from oxidative enzymes

### 2. Key features of history and physical exam suggesting DVT or PE

- **Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)**:
  - **Location**: popliteal and femoral veins, iliac veins (in pediatrics); note that "superficial femoral vein" is actually a deep vein
  - Proximal DVTs are above the knee; distal DVTs are below the knee
  - **Clinical signs**: lower extremity pain, edema, erythema, palpable cords
  - Homan's sign is not sensitive or specific
  - **Diagnosis**: ultrasonography
  - **Provoking risk factors**: surgery, trauma, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, malignancy, heart failure, autoimmunity, obesity, central lines in children
  - Untreated risk: extension, end organ damage, embolization
- **Pulmonary Embolism (PE)**:
  - **Location**: pulmonary arteries (segmental and subsegmental)
  - **Clinical signs**: dyspnea, tachypnea, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis
  - **Vitals**: tachycardia, tachypnea, hypoxemia on pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas
  - **Diagnosis**: spiral chest CT angiogram (CTA); alternatives include V/Q scan or angiogram
  - **Clinical score assessment**: modified Wells Score
  - **Provoking risk factors**: DVT, surgery, trauma, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, malignancy, heart failure, obesity, autoimmunity
- **Important case presentation clues**:
  - Young age (inherited vs. acquired)
  - Immobilization after surgery
  - Recurrent pregnancy loss (suggests antiphospholipid syndrome)
  - Oral contraceptive use
  - Family history of stroke at young age
  - Anatomic considerations: May-Thurner anatomy, thoracic outlet syndrome
  - Unusual clot location (e.g., mesenteric) may suggest myeloproliferative neoplasm or PNH

### 3. Most common inherited hypercoagulable conditions

- **Factor V Leiden** (most common):
  - Autosomal dominant inheritance
  - Point mutation R506Q making Factor V resistant to inactivation by activated Protein C
  - Heterozygous: 5x increased thrombotic risk
  - Homozygous: 18-20x increased thrombotic risk
  - Diagnosis: genetic testing
  - Treatment: heterozygotes do not usually need lifelong anticoagulation after first unprovoked VTE
- **Prothrombin G20210A**:
  - Autosomal dominant inheritance
  - G20210A point mutation in noncoding region
  - Heterozygotes have ~30% higher plasma prothrombin levels
  - Heterozygous: 3.8x increased thrombotic risk
  - Diagnosis: genetic testing
  - Treatment: heterozygotes do not usually need lifelong anticoagulation after first unprovoked VTE
- **Antithrombin (ATIII) Deficiency**:
  - Autosomal dominant inheritance (acquired deficiency also possible)
  - Antithrombin binds and inhibits thrombin (FII), FIXa, FXa—key regulator of coagulation cascade
  - Clinical presentation: venous (rarely arterial) thrombosis in 2nd or 3rd decade; episodic thrombosis
  - Diagnosis: ATIII functional assay; levels 40-60% (sometimes <80%)
  - Treatment: very high risk of recurrence; lifelong anticoagulation
  - Note: heparin resistance can occur
  - Heterozygotes are symptomatic
- **Protein C Deficiency**:
  - Autosomal dominant inheritance; mutations in PROC gene (chromosome 2)
  - Activated Protein C inactivates FVa and FVIIIa, reducing thrombin generation
  - Clinical presentation: venous and arterial thrombosis, neonatal purpura fulminans
  - Diagnosis: antigen and functional activity level <55%
  - Treatment: high risk of recurrence
- **Protein S Deficiency**:
  - Autosomal dominant inheritance; mutations in PROS1 gene (chromosome 3)
  - Cofactor for activated Protein C
  - Clinical presentation: venous and arterial thrombosis, neonatal purpura fulminans
  - Diagnosis: antigen and functional activity level <35%
  - Treatment: high risk of recurrence
- **Testing considerations**:
  - Best to test non-DNA based tests when patient is not on anticoagulation or in acute thrombosis
  - DNA-based tests (Factor V Leiden, Prothrombin G20210A) unaffected by anticoagulation
  - Best conducted with hematology consultation
  - Basic workup: Factor V Leiden, Prothrombin 20210A, Protein C, Protein S, Antithrombin, +/- antiphospholipid antibodies

### 4. Most common acquired hypercoagulable states: pathophysiology and management

#### **Strong Thrombotic Risk ("Big Three" in adults)**

**Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)**:
- Systemic autoimmune disorder with recurrent thrombosis (arterial and venous)
- Autoantibodies against protein-lipid complexes; main target is anti-β2-GPI antibody
- Anti-β2-GPI antibody binds β2-GPI, maintains open conformation, enables binding to cell surface receptor, activating platelets, monocytes, and endothelial cells
- Clinical features: obstetric morbidity, mild thrombocytopenia, nephropathy, cardiac valve thickening
- **Diagnosis (Sapporo Criteria)**:
  - **Lupus anticoagulant**: prolonged PTT that doesn't correct with mixing study, corrects with excess lipids; heterogeneous IgG and IgM antibodies targeting phospholipid-protein complex
  - **Anticardiolipin antibodies** by ELISA
  - **Anti-β2-GPI antibodies** by ELISA
  - Must be persistent on repeat testing at 12 weeks
- **Management**:
  - High recurrence rate (~40% over 5 years)
  - Lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin for prior thrombosis
  - Patients with arterial and venous thrombosis may need both anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy
  - DOACs are NOT used in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome (positive for all three antibodies) or with arterial thrombosis
  - Positive serology only without clinical thrombosis: observe closely

**Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)**:
- Complication of heparin treatment causing thrombocytopenia and thrombosis
- **Pathophysiology**:
  - Platelet factor 4 (PF4) released from platelet granules binds heparin through electrostatic interaction (PF4 is positively charged, heparin is negatively charged)
  - Antibodies form against heparin-PF4 complex
  - HIT antibodies bind heparin/PF4 complexes on platelet membrane
  - IgG Fc domains engage platelet FcγRIIa (CD32) receptors
  - Leads to platelet activation, granule release, procoagulant microparticle formation
  - Results in thrombocytopenia and thrombosis (arterial and venous)
- **Diagnosis**:
  - **4 T's Score**: Thrombocytopenia (50% decrease, nadir >20K), Timing (5-10 days after heparin; <1 day with recent exposure), Thrombosis, no other cause
  - Screening: Heparin-PF4 ELISA
  - Confirmatory: heparin-induced platelet aggregation (at Stanford) or serotonin release assay
- **Management**:
  - Stop all heparin
  - Alternative anticoagulants: argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, DOACs
  - LMWH contraindicated due to high cross-reactivity

**Cancer-Associated Thrombosis (Trousseau Syndrome)**:
- Clinical presentation: migratory superficial thrombophlebitis, DVT, PE, arterial thrombosis (splenic infarcts, mesenteric thrombosis, TIA), nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (marantic endocarditis)
- Etiology: underlying visceral cancer (pancreatic, GI)
- **Pathogenesis**:
  - Cancer procoagulants: tissue factor, microparticles
  - Chronic DIC with increased D-dimer
- **Treatment**: LMWH more effective than warfarin; can use DOACs

#### **Other Acquired Hypercoagulable States**

**Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)**:
- Systemic activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis with resultant thrombosis/end organ damage AND consumptive coagulopathy (bleeding)
- DIC is always secondary to an underlying condition:
  - Infection, malignancy (APML), trauma, vascular malformation, cirrhosis, heat stroke, post-resuscitation
- **Pathophysiology**:
  - Underlying condition triggers endothelial damage and neutrophil activation
  - Activation of intravascular coagulation
  - Fibrin deposition leads to platelet consumption and impaired coagulation
  - Fibrinolysis produces D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDPs)
  - High FDPs interfere with normal fibrin polymerization and fibrinogen/platelet binding
  - Microvascular thrombosis causes organ ischemia/organ failure
  - Consumptive coagulopathy causes bleeding
- **Laboratory diagnosis**:
  - Thrombocytopenia
  - Prolonged PTT and PT (decreased clotting factors including Factor V and VIII)
  - Decreased fibrinogen (key finding unless congenital hypofibrinogenemia or liver disease)
  - Increased D-dimer
  - Can send individual factor levels to distinguish from liver failure: in liver failure, Factor VIII is elevated; in DIC, Factor VIII is decreased
- **Treatment**:
  - Treat the underlying cause (most important)
  - Product support based on clinical presentation: platelets, cryoprecipitate, FFP for bleeding
  - Anticoagulation not indicated unless thrombus in high-risk vasculature or end organ damage
  - Heparin may be useful in chronic DIC

**Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)**:
- Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) caused by deficiency of ADAMTS13
- **Pathophysiology**:
  - ADAMTS13 is a metalloprotease that cleaves von Willebrand factor
  - In ADAMTS13 deficiency, ultra-large vWF multimers remain uncleaved, undergo shearing and conformational change
  - Ultra-large activated vWF multimers bind platelets, causing platelet adhesion and aggregation
  - Drives microthrombosis, thrombocytopenia, and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)
- **Clinical presentation**:
  - Challenging diagnosis; "clinical pentad" (fever, thrombocytopenia, MAHA, neurological symptoms, renal insufficiency) present in <10% of patients
  - Most common: primary immune-mediated (59%), but also associated with autoimmune disease, infection, HIV, congenital causes, cancer, pregnancy
- **Laboratory diagnosis**:
  - Bicytopenia with hemolytic anemia (DAT negative, microangiopathy) and thrombocytopenia
  - PLASMIC score for clinical prediction
- **Treatment**:
  - Therapeutic plasma exchange
  - In consultation with hematology: steroids, rituximab, caplacizumab

**Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS) - Shiga Toxin Mediated**:
- Thrombotic microangiopathy caused by enteric infection with Shiga toxin-secreting E. coli (O157:H7) or Shigella dysenteriae
- **Pathophysiology**:
  - Local mucosal injury and systemic transport of toxin
  - Toxin binds to Gb3 (richest in kidney), causes inhibition of protein synthesis and ribotoxic stress
  - End-organ microvascular injury drives platelet activation, thrombocytopenia, and hemolytic anemia
- **Clinical presentation**:
  - Preceding illness with diarrhea
  - Acute kidney injury and bicytopenia (thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolysis)
  - More common in young children; most cases sporadic; large outbreaks also occur
- **Laboratory diagnosis**:
  - Bicytopenia with hemolytic anemia (DAT negative, microangiopathy) and thrombocytopenia
- **Treatment**: supportive care

#### **Mild/Moderate Thrombotic Risk**
- Surgery, old age, immobilization, pregnancy, oral estrogen-containing contraceptives, myeloproliferative disease

**Note**: In children, the "big three" are central venous catheters, cardiac disease, and cancer

## Detailed Content

### Virchow's Triad and Thrombus Formation

A **thrombosis** is a clot in a blood vessel that leads to obstruction of that vessel. The obstruction results in ischemia due to loss of blood supply and can lead to end organ necrosis. Virchow's Triad describes not only the result of a thrombus but also the precipitating factors. In most patients, at least one of these factors is identified as causing a thrombus.

### Arterial vs. Venous Thrombosis: Key Distinctions

**Arterial Thrombosis**:
- Occurs in high-flow environments (e.g., coronary artery)
- Associated with atherosclerotic damage and inflammation
- Ruptured plaque and high shear forces promote binding and unfolding of von Willebrand factor
- This incites platelet aggregation and activation
- Histopathology: fibrin, leukocytes, and abundant platelets ("white clot")
- Clinical presentations: acute stroke, myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease
- Treatment: antiplatelet agents

**Venous Thrombosis**:
- Occurs in low-flow environments (e.g., deep veins of legs)
- Disorder of plasma coagulation
- Fibrin-rich, originating in areas of slower blood flow
- Endothelium becomes activated, setting off inflammation and coagulation pathway activation
- Coagulation system imbalances: inappropriate activation, gain of coagulation factor function, inadequate thrombin control, or suppressed fibrinolysis
- Components of cancer or chronic heart, lung, or renal disease often implicated
- Histopathology: fibrin, leukocytes, and red blood cells ("red clot"); platelets less prominent
- Clinical presentations: DVT, PE
- Treatment: anticoagulation

### Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism: Clinical Approach

**History and Physical Examination Considerations**:
- **Age**: suggests inherited vs. acquired causes
- **Provoking factors**: central line, surgery, immobilization, cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, nephrotic syndrome
- **Past medical history**: prior thrombosis, complications during high-risk situations (surgery, pregnancy)
- **Anatomic etiology**: May-Thurner anatomy, thoracic outlet syndrome
- **Family history**: thrombophilia
- **Exam findings**: signs of superficial or deep venous thromboembolism, signs of PE, unusual site (e.g., mesenteric vein thrombosis suggests myeloproliferative neoplasm or PNH)
- **Risk assessment**: oxygenation/perfusion, clot size and location, occlusive vs. non-occlusive, risk of extension and embolization

**Strong risk factors** like cancer, HIT, and APS may breakthrough conventional antithrombotic therapy (warfarin with therapeutic INR).

### Inherited Thrombophilias: Relative Risk

The most common inherited thrombophilias and their relative risks for VTE:
- **Factor V Leiden, heterozygous**: Common; 4.9-fold risk
- **Factor V Leiden, homozygous**: Uncommon; 18-fold risk
- **Prothrombin G20210A, heterozygous**: Common; 3.8-fold risk
- **Compound heterozygous (PG20210A + FVL)**: Uncommon; 20-fold risk
- **Protein C deficiency**: Rare; 24.1-fold risk
- **Protein S deficiency**: Rare; 30.6-fold risk
- **Antithrombin deficiency**: Rare; 28.2-fold risk

### Acquired Hypercoagulable States: Special Considerations

**Cancer and Thrombosis**:
Cancer is one of the few conditions often associated with thrombi on both the arterial and venous side. Trousseau syndrome may refer specifically to thrombophlebitis migrans and cancer-associated thrombosis, but also encompasses other unusual forms such as marantic endocarditis and TMA.

**Antiphospholipid Syndrome**:
The historical name reflects the initial hypothesis that antibodies were directed against phospholipids only. We now know the antibodies target protein-lipid complexes. Patients can have obstetric APS (recurrent pregnancy loss) and/or thrombotic APS (arterial/venous thrombi). A lupus anticoagulant is an in-vitro phenomenon where the PTT is prolonged (only in test tube) and often does not correct with plasma due to it being a strong inhibitor. However, the lupus anticoagulant antibody is a sign of antiphospholipid syndrome, which is a hypercoagulable condition.

**DIC Variants**:
Some patients exist in a chronic state of DIC (such as with malignancy or cirrhosis). Acute DIC is more often seen in patients with acute infection or trauma/shock. DIC is a dynamic process.

**Thrombotic Microangiopathies**:
TMA is a pathologic/histologic term for arteriolar/capillary thrombosis due to endothelial injury. Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) is a laboratory diagnosis. Not all MAHA is caused by TMA (e.g., mechanical valve), and not all TMA presents with MAHA (e.g., if isolated to single organ), but there is overlap. DIC is a systemic disorder that can present with MAHA and thrombocytopenia but is a disease of both thrombosis and bleeding.

### Anticoagulation Overview

**Antiplatelet Agents**:
- COX-1 inhibitors (ASA, NSAIDs)
- ADP receptor antagonists (clopidogrel, prasugrel)
- GPIIb-IIIa antagonists (abciximab)

**Anticoagulants**:
- **Unfractionated Heparin**: IV administration, binds antithrombin and inhibits Factor Xa and IIa, monitored with HAL (goal 0.3-0.7) or PTT, 1-hour half-life, fully reversible with protamine; risk of HIT, dependent on antithrombin
- **Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH)**: SubQ administration, binds antithrombin primarily inhibiting Factor Xa, monitored with Anti-Xa (goal 0.5-1.0 after third dose), 4-7 hour half-life, partially reversible with protamine; typically contraindicated in renal failure
- **IV Thrombin Inhibitors (Argatroban, Bivalirudin)**: IV administration, directly inhibits IIa, monitored with PTT, short half-life (bivalirudin 15-30 min, argatroban 45 min-1 hr), no reversal agent but short half-life; commonly used in ICU setting
- **Warfarin (Coumadin)**: PO administration, vitamin K antagonist inhibiting glutamic acid carboxylation in FII, FVII, FIX, FX, Protein C, Protein S; monitored with INR, 20-60 hour half-life, reversible with vitamin K; difficult to titrate, bridge with heparin to avoid skin necrosis
- **DOACs (Rivaroxaban, Apixaban)**: PO administration, direct Xa inhibitors, no routine monitoring, variable half-life (rivaroxaban 5-9h, apixaban 8-12h), reversible with andexanet-alpha or PCC; difficulty with reversibility, absorption, renal/liver clearance

**Thrombolytics**:
- Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)
- Streptokinase and urokinase

## Key Concepts

1. **Virchow's Triad** is the fundamental framework for understanding thrombosis: hypercoagulability, vascular endothelial damage, and blood flow stasis.

2. **Arterial thrombi** are platelet-rich "white clots" treated with antiplatelet agents; **venous thrombi** are fibrin-rich "red clots" treated with anticoagulation.

3. **Immunothrombosis** represents the evolutionary link between coagulation and innate immunity, with thrombosis helping to contain pathogens.

4. **Factor V Leiden and Prothrombin G20210A** are the most common inherited thrombophilias; heterozygotes have modest increased risk and typically don't require lifelong anticoagulation after a first unprovoked VTE.

5. **Antithrombin, Protein C, and Protein S deficiencies** are rare but carry high thrombotic risk and typically require lifelong anticoagulation after any VTE.

6. The **"Big Three" acquired hypercoagulable states** in adults are antiphospholipid syndrome, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and cancer (Trousseau syndrome).

7. **Antiphospholipid syndrome** requires persistent positive antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2-GPI) at 12 weeks and is treated with lifelong warfarin (not DOACs in triple-positive or arterial thrombosis).

8. **HIT** is diagnosed with the 4 T's score and confirmed with functional assays; treatment requires stopping all heparin and using alternative anticoagulants.

9. **DIC** is always secondary to an underlying condition; treatment focuses on addressing the underlying cause and providing supportive care based on clinical presentation (bleeding vs. thrombosis).

10. **TTP** is caused by ADAMTS13 deficiency leading to ultra-large vWF multimers; treatment is therapeutic plasma exchange with hematology consultation.

11. **Shiga toxin-mediated HUS** follows E. coli O157:H7 infection and is treated with supportive care, predominantly affecting children.

12. Testing for inherited thrombophilias should be done in consultation with hematology, ideally when not on anticoagulation or during acute thrombosis (except for DNA-based tests).

13. A **low fibrinogen** should raise suspicion for DIC unless the patient has hepatic synthetic dysfunction or congenital hypofibrinogenemia.

14. **Cancer, HIT, and APS** are strong enough risk factors that they may cause breakthrough thrombosis despite therapeutic anticoagulation.

15. **Mesenteric vein thrombosis** should raise suspicion for underlying myeloproliferative neoplasm or PNH.
