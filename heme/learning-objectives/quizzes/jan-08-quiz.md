## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Erythropoietin (EPO) is the primary growth factor for the erythroid lineage, stimulating red blood cell production rather than platelet production. Targeting EPO would increase erythropoiesis, not thrombopoiesis.
- **B)** Incorrect. G-CSF drives granulopoiesis, specifically promoting the production and maturation of neutrophils. It would not selectively increase platelet counts.
- **C)** Correct. Thrombopoietin (TPO) is the primary lineage-specific growth factor for megakaryopoiesis, directly stimulating megakaryocyte proliferation and differentiation to increase platelet production.
- **D)** Incorrect. Stem cell factor (SCF) acts on early multipotent hematopoietic progenitors via the c-Kit receptor and affects multiple blood cell lineages. Targeting SCF would not selectively increase platelet production.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The bone marrow does not become a significant site of hematopoiesis until later in fetal development, typically not until mid-to-late gestation. At 6 weeks, the marrow cavity has not yet formed.
- **B)** Correct. The yolk sac is the initial and predominant site of hematopoiesis at approximately 6 weeks of embryonic development, producing primarily primitive erythroid cells with embryonic hemoglobins.
- **C)** Incorrect. The fetal liver begins to take over hematopoiesis around weeks 6-8 and becomes the major site during mid-gestation, but at exactly 6 weeks the yolk sac is still the predominant site.
- **D)** Incorrect. The AGM region contributes definitive hematopoietic stem cells around a similar time, but it is not the predominant site of active blood cell production at this stage; its role is primarily the generation of definitive HSCs that will seed other organs.

### Q3. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. The kidneys produce EPO, which binds the EPO receptor on erythroid progenitors and signals through the JAK2-STAT5 pathway to promote red blood cell survival and proliferation. In chronic kidney disease, EPO production falls, directly causing anemia.
- **B)** Incorrect. The SCF-c-Kit pathway activates multiple downstream signals including NF-kB, but SCF acts on early multipotent progenitors and is not the growth factor whose decreased production in kidney disease causes anemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. The TGF-beta/SMAD pathway is involved in regulation and inhibition of hematopoiesis, not in the EPO-driven erythropoietic signaling that is lost in chronic kidney disease.
- **D)** Incorrect. The Flt3 receptor and Ras-MAPK pathway are important for early progenitor cell proliferation and dendritic cell development, not for the terminal erythroid differentiation that depends on renal EPO production.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Increased 2,3-BPG production is an acute adaptation that shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve rightward to improve oxygen delivery to tissues, but it does not increase oxygen-carrying capacity (i.e., it does not increase red blood cell mass over days).
- **B)** Correct. Under hypoxic conditions, prolyl hydroxylases cannot function because they require molecular oxygen as a substrate. Without hydroxylation, HIF-alpha is stabilized rather than degraded, translocates to the nucleus, and upregulates EPO gene transcription, initiating increased erythropoiesis over the following days.
- **C)** Incorrect. Low oxygen tension does not directly stimulate JAK2 in the bone marrow. JAK2 is activated downstream of the EPO receptor after EPO binds, so the initiating event must be EPO production, which depends on HIF stabilization in the kidney.
- **D)** Incorrect. Erythropoietin is not stored in hepatic reserves for later release. EPO is synthesized de novo primarily by peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney (and to a lesser extent by hepatocytes) in response to hypoxia-driven HIF stabilization.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Delta-ALA synthase is the rate-limiting first enzyme of heme synthesis and is not inhibited by lead; lead poisoning actually causes a compensatory increase in ALA synthase activity. Porphobilinogen deaminase deficiency is associated with acute intermittent porphyria, not lead poisoning.
- **B)** Correct. This presentation is classic for lead poisoning. Lead inhibits ALA dehydrase (causing accumulation of delta-aminolevulinic acid) and ferrochelatase (the final enzyme, preventing iron insertion into protoporphyrin IX, which explains the elevated free erythrocyte protoporphyrin and basophilic stippling).
- **C)** Incorrect. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase deficiency is associated with porphyria cutanea tarda, and coproporphyrinogen oxidase deficiency with hereditary coproporphyria. Neither enzyme is characteristically inhibited by lead.
- **D)** Incorrect. While ferrochelatase is indeed inhibited by lead, delta-ALA synthase is not. In fact, ALA synthase activity is upregulated in lead poisoning as a compensatory response to impaired downstream heme production.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Both fetal hemoglobin (alpha2-gamma2) and adult hemoglobin (alpha2-beta2) contain two alpha globin chains. The difference lies in the non-alpha chains (gamma vs. beta), not in the proportion of alpha chains.
- **B)** Correct. Fetal hemoglobin gamma chains have reduced affinity for 2,3-BPG compared to adult beta chains. Since 2,3-BPG normally stabilizes the T-state (deoxygenated form) and decreases oxygen affinity, reduced 2,3-BPG binding means HbF remains in the high-affinity R-state, shifting the curve left and facilitating oxygen transfer from mother to fetus.
- **C)** Incorrect. Susceptibility to methemoglobin formation (oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+) is not the mechanism responsible for the left-shifted oxygen dissociation curve of fetal hemoglobin. Methemoglobin cannot bind oxygen at all rather than binding it more tightly.
- **D)** Incorrect. Fetal hemoglobin does not have a significantly different molecular weight from adult hemoglobin. Both are tetramers of approximately 64,000 daltons, and molecular weight is not the basis for the difference in oxygen affinity.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The aspirate, not the biopsy, provides superior individual cell morphology because aspirated cells can be spread on a slide for detailed Wright-Giemsa staining and microscopic examination of nuclear and cytoplasmic features.
- **B)** Correct. The biopsy core preserves overall tissue architecture, allowing assessment of cellularity, fibrosis, and spatial relationships between cells. In a "dry tap" scenario where fibrotic or densely packed marrow prevents aspiration, the biopsy is the only way to evaluate marrow structure.
- **C)** Incorrect. Flow cytometry requires a suspension of individual cells, which is obtained from the aspirate, not the biopsy core. The solid tissue of a biopsy is not directly amenable to flow cytometric immunophenotyping.
- **D)** Incorrect. Cytogenetic analysis (karyotyping, FISH) is typically performed on aspirated cells that can be cultured or prepared as individual cell suspensions. The biopsy core is not the preferred specimen for cytogenetic studies.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Promyelocytes do retain mitotic capability, but they are not the last stage to do so. The promyelocyte divides to produce myelocytes, which themselves still undergo further division.
- **B)** Correct. The myelocyte is the last stage of granulocyte maturation that retains the ability to divide. After the myelocyte stage, cells progress to metamyelocytes, bands, and segmented neutrophils, all of which undergo terminal differentiation without further mitosis.
- **C)** Incorrect. Metamyelocytes have already lost the ability to divide. They are post-mitotic cells that continue to mature morphologically (with progressive nuclear indentation) but no longer replicate their DNA or undergo cell division.
- **D)** Incorrect. Band neutrophils are post-mitotic, terminally differentiated cells. They are nearly mature neutrophils with an unsegmented, horseshoe-shaped nucleus and do not divide.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While the bone marrow storage pool can release mature neutrophils (not myelocytes) in response to stress signals, this process takes hours and involves mature cells, not immature myelocytes. The rapidity of the response (minutes) rules out marrow release as the primary mechanism.
- **B)** Correct. Approximately half of circulating neutrophils normally adhere to blood vessel walls in the marginating pool. Epinephrine (a catecholamine) reduces selectin- and integrin-mediated adhesion, rapidly mobilizing these marginated neutrophils into the freely circulating pool, doubling the measured WBC count within minutes.
- **C)** Incorrect. Proliferation of myeloid progenitors takes days to weeks to produce mature circulating cells. A doubling of the WBC count within minutes is far too rapid to be explained by new cell production, even with G-CSF stimulation.
- **D)** Incorrect. Eosinophils are a minor component of the total WBC count, and their demargination would not account for a near doubling of the total WBC. Furthermore, eosinophils reside primarily in tissues rather than in a vascular marginating pool like neutrophils.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not undergo asymmetric cell division. Standard cell division, whether symmetric or asymmetric, would produce separate daughter cells rather than the single large polyploid cell needed for massive platelet production.
- **B)** Correct. Megakaryocytes undergo endoreduplication (endomitosis), in which the DNA replicates repeatedly without nuclear or cellular division, producing a large polyploid cell (up to 64N or more). This results in a massive cytoplasmic volume that is ultimately fragmented into thousands of platelets.
- **C)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not form by fusion of multiple progenitor cells. They arise from a single megakaryocyte progenitor that becomes polyploid through endoreduplication, not through cell-cell fusion.
- **D)** Incorrect. Rapid sequential mitoses with incomplete cytokinesis would produce a multinucleated cell with separate nuclei, which is not the mechanism in megakaryocytes. Megakaryocytes have a single polylobulated nucleus resulting from DNA replication without any nuclear or cellular division.

### Q11. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) blasts would express myeloid markers such as CD13, CD33, and myeloperoxidase. The presence of CD19 (a B-cell marker) and CD10 (CALLA, a pre-B-cell marker) points to a lymphoid rather than myeloid origin.
- **B)** Incorrect. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a disease of mature B-lymphocytes typically seen in older adults, expressing CD5, CD19, and CD23 but not CD34 or CD10. The CD34 positivity here indicates immature blasts, not a mature lymphoid malignancy.
- **C)** Correct. The combination of CD34 (immature blast marker), CD19 (pan-B-cell marker), and CD10 (CALLA, characteristic of precursor B-cell neoplasms) in a 5-year-old child is the classic immunophenotype of acute B-lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), the most common childhood cancer.
- **D)** Incorrect. Mantle cell lymphoma is a mature B-cell lymphoma seen in older adults that characteristically expresses CD5, CD19, and cyclin D1, but not CD34 or CD10. The CD34 positivity here indicates an immature precursor neoplasm, not a mature lymphoma.

### Q12. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Antigen presentation by follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) to centrocytes occurs in the light zone, not the dark zone. The light zone is where B-cells are tested for improved antigen affinity after mutation.
- **B)** Incorrect. Selection of B-cells based on improved antigen affinity is a light zone process. Centrocytes in the light zone compete for antigen presented by FDCs, and those with higher-affinity receptors receive survival signals.
- **C)** Correct. The dark zone of the germinal center contains centroblasts, which are rapidly proliferating B-cells that undergo somatic hypermutation of their immunoglobulin variable region genes. This introduces point mutations that may improve or worsen antigen binding affinity.
- **D)** Incorrect. Isotype class switching occurs in the light zone and involves T follicular helper (Tfh) cell signals such as CD40L and cytokines. While it is a germinal center process, it is not the primary activity of the dark zone centroblasts.

### Q13. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. BCL2 is normally expressed by many cell types including B-cells at various stages of development. The key point is that germinal center B-cells specifically downregulate BCL2, so its presence in GC B-cells is abnormal, not because it is a T-cell-only protein.
- **B)** Correct. Normal germinal center B-cells downregulate BCL2 (an anti-apoptotic protein) so that B-cells failing to improve antigen affinity during somatic hypermutation can be eliminated by apoptosis. Aberrant BCL2 expression, typically from the t(14;18) translocation in follicular lymphoma, prevents this quality-control mechanism and allows accumulation of B-cells regardless of affinity.
- **C)** Incorrect. BCL2 is an anti-apoptotic protein that prevents programmed cell death; it does not directly activate the cell cycle or drive proliferation. Follicular lymphoma characteristically has a low proliferation rate; the problem is impaired apoptosis, not uncontrolled division.
- **D)** Incorrect. BCL2 does not block somatic hypermutation. Somatic hypermutation can still occur in cells expressing BCL2. The pathologic consequence is that mutant cells that would normally die due to poor antigen affinity instead survive because BCL2 blocks apoptosis.

### Q14. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Positive selection occurs in the thymic cortex, not the medulla. In the cortex, cortical thymic epithelial cells present self-MHC to double-positive thymocytes, and those that can weakly recognize self-MHC survive.
- **B)** Incorrect. The beta-selection checkpoint occurs during the double-negative (DN) stage in the thymic cortex, well before thymocytes reach the medulla. It ensures productive rearrangement of the TCR beta chain before further maturation proceeds.
- **C)** Correct. Hassall corpuscles are concentrically arranged epithelial structures found in the thymic medulla. The medulla houses mature single-positive T-cells that have passed both positive selection (in the cortex) and negative selection. Hassall corpuscles are thought to play a role in TSLP production and regulatory T-cell development.
- **D)** Incorrect. Proliferation of double-negative thymocytes in response to IL-7 occurs in the thymic cortex during early T-cell development, not in the medulla where Hassall corpuscles are found.

### Q15. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The spleen does not produce erythropoietin (EPO is produced by the kidneys and to a lesser extent by the liver). Furthermore, iron granules visible on a standard blood smear (siderotic granules/Pappenheimer bodies) are not the characteristic small, dark, round inclusions described here.
- **B)** Correct. Howell-Jolly bodies are nuclear remnants (fragments of DNA) that are normally removed from red blood cells by splenic macrophages through a process called pitting. After splenectomy, this quality-control function is lost, and Howell-Jolly bodies persist in circulating RBCs as a hallmark of asplenia.
- **C)** Incorrect. The spleen does not perform pH buffering for the blood, and hemoglobin does not precipitate due to pH changes in this manner. Precipitated hemoglobin (Heinz bodies) is associated with oxidative damage and G6PD deficiency, not with splenectomy per se.
- **D)** Incorrect. While asplenic patients are at increased risk for certain infections (especially encapsulated bacteria and intraerythrocytic parasites like Babesia), the inclusions described here are not parasitic. The small, dark, round inclusions appearing uniformly after splenectomy are Howell-Jolly bodies.

### Q16. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Hemoglobin oxygen affinity determines how readily hemoglobin releases oxygen to tissues, but it is not the biophysical property being tested when RBCs squeeze through the narrow slits of the splenic sinusoids. Passage through these slits is a mechanical, not biochemical, challenge.
- **B)** Correct. The narrow inter-endothelial slits (1-3 microns) between the cords of Billroth and the splenic sinusoids require red blood cells to undergo extreme deformation to pass through. Aged, rigid, or abnormally shaped RBCs (e.g., spherocytes, sickle cells) cannot deform sufficiently and become trapped for phagocytosis by macrophages.
- **C)** Incorrect. While erythrocyte surface charge (due to sialic acid residues) contributes to preventing RBC aggregation, it is not the primary property tested by passage through the splenic cords. The physical squeezing through narrow slits primarily tests membrane flexibility and cell shape.
- **D)** Incorrect. Although ATP is required to maintain the RBC membrane and cytoskeletal proteins that support deformability, the splenic filtration process directly tests the physical deformability of the cell, not its metabolic rate of ATP production.

### Q17. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Intracellular organisms like Salmonella are handled primarily by cellular immunity (macrophages, T-cells), not by splenic opsonization of circulating organisms. While asplenic patients may have some increased susceptibility, Salmonella is not the classic cause of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI).
- **B)** Correct. Asplenic patients are uniquely susceptible to overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) by encapsulated bacteria, especially Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis. The spleen is critical for filtering and opsonizing encapsulated organisms that evade complement-mediated killing, which is why vaccination is essential before or after splenectomy.
- **C)** Incorrect. Anaerobic infections like those caused by Clostridium perfringens are typically associated with wound contamination or bowel perforation, not with asplenia. The spleen does not play a critical role in defense against anaerobes.
- **D)** Incorrect. Fungal pathogens like Aspergillus fumigatus are primarily controlled by neutrophils and macrophages, and invasive aspergillosis is associated with severe neutropenia or immunosuppressive therapy, not with splenectomy.

### Q18. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Positive selection rescues thymocytes that weakly recognize self-MHC, confirming their TCR is functional. Thymocytes that strongly recognize self-antigen are dangerous because they could attack the body's own tissues, so they must be eliminated, not allowed to mature.
- **B)** Correct. Double-positive thymocytes that strongly bind self-antigen presented on MHC undergo negative selection (clonal deletion) via apoptosis. This process eliminates potentially autoreactive T-cells and is essential for establishing central tolerance, preventing autoimmune disease.
- **C)** Incorrect. Receptor editing (replacing the light chain in B-cells or, rarely, the TCR alpha chain) is primarily a mechanism of B-cell tolerance in the bone marrow. While some TCR alpha chain re-rearrangement may occur, negative selection via apoptosis is the dominant mechanism for eliminating strongly self-reactive thymocytes.
- **D)** Incorrect. While some self-reactive thymocytes can be diverted to become regulatory T-cells (Tregs), this is not the exclusive or primary fate of strongly self-reactive thymocytes. The predominant outcome of strong self-antigen recognition is apoptosis through negative selection.

### Q19. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Germinal centers are located within B-cell follicles of the cortex and contain proliferating B-cells (centroblasts and centrocytes), not diffuse T-cell populations. They also lack high endothelial venules.
