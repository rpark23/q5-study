**Q1.** A 72-year-old man is found to have an M-spike of 1.8 g/dL on serum protein electrophoresis. Bone marrow biopsy shows 6% clonal plasma cells. He has normal renal function, calcium, hemoglobin, and no bone lesions on imaging. Which of the following is the most appropriate diagnosis?

- A) Smoldering myeloma
- B) Active multiple myeloma
- C) Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)
- D) Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Smoldering myeloma requires either an M-spike of 3 g/dL or higher, or 10% or more clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow. This patient has an M-spike of only 1.8 g/dL and 6% plasma cells, both below the thresholds for smoldering myeloma.
- **B)** Incorrect. Active multiple myeloma requires either end-organ damage (CRAB criteria: hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia, bone lesions) or one of the SLiM biomarkers. This patient has none of these features, ruling out active myeloma.
- **C)** Correct. MGUS is defined by an M-spike less than 3 g/dL, fewer than 10% clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow, and the absence of end-organ damage (CRAB criteria). This patient meets all three criteria, making MGUS the appropriate diagnosis.
- **D)** Incorrect. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia is specifically associated with an IgM paraprotein and lymphoplasmacytic infiltration of the bone marrow, often with the MYD88 L265P mutation. The question does not specify an IgM isotype, and the clinical presentation is more consistent with MGUS.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A rate of less than 0.1% per year significantly underestimates the progression risk from MGUS. At that rate, monitoring would be far less important than it actually is in clinical practice.
- **B)** Correct. MGUS progresses to a malignant plasma cell disorder (multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, or related conditions) at approximately 1% per year, and this risk persists indefinitely without a plateau. This is why lifelong monitoring is recommended for all MGUS patients.
- **C)** Incorrect. A 10% annual progression rate would make MGUS a far more urgent condition requiring early intervention. The actual rate of approximately 1% per year supports a watch-and-wait approach with regular monitoring rather than immediate treatment.
- **D)** Incorrect. A 25% annual rate would mean the majority of MGUS patients would develop malignancy within a few years, which does not reflect clinical reality. MGUS is considered a premalignant condition with a low but persistent annual risk of progression.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. MGUS requires an M-spike less than 3 g/dL and fewer than 10% marrow plasma cells. This patient has an M-spike of 3.5 g/dL and 15% plasma cells, both exceeding the MGUS thresholds. Even though the M-spike could theoretically be benign, the quantitative criteria place this beyond MGUS.
- **B)** Correct. Smoldering myeloma is defined by an M-spike of 3 g/dL or higher, and/or 10% or more clonal bone marrow plasma cells, in the absence of CRAB features (hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia, bone lesions) or SLiM criteria. This patient meets both the M-spike and marrow thresholds but has no end-organ damage, making smoldering myeloma the correct diagnosis.
- **C)** Incorrect. Active multiple myeloma requires either CRAB features or SLiM biomarkers in addition to the clonal plasma cell population. This patient has no end-organ damage or myeloma-defining events, so she does not meet criteria for active disease and does not require immediate treatment.
- **D)** Incorrect. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia is a lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma that produces an IgM paraprotein. There is no mention of IgM isotype, lymphoplasmacytic morphology, or MYD88 mutation, so this diagnosis is not supported.

### Q4. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. The SLiM criteria are three biomarkers that define active myeloma even without traditional CRAB features: Sixty percent or more bone marrow plasma cells (S), involved-to-uninvolved serum free Light chain ratio of 100 or more (Li), and more than one focal lesion on MRI (M). Any single SLiM criterion is sufficient to diagnose active myeloma requiring treatment.
- **B)** Incorrect. Serum albumin, beta-2 microglobulin, and LDH are components of the Revised International Staging System (R-ISS) used for prognostic stratification of already-diagnosed myeloma, not for defining whether myeloma is active. These markers do not constitute the SLiM criteria.
- **C)** Incorrect. Splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, immunoparesis, and monocytosis are not part of the SLiM criteria. Splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy are more characteristic of lymphoproliferative disorders such as Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia or CLL, not myeloma.
- **D)** Incorrect. Skin rash, liver enzyme elevation, interstitial lung disease, and myopathy are not related to myeloma diagnostic criteria. These findings might suggest a systemic autoimmune condition or a drug reaction, not the SLiM criteria for myeloma.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. This reverses the roles of the two tests. SPEP (serum protein electrophoresis) separates serum proteins by electrophoretic mobility and detects/quantifies the M-spike as an abnormal band. Immunofixation is the test that identifies the specific immunoglobulin isotype (heavy and light chain type), not SPEP.
- **B)** Correct. SPEP detects and quantifies the M-spike (monoclonal protein) as a discrete band on the electrophoresis gel. Immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE) then identifies the specific heavy chain class (IgG, IgA, IgM) and light chain type (kappa or lambda) of the monoclonal protein. Both tests are used together in the initial diagnostic workup.
- **C)** Incorrect. The serum free light chain assay is an important adjunct test, especially for detecting light-chain-only myeloma or non-secretory disease where SPEP may be negative. However, it does not replace SPEP, which remains essential for detecting and quantifying intact immunoglobulin M-spikes.
- **D)** Incorrect. Immunofixation is used both for initial diagnosis (to characterize the type of monoclonal protein) and during follow-up (to confirm complete response, when the M-spike disappears on SPEP). It is a critical component of the initial workup, not limited to treatment monitoring.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy is common in myeloma patients (particularly from bortezomib or thalidomide), the question specifically asks about neuropathy mediated by the paraprotein itself, not by treatment. Drug-related neuropathy is a separate mechanism from paraprotein-mediated nerve damage.
- **B)** Correct. Monoclonal proteins can function as autoantibodies directed against nerve components, such as myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG), gangliosides, or other peripheral nerve antigens. This antibody-mediated attack on nerve structures causes demyelination or axonal damage, resulting in paraprotein-mediated peripheral neuropathy. This can occur in MGUS, myeloma, or WM.
- **C)** Incorrect. While hypercalcemia can cause neurologic symptoms (confusion, lethargy, weakness), it does not cause a true peripheral neuropathy with abnormal nerve conduction studies. The question describes a structurally confirmed neuropathy attributed to the paraprotein, not a metabolic effect of elevated calcium.
- **D)** Incorrect. Spinal nerve root compression from vertebral bone lesions (radiculopathy) can occur in myeloma, but this would present with a dermatomal distribution and would be identified on imaging, not attributed to the paraprotein. The question specifically indicates the physician suspects a paraprotein-mediated mechanism, not a compressive one.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Myeloma cells do not directly invade and digest bone through their own enzymatic activity. Instead, they cause bone destruction indirectly by altering the bone marrow microenvironment and signaling to bone-remodeling cells (osteoclasts and osteoblasts).
- **B)** Correct. Myeloma cells interact with the bone marrow microenvironment to activate osteoclasts (via RANKL and other cytokines) while simultaneously suppressing osteoblast function (via DKK1 and sclerostin). This uncoupled bone remodeling produces purely lytic lesions without a compensatory blastic response, which is also why standard bone scans (which detect osteoblast activity) are unreliable in myeloma.
- **C)** Incorrect. Myeloma cells produce monoclonal immunoglobulins, but these paraproteins do not function as autoantibodies targeting bone matrix proteins. The mechanism of bone destruction is through cytokine-mediated activation of osteoclasts and suppression of osteoblasts, not through autoantibody-directed bone damage.
- **D)** Incorrect. PTHrP secretion is the classic mechanism of humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy seen in solid tumors (e.g., squamous cell carcinoma). In myeloma, bone destruction and hypercalcemia are driven by local osteoclast activation within the marrow microenvironment, not by systemic PTHrP production.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Albumin is not involved in myeloma cast nephropathy. Free light chains in the renal tubules interact with a specific tubular mucoprotein, not with filtered albumin, to form the obstructing intratubular casts.
- **B)** Correct. Cast nephropathy (myeloma kidney) results from free light chains binding to Tamm-Horsfall protein (uromodulin) in the distal tubule and loop of Henle. This interaction forms large, waxy, obstructing casts that damage the tubular epithelium and lead to acute kidney injury. Dehydration and acidic pH promote cast formation.
- **C)** Incorrect. Beta-2 microglobulin is a prognostic serum marker in myeloma that reflects tumor burden and renal function, but it is not the protein that interacts with light chains to form intratubular casts. The cast-forming interaction is specifically between free light chains and Tamm-Horsfall protein.
- **D)** Incorrect. Transferrin is an iron-transport protein and is not involved in the pathogenesis of myeloma cast nephropathy. The specific interaction responsible for cast formation occurs between monoclonal free light chains and Tamm-Horsfall protein in the renal tubules.

### Q9. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Lytic bone lesions are characteristic of multiple myeloma, not Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia. WM typically does not cause bone destruction, and the IgM paraprotein with lymphoplasmacytic morphology and MYD88 mutation all point away from myeloma.
- **B)** Incorrect. Cast nephropathy is primarily caused by free light chains binding to Tamm-Horsfall protein in the renal tubules, a complication more characteristic of multiple myeloma. While WM can affect the kidneys, the dominant clinical issue caused by high IgM levels is hyperviscosity, not cast nephropathy.
- **C)** Correct. IgM is a large pentameric immunoglobulin that markedly increases serum viscosity at high concentrations. Hyperviscosity syndrome manifests with blurred vision (from retinal vein engorgement), mucosal bleeding (epistaxis), and neurologic symptoms (fatigue, headache, confusion). This is a characteristic and sometimes life-threatening complication of Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia.
- **D)** Incorrect. While amyloid deposition can occur in the setting of plasma cell dyscrasias (AL amyloidosis), the acute symptoms described here -- blurred vision, epistaxis, and fatigue with a very high IgM level -- are classic for hyperviscosity syndrome, not amyloidosis. Amyloidosis typically presents with organ-specific dysfunction (nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, neuropathy) developing over a longer timeframe.

### Q10. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Lytic bone lesions and hypercalcemia are hallmarks of multiple myeloma, not Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia. WM does not typically cause osteolytic bone destruction because the lymphoplasmacytic cells do not activate osteoclasts in the same way myeloma plasma cells do.
- **B)** Incorrect. Cast nephropathy with renal failure is a characteristic complication of multiple myeloma (caused by free light chain deposition in renal tubules), not WM. While WM can cause renal disease, it is less common and occurs through different mechanisms (e.g., IgM deposition).
- **C)** Correct. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia characteristically presents with lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly (reflecting its lymphomatous nature as a lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma), hyperviscosity symptoms (from the large IgM pentamer), and notably lacks the lytic bone disease seen in myeloma. This constellation distinguishes WM from multiple myeloma.
- **D)** Incorrect. Rouleaux formation (stacking of red blood cells) on peripheral smear can be seen in both myeloma and WM, as it results from elevated serum protein levels causing red cells to aggregate. It does not discriminate between the two diagnoses.

### Q11. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A graft-versus-myeloma effect is a feature of allogeneic (donor) stem cell transplant, not autologous transplant. In autologous transplant, the patient receives their own stem cells, so there is no immunologic graft-versus-tumor effect.
- **B)** Correct. In autologous stem cell transplant for myeloma, the patient's own stem cells are collected (usually from peripheral blood after mobilization), followed by administration of high-dose melphalan (a dose that would otherwise be marrow-ablative). The reinfused stem cells then rescue bone marrow function. This approach deepens the treatment response but is not considered curative, and maintenance therapy (e.g., lenalidomide) is typically continued afterward.
- **C)** Incorrect. Autologous transplant in myeloma deepens the response and extends progression-free survival, but it is not curative in the majority of patients. Most patients will eventually relapse, which is why maintenance therapy (typically lenalidomide) is standard after transplant.
- **D)** Incorrect. In autologous transplant for myeloma, stem cells are collected from the peripheral blood (after mobilization with G-CSF with or without plerixafor), not surgically from the bone marrow. The cells are cryopreserved, then the patient receives high-dose melphalan before the stem cells are reinfused -- it is not an immediate same-day procedure.

### Q12. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. CD20 is a B-cell marker targeted by rituximab and is expressed on mature B-cells and B-cell lymphomas, but it is not expressed on terminally differentiated plasma cells or myeloma cells. CAR-T therapy directed at CD20 would not effectively target myeloma.
- **B)** Incorrect. CD19 is a pan-B-cell marker that is the target for CAR-T therapy in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (e.g., tisagenlecleucel) and certain B-cell lymphomas. However, myeloma cells generally lose CD19 expression during plasma cell differentiation, making it an ineffective target for myeloma.
- **C)** Correct. BCMA (B-cell maturation antigen) is highly expressed on the surface of malignant plasma cells and is the target for the CAR-T cell therapies approved for relapsed/refractory myeloma, including idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel) and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel). BCMA is a member of the TNF receptor superfamily and plays a role in plasma cell survival.
- **D)** Incorrect. CD38 is indeed highly expressed on myeloma cells and is the target of the monoclonal antibody daratumumab, which is a mainstay of myeloma therapy. However, CD38 is not the target of the currently approved CAR-T constructs in myeloma, which are directed against BCMA. CD38-directed CAR-T is under investigation but is not the standard approach.
