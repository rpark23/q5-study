## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Coagulation factors for the intrinsic pathway are produced primarily by the liver, not by platelets. Platelets contribute to hemostasis through plug formation and by providing a phospholipid surface for coagulation factor assembly, but they do not synthesize these factors.
- **B)** Correct. Platelets are integral to primary hemostasis. They seal damaged endothelium by adhering to exposed subendothelial collagen via von Willebrand factor (VWF) through the GPIb-IX-V receptor, then aggregate with other platelets through GP-IIb/IIIa to form the initial platelet plug.
- **C)** Incorrect. The fibrinolytic system is activated by tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and involves plasmin to dissolve clots. Platelets do not activate fibrinolysis; rather, they promote clot formation, which is the opposite function.
- **D)** Incorrect. Antibody production is the function of plasma cells (terminally differentiated B-cells), not platelets. Platelets are anucleate cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes and do not produce immunoglobulins.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not undergo conventional mitotic cell divisions to expand their numbers. They replicate DNA without dividing (endomitosis), which is a fundamental distinction in megakaryocyte biology that explains how one cell can produce thousands of platelets.
- **B)** Correct. Megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis, a process of DNA replication without cell division that produces polyploid cells reaching a mean ploidy of 16N (and up to 64N). This massive DNA amplification drives cytoplasmic expansion, allowing a single megakaryocyte to extend proplatelet processes into bone marrow sinusoids and release 2,000-4,000 platelets.
- **C)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not fuse with surrounding cells to enlarge. Their large size (5-20 times that of a red blood cell) results entirely from endomitosis, not cell fusion.
- **D)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not absorb circulating proteins to increase their size. Their growth results from internal DNA replication (endomitosis) driving cytoplasmic expansion.

### Q3. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Erythropoietin (EPO) is the primary growth factor for red blood cell production (erythropoiesis), not platelet production. It is produced by the kidneys in response to hypoxia and stimulates erythroid progenitors in the bone marrow.
- **B)** Incorrect. G-CSF stimulates the production and differentiation of neutrophils (granulopoiesis), not platelets. It is used clinically to treat neutropenia, not thrombocytopenia.
- **C)** Correct. Thrombopoietin (TPO) is the major regulator of megakaryocyte development and platelet production. It is constitutively produced by the liver and kidney, binds to the c-MPL receptor on megakaryocytes, and activates the JAK2 signaling pathway. A negative feedback loop regulates TPO levels based on total platelet mass.
- **D)** Incorrect. Interleukin-2 is a cytokine important for T-cell proliferation and immune regulation, not platelet production. While some interleukins (IL-3, IL-6, IL-11) contribute to early megakaryocyte differentiation, TPO is the primary regulator.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A count of 50,000-100,000 per microliter represents thrombocytopenia, not the normal range. This level of thrombocytopenia can be seen in conditions such as hypersplenism or mild ITP.
- **B)** Correct. The normal platelet count range is 150,000-450,000 per microliter, with an average of approximately 250,000. Counts below 150,000 define thrombocytopenia, and counts above 450,000 define thrombocytosis.
- **C)** Incorrect. A count of 500,000-800,000 per microliter represents thrombocytosis, which can be reactive (infection, inflammation, iron deficiency) or clonal (myeloproliferative neoplasms such as essential thrombocythemia).
- **D)** Incorrect. A count of 1,000,000-2,000,000 per microliter represents marked thrombocytosis, which is significantly above the normal range and can be seen in severe reactive states or myeloproliferative neoplasms.

### Q5. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. A lifespan of 24-48 hours is too short for normal platelets. This is closer to the dramatically shortened platelet lifespan seen in severe immune thrombocytopenic purpura, where antibody-coated platelets are rapidly cleared.
- **B)** Incorrect. While 3-5 days is within the general range, the accepted normal platelet lifespan is 7-10 days. Platelets survive in circulation for this period before being removed by the reticuloendothelial system.
- **C)** Correct. Normal platelet lifespan is 7-10 days. After this period, platelets that have not been consumed in hemostatic processes are removed from circulation. This lifespan, combined with limited bone marrow reserve, means thrombocytopenia develops rapidly if production fails.
- **D)** Incorrect. A 21-30 day lifespan is far longer than the actual platelet lifespan. This duration is closer to the lifespan of red blood cells (approximately 120 days), not platelets.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Hemarthroses (joint bleeding) and deep muscle hematomas are characteristic of secondary hemostasis defects, particularly coagulation factor deficiencies such as hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) or hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency), not platelet disorders.
- **B)** Correct. Mucocutaneous bleeding -- including petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, and menorrhagia -- is the hallmark bleeding pattern of primary hemostasis defects (platelet quantity or function disorders and von Willebrand disease). Petechiae are virtually pathognomonic for a platelet or primary hemostasis defect.
- **C)** Incorrect. Delayed bleeding after initial hemostasis is more characteristic of coagulation factor deficiencies (secondary hemostasis defects), where the initial platelet plug forms normally but the fibrin clot fails to stabilize it.
- **D)** Incorrect. Large retroperitoneal hematomas are deep tissue bleeds more consistent with coagulation factor deficiencies or anticoagulant use, not with platelet disorders. Platelet disorders cause superficial mucocutaneous bleeding.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While infection and malignancy are specific causes of thrombocytopenia, they are not the two broad mechanistic categories. The fundamental framework for thrombocytopenia classifies causes by whether the problem is decreased production or increased destruction/sequestration.
- **B)** Correct. Thrombocytopenia results from either decreased production (bone marrow failure, infiltration, congenital syndromes, drug suppression) or increased destruction/sequestration (immune-mediated, consumptive, or splenic sequestration). This framework guides the clinical evaluation and workup.
- **C)** Incorrect. Genetic mutations and drug toxicity are specific causes within the broader categories but do not represent the two fundamental mechanistic categories. A genetic mutation could cause either decreased production or increased destruction depending on the specific disorder.
- **D)** Incorrect. Iron deficiency and vitamin B12 deficiency are more commonly associated with anemia (iron deficiency anemia, megaloblastic anemia) than with thrombocytopenia as a primary finding. They do not represent the two fundamental categories of thrombocytopenia.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Childhood ITP is typically self-limited, with the majority of children (approximately 70-80%) experiencing spontaneous resolution within 6 months. Chronic ITP is more commonly seen in adults, not children.
- **B)** Correct. Childhood ITP typically follows a viral infection due to molecular mimicry, in which the immune stimulus triggers cross-reactive antiplatelet antibodies. Most cases resolve spontaneously within months, distinguishing childhood ITP from adult ITP, which more commonly follows a chronic course.
- **C)** Incorrect. Splenectomy is not an immediate treatment for childhood ITP. Because most cases resolve spontaneously, observation or temporizing measures (IVIG, corticosteroids) are the initial approach. Splenectomy is reserved for chronic, refractory cases.
- **D)** Incorrect. ITP is caused by immune-mediated peripheral destruction of platelets, not bone marrow failure. The bone marrow in ITP shows adequate or increased megakaryocytes, reflecting compensatory increased production in response to peripheral destruction.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Factor VIII is a coagulation factor in the intrinsic pathway, and its deficiency causes hemophilia A, not TTP. TTP involves a defect in VWF processing, not coagulation factor deficiency.
- **B)** Correct. ADAMTS13 is a metalloprotease that cleaves ultra-large von Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers into smaller, less thrombogenic forms. Deficiency of ADAMTS13 (either acquired through autoantibodies or congenital through mutations) leads to accumulation of ultra-large VWF multimers that spontaneously aggregate platelets in small vessels, causing thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. Cyclooxygenase (COX) is the enzyme inhibited by aspirin, which blocks thromboxane A2 synthesis and impairs platelet activation. COX deficiency does not cause TTP.
- **D)** Incorrect. Thrombin is a key enzyme in the coagulation cascade that converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Its deficiency or dysfunction does not cause TTP, which is a disorder of VWF processing and platelet aggregation in the microvasculature.

### Q10. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Bernard-Soulier syndrome is a congenital macrothrombocytopenia caused by deficiency of the GPIb-IX-V receptor. It presents with large platelets and a platelet adhesion defect but does not cause eczema or immunodeficiency.
- **B)** Incorrect. Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a qualitative platelet disorder caused by GP-IIb/IIIa deficiency. It presents with mucocutaneous bleeding but characteristically has normal platelet counts and size. It does not cause eczema or immunodeficiency.
- **C)** Correct. Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome is an X-linked recessive disorder presenting with the classic triad of thrombocytopenia with characteristically small platelets (microthrombocytopenia), eczema, and immunodeficiency. The small platelet size is a key distinguishing feature from other congenital thrombocytopenias.
- **D)** Incorrect. TAR syndrome presents with thrombocytopenia and bilateral absence of the radius bones. It does not cause eczema or immunodeficiency, and the thrombocytopenia typically resolves by school age.
