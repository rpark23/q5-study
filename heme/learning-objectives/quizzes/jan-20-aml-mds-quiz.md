# Quiz: AML and MDS
### Lecture by Gabriel Mannis and Jason Gotlib (January 20, Part 1)

---

**Q1.** A 72-year-old man presents with fatigue, easy bruising, and recurrent infections over the past 3 months. CBC reveals WBC 2.1, Hgb 9.2, and platelets 68K. Bone marrow biopsy shows 8% blasts with dysplastic changes in all three lineages. Which of the following best describes the distinction between this patient's diagnosis and AML?

- A) AML requires the presence of Auer rods, whereas this condition does not
- B) AML is defined by >=20% blasts in the marrow (or characteristic translocations), and this patient's blast count is below that threshold
- C) AML presents exclusively in younger patients, whereas this disease affects the elderly
- D) AML always presents with marked leukocytosis, which is absent here

**Q2.** A 45-year-old woman presents with DIC, bleeding gums, and pancytopenia. Bone marrow biopsy reveals hypergranular promyelocytes with numerous Auer rods. Cytogenetics show t(15;17). Which of the following statements about this patient's disease is most accurate?

- A) This is the most common subtype of AML and carries a poor prognosis
- B) The translocation produces the PML-RARalpha fusion, and treatment with ATRA plus arsenic trioxide yields cure rates exceeding 80-90%
- C) Immediate intensive "7+3" chemotherapy is required, as targeted therapy is ineffective
- D) The DIC will resolve only after the blast count normalizes with chemotherapy

**Q3.** A 68-year-old man with newly diagnosed AML has a WBC of 125,000 with 80% blasts. He is dyspneic and confused. A chest X-ray shows bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. Which of the following should be AVOIDED in the immediate management of this patient?

- A) Hydroxyurea to rapidly reduce the white cell count
- B) Leukapheresis to mechanically remove circulating blasts
- C) Packed red blood cell transfusion to correct anemia
- D) Initiation of induction chemotherapy

**Q4.** The progression from normal hematopoiesis to AML often follows a stepwise accumulation of mutations. Which of the following correctly orders this progression?

- A) MDS --> CHIP --> CCUS --> AML
- B) CCUS --> CHIP --> MDS --> AML
- C) CHIP --> CCUS --> MDS --> AML
- D) CHIP --> MDS --> CCUS --> AML

**Q5.** A 60-year-old woman with AML undergoes cytogenetic and molecular testing. Her karyotype shows t(8;21), and molecular testing reveals no FLT3-ITD mutation. How does this genetic profile influence her prognosis and treatment?

- A) This is a poor-risk finding, and allogeneic stem cell transplant in first remission is recommended
- B) This is a favorable-risk finding; consolidation with high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC) may be sufficient without transplant in first remission
- C) This translocation is only seen in MDS and suggests the diagnosis should be revised
- D) FLT3 inhibitors should be added to her regimen given this cytogenetic profile

**Q6.** A patient with MDS is found to have an isolated deletion of chromosome 5q [del(5q)]. She is transfusion-dependent with a hemoglobin of 8.5 g/dL. Which targeted therapy is specifically indicated for this cytogenetic abnormality in lower-risk MDS?

- A) Azacitidine (hypomethylating agent)
- B) Lenalidomide
- C) Venetoclax
- D) Ruxolitinib

**Q7.** Standard induction therapy for fit patients with AML is commonly referred to as "7+3." Which of the following correctly describes this regimen?

- A) 7 days of an anthracycline plus 3 days of cytarabine
- B) 7 days of cytarabine plus 3 days of an anthracycline
- C) 7 cycles of low-dose cytarabine plus 3 cycles of consolidation
- D) 7 days of ATRA plus 3 days of arsenic trioxide

**Q8.** An 80-year-old man with multiple comorbidities is diagnosed with AML. He is deemed unfit for intensive induction chemotherapy. Which of the following regimens has become a standard of care for unfit AML patients?

- A) Hydroxyurea monotherapy for cytoreduction
- B) Low-dose cytarabine alone
- C) Hypomethylating agent (azacitidine or decitabine) combined with venetoclax
- D) Allogeneic stem cell transplant with reduced-intensity conditioning

**Q9.** Which of the following clinical features best distinguishes MDS from aplastic anemia, given that both can present with pancytopenia?

- A) MDS presents exclusively in patients over age 80
- B) MDS shows morphologic dysplasia and clonal cytogenetic abnormalities on bone marrow examination, whereas aplastic anemia shows a hypocellular marrow without dysplasia
- C) Aplastic anemia always presents with an elevated reticulocyte count
- D) MDS is always associated with marked leukocytosis

**Q10.** A 55-year-old man treated with alkylating agents for a prior malignancy 6 years ago now presents with pancytopenia. Bone marrow shows 25% blasts with complex cytogenetics including deletions of chromosomes 5 and 7. Which of the following best characterizes his disease?

- A) De novo AML with favorable-risk cytogenetics likely to respond well to standard induction
- B) Therapy-related AML, which is associated with complex/unfavorable cytogenetics and a poor prognosis
- C) MDS that is unlikely to progress to AML given the blast percentage
- D) Chronic myelogenous leukemia in blast crisis

**Q11.** Approximately 30% of MDS cases progress to AML. Which of the following mechanisms most directly underlies this transformation?

- A) Acquisition of additional somatic mutations that confer a proliferative advantage to the dysplastic clone
- B) Immune exhaustion from chronic pancytopenia leading to unchecked blast proliferation
- C) Viral infection triggering blast transformation in dysplastic progenitors
- D) Telomere lengthening in MDS stem cells that prevents normal apoptosis

**Q12.** A 65-year-old man with AML in remission after induction has molecular testing showing a FLT3-ITD mutation. Which of the following best describes the role of FLT3 in AML management?

- A) FLT3-ITD is a favorable prognostic marker that eliminates the need for consolidation therapy
- B) FLT3-ITD confers intermediate-to-adverse risk, and FLT3 inhibitors (e.g., midostaurin, gilteritinib) can be incorporated into treatment
- C) FLT3 mutations are only relevant in MDS and do not affect AML prognosis
- D) FLT3-ITD indicates the patient should receive ATRA-based therapy

---

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Auer rods are crystallized granules found in myeloblasts and are suggestive of AML (particularly APL), but their presence is not required for diagnosis. AML is defined by blast percentage (>=20%) or by specific recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities, not by the presence of Auer rods.
- **B)** Correct. AML is defined by >=20% blasts in the bone marrow or peripheral blood, or by the presence of certain recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities [such as t(8;21), inv(16), or t(15;17)] regardless of blast count. This patient has 8% blasts with trilineage dysplasia, which is consistent with MDS, not AML.
- **C)** Incorrect. AML can occur at any age, including in elderly patients. While MDS is more common in older adults (median age ~70), AML is also common in the elderly. Age alone does not distinguish these diagnoses.
- **D)** Incorrect. AML does not always present with leukocytosis. Many patients with AML present with low or normal white blood cell counts (aleukemic leukemia), particularly when the marrow is packed with blasts that fail to enter the circulation efficiently. The distinguishing feature is blast percentage, not WBC count.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) with t(15;17) is actually one of the less common subtypes of AML, accounting for approximately 5-10% of cases. Far from carrying a poor prognosis, it has one of the best prognoses of any AML subtype when treated appropriately, with cure rates exceeding 80-90%.
- **B)** Correct. The t(15;17) translocation produces the PML-RARalpha fusion protein, which blocks myeloid differentiation at the promyelocyte stage. Treatment with ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid), which forces promyelocyte differentiation, plus arsenic trioxide, which degrades the fusion protein, is now the standard of care and achieves cure rates exceeding 80-90%. This is one of the greatest success stories in targeted cancer therapy.
- **C)** Incorrect. Standard "7+3" intensive chemotherapy is not the preferred first-line treatment for APL. The ATRA plus arsenic trioxide regimen is far more effective and less toxic for this specific subtype. In fact, for low-to-intermediate risk APL, chemotherapy can often be avoided entirely.
- **D)** Incorrect. The DIC in APL is caused by procoagulant substances released from the abnormal promyelocytes, not simply by the blast count. ATRA itself begins to resolve the coagulopathy quickly (within days of initiation), even before the blast count normalizes, by promoting differentiation of the abnormal promyelocytes.

### Q3. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Hydroxyurea is an appropriate intervention in leukostasis. It rapidly reduces the white cell count by inhibiting DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing blast cells, helping to alleviate the hyperviscosity and microvascular sludging that cause the patient's symptoms.
- **B)** Incorrect. Leukapheresis is an appropriate emergency intervention for symptomatic leukostasis. It mechanically removes circulating blasts to rapidly reduce the white cell burden while definitive chemotherapy is being initiated.
- **C)** Correct. In leukostasis, packed red blood cell transfusion should be avoided because increasing the red cell mass and blood viscosity worsens sludging of the large, sticky blasts in the microvasculature, exacerbating end-organ damage to the lungs and brain. The patient's dyspnea and confusion are from leukostasis, not anemia, so the priority is reducing blast count rather than increasing oxygen-carrying capacity.
- **D)** Incorrect. Initiation of induction chemotherapy is appropriate and necessary in this clinical scenario. Definitive treatment of the underlying AML is the most important intervention for leukostasis, as it addresses the root cause of blast overproduction.

### Q4. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. This sequence places MDS before CHIP, which is the reverse of the actual progression. CHIP (clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential) represents the earliest detectable step -- clonal mutations without any clinical consequence -- and must precede MDS.
- **B)** Incorrect. This sequence places CCUS before CHIP, which is incorrect. CHIP (clonal mutations without cytopenias) necessarily precedes CCUS (clonal mutations with cytopenias), as CCUS represents a further progression from the CHIP state.
- **C)** Correct. The stepwise progression is: CHIP (clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential -- somatic mutations in hematopoietic cells without cytopenias or dysplasia) --> CCUS (clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance -- clonal mutations now accompanied by cytopenias, but not yet meeting full MDS diagnostic criteria) --> MDS (morphologic dysplasia, cytopenias, and clonal abnormalities meeting diagnostic criteria) --> AML (>=20% blasts or defining translocations).
- **D)** Incorrect. This sequence places MDS before CCUS, which is incorrect. CCUS is an intermediate state between CHIP and MDS -- it has cytopenias and clonal mutations but does not yet meet the full diagnostic criteria for MDS (such as specific morphologic dysplasia or characteristic cytogenetic changes).

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The t(8;21) translocation is classified as favorable-risk, not poor-risk, in AML. It is one of the core-binding factor (CBF) translocations that respond well to chemotherapy, and allogeneic transplant in first remission is generally not recommended for favorable-risk patients because the risks of transplant outweigh the benefits.
- **B)** Correct. The t(8;21) is a core-binding factor translocation classified as favorable-risk in AML. Patients with this cytogenetic profile generally respond well to induction chemotherapy and benefit from consolidation with high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC). In the absence of adverse molecular features (like FLT3-ITD), allogeneic stem cell transplant is typically not pursued in first complete remission.
- **C)** Incorrect. The t(8;21) translocation is a well-characterized AML-associated abnormality, not specific to MDS. In fact, t(8;21) is one of the recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities that define AML regardless of blast count, so its presence confirms the AML diagnosis.
- **D)** Incorrect. FLT3 inhibitors are indicated when a FLT3 mutation (such as FLT3-ITD) is present. This patient is FLT3-ITD negative, so FLT3 inhibitors are not indicated. The t(8;21) translocation does not call for FLT3-targeted therapy.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Azacitidine is a hypomethylating agent used broadly in MDS (particularly higher-risk MDS), but it is not the specifically targeted therapy for del(5q) MDS. While it can be effective, lenalidomide has a unique and specific mechanism of action in this cytogenetic subtype.
- **B)** Correct. Lenalidomide is specifically effective in lower-risk MDS with isolated del(5q), achieving transfusion independence in approximately 67% of patients. Its mechanism involves targeting casein kinase 1 alpha (CK1alpha), which is haploinsufficient in del(5q) cells. Lenalidomide promotes degradation of CK1alpha through the cereblon E3 ubiquitin ligase, selectively killing the del(5q) clone while sparing normal hematopoiesis.
- **C)** Incorrect. Venetoclax is a BCL-2 inhibitor used primarily in AML (in combination with hypomethylating agents for unfit patients). It is not the standard targeted therapy for del(5q) MDS, although it is being investigated in various MDS settings.
- **D)** Incorrect. Ruxolitinib is a JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor used primarily in myeloproliferative neoplasms (particularly myelofibrosis and polycythemia vera). It does not have an established role in the treatment of del(5q) MDS.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. This reverses the components. The "7" refers to cytarabine (the drug given for the longer duration) and the "3" refers to the anthracycline (given for the shorter duration). Getting this backward is a common mistake, but the regimen is 7 days of cytarabine plus 3 days of anthracycline.
- **B)** Correct. "7+3" refers to 7 continuous days of cytarabine (ara-C) given as a continuous infusion, plus 3 days of an anthracycline (typically daunorubicin or idarubicin) given on days 1-3. This regimen has been the backbone of AML induction therapy for decades and achieves complete remission in 60-80% of younger, fit patients.
- **C)** Incorrect. "7+3" refers to a single induction cycle with specific drug durations (days), not to multiple cycles of therapy. The numbers describe days of drug administration within one induction course, not the number of treatment cycles.
- **D)** Incorrect. ATRA plus arsenic trioxide is the specific regimen for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) with t(15;17), not the standard "7+3" induction for AML broadly. These are fundamentally different treatment approaches for different disease subtypes.

### Q8. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Hydroxyurea monotherapy provides only temporary cytoreduction (lowering white blood cell counts) and does not induce remission or meaningfully improve survival in AML. It may be used as a bridge to definitive therapy but is not a standard of care treatment regimen.
- **B)** Incorrect. Low-dose cytarabine alone was historically used for unfit patients, but it has been largely supplanted by the combination of hypomethylating agents plus venetoclax, which demonstrated significantly superior response rates and survival outcomes in clinical trials.
- **C)** Correct. The combination of a hypomethylating agent (azacitidine or decitabine) with venetoclax (a BCL-2 inhibitor) has become the standard of care for older or unfit AML patients. The landmark VIALE-A trial showed that azacitidine plus venetoclax significantly improved complete remission rates and overall survival compared to azacitidine alone. This regimen is generally well-tolerated in patients who cannot withstand intensive "7+3" induction.
- **D)** Incorrect. Allogeneic stem cell transplant, even with reduced-intensity conditioning, is a highly intensive procedure that is generally not appropriate for an 80-year-old with multiple comorbidities. Transplant eligibility depends on overall fitness, organ function, and comorbidity burden, not just the use of reduced-intensity conditioning.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. MDS does not present exclusively in patients over age 80. While MDS is more common in the elderly (median age at diagnosis ~70), it can occur in younger patients as well, including cases of therapy-related MDS. Age alone is not a distinguishing feature between MDS and aplastic anemia.
- **B)** Correct. The key distinction lies in the bone marrow findings. MDS is characterized by morphologic dysplasia (abnormal-appearing cells in one or more lineages, such as hypolobated megakaryocytes, ring sideroblasts, or pseudo-Pelger-Huet neutrophils) and often clonal cytogenetic or molecular abnormalities. Aplastic anemia shows a markedly hypocellular (empty) marrow without dysplastic morphology, and it is typically not clonal (though overlap conditions exist).
- **C)** Incorrect. Aplastic anemia does not always present with an elevated reticulocyte count. In fact, the reticulocyte count in aplastic anemia is typically low because the bone marrow is failing to produce adequate red cell precursors. A low reticulocyte count in the setting of anemia is a hallmark of bone marrow failure.
- **D)** Incorrect. MDS is not associated with leukocytosis. MDS is characterized by cytopenias (low blood counts) due to ineffective hematopoiesis. Leukocytosis would be more suggestive of a myeloproliferative neoplasm or a reactive process.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. This is not de novo AML, nor does it have favorable cytogenetics. The history of prior alkylating agent exposure makes this therapy-related, and complex cytogenetics with deletions of chromosomes 5 and 7 are classified as adverse-risk. De novo AML with favorable-risk cytogenetics (e.g., core-binding factor translocations) carries a much better prognosis.
- **B)** Correct. This is therapy-related AML (t-AML), arising 5-7 years after exposure to alkylating agents. Alkylating agent-related t-AML characteristically presents with complex karyotypes, deletions of chromosomes 5 and 7, and often a preceding MDS-like phase. The prognosis is poor compared to de novo AML, with lower remission rates and shorter survival, and allogeneic transplant is often pursued if the patient is eligible.
- **C)** Incorrect. With 25% blasts, this patient meets the AML threshold (>=20% blasts), so the diagnosis is AML, not MDS. Furthermore, the presence of 25% blasts does not suggest MDS -- it definitively indicates AML by WHO criteria.
- **D)** Incorrect. CML in blast crisis would be characterized by the Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) and the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene, which is not described here. The clinical history of prior alkylating agent exposure and the cytogenetic pattern (deletions of 5 and 7) are classic for therapy-related AML, not CML.

### Q11. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. MDS-to-AML transformation occurs through clonal evolution -- the stepwise acquisition of additional somatic mutations (e.g., in RAS, FLT3, TP53, RUNX1) that shift the balance from ineffective hematopoiesis (characteristic of MDS) toward uncontrolled blast proliferation (characteristic of AML). Each new mutation can confer a survival or proliferative advantage, driving the expansion of an increasingly aggressive subclone.
- **B)** Incorrect. While immune dysfunction can occur in MDS, the transformation to AML is driven by intrinsic genetic changes within the malignant clone, not by immune exhaustion. The immune system does not directly prevent blast proliferation in a way that its exhaustion would trigger transformation.
- **C)** Incorrect. Viral infection is not a recognized mechanism of MDS-to-AML transformation. While certain viruses can contribute to hematologic malignancies in other contexts (e.g., HTLV-1 and adult T-cell leukemia), MDS-to-AML progression is driven by accumulation of somatic mutations within the dysplastic clone.
- **D)** Incorrect. Telomere lengthening would prevent cellular senescence, but this is not the mechanism of MDS-to-AML transformation. In fact, telomere shortening (not lengthening) is more commonly associated with genomic instability in MDS. The transformation is driven by the acquisition of new driver mutations, not by telomere dynamics.

### Q12. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. FLT3-ITD is not a favorable prognostic marker -- it is one of the most important adverse prognostic factors in AML. Patients with FLT3-ITD have higher relapse rates and shorter overall survival compared to FLT3-wild-type patients. Consolidation therapy (and often transplant) is critically important, not eliminated, in these patients.
- **B)** Correct. FLT3-ITD (internal tandem duplication) is one of the most common mutations in AML (~25-30% of cases) and confers an intermediate-to-adverse prognosis, particularly when the allelic ratio is high or when it occurs without concurrent NPM1 mutation. Targeted FLT3 inhibitors are now incorporated into standard management: midostaurin is added to induction chemotherapy for newly diagnosed patients, and gilteritinib is used as monotherapy for relapsed/refractory FLT3-mutated AML.
- **C)** Incorrect. FLT3 mutations are highly relevant in AML and are among the most commonly tested molecular markers at diagnosis. While FLT3 mutations can rarely occur in other contexts, their primary clinical significance is in AML prognostication and treatment selection. They are not primarily relevant to MDS.
- **D)** Incorrect. ATRA-based therapy is specifically used for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) with the PML-RARalpha fusion from t(15;17). FLT3-ITD has no relationship to APL biology, and the presence of FLT3-ITD calls for FLT3 inhibitor therapy, not ATRA.
