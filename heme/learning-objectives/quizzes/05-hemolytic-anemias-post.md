## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The liver does not stop producing haptoglobin during hemolysis. In fact, haptoglobin is an acute phase reactant, meaning its production can actually increase during inflammation. The decrease in serum haptoglobin is due to consumption, not decreased synthesis.
- **B)** Correct. When red blood cells lyse (intravascular hemolysis), free hemoglobin is released into the plasma. Haptoglobin binds this free hemoglobin, and the haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex is irreversibly cleared by macrophages in the reticuloendothelial system (liver and spleen). During active hemolysis, haptoglobin is consumed faster than the liver can replenish it, leading to decreased or undetectable levels.
- **C)** Incorrect. Haptoglobin is an acute phase reactant, meaning it rises during inflammation, not is consumed by it. Its decrease in hemolysis is specifically because it binds free hemoglobin and the complex is cleared, not because of an inflammatory consumptive process.
- **D)** Incorrect. Free hemoglobin does not directly degrade haptoglobin through oxidative damage. The mechanism is a binding reaction: haptoglobin forms a stable complex with free hemoglobin, and this complex is then recognized and cleared by hepatic and splenic macrophages through receptor-mediated endocytosis.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Alpha thalassemia trait corresponds to two functioning alpha-globin genes (not one), and it typically shows a normal hemoglobin electrophoresis. With two gene deletions, alpha chain production is reduced but not severely enough to generate detectable abnormal hemoglobins.
- **B)** Correct. One functioning alpha-globin gene (genotype --/-alpha) corresponds to HbH disease. With severely reduced alpha chain production, excess beta chains that cannot find alpha partners form beta-4 tetramers called HbH. HbH is an unstable hemoglobin detectable on electrophoresis and causes a moderate to severe hemolytic anemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. Hydrops fetalis occurs when there are zero functioning alpha-globin genes (genotype --/--), not one. In this lethal condition, no alpha chains are produced, so in fetal life, excess gamma chains form gamma-4 tetramers called Hb Bart's. This condition is incompatible with extrauterine life.
- **D)** Incorrect. The silent carrier state corresponds to three functioning alpha-globin genes (one gene deleted), not one. Silent carriers are clinically normal with normal or near-normal hematologic parameters. HbA2 is not characteristically elevated in alpha thalassemia; elevated HbA2 is the hallmark of beta thalassemia trait.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Maternal antibodies (IgG crossing the placenta) do not protect against the hemolysis of thalassemia. The problem in beta thalassemia major is an intrinsic defect in globin chain synthesis, not an immune-mediated process that antibodies could modulate.
- **B)** Correct. At birth, fetal hemoglobin (HbF = alpha2-gamma2) predominates and does not require beta-globin chains. Over the first 3-6 months of life, the physiologic hemoglobin switch from gamma-globin to beta-globin expression occurs. In beta thalassemia major, the inability to produce adequate beta chains only becomes clinically apparent as the body attempts this switch, HbF declines, and severe anemia develops.
- **C)** Incorrect. The beta-globin gene is present and transcriptionally active from early development. The issue is not that beta-globin production begins at 3-6 months, but rather that the gamma-to-beta globin switching process shifts the predominant hemoglobin from HbF (which does not need beta chains) to HbA (which requires beta chains), unmasking the deficiency.
- **D)** Incorrect. While the liver is the primary site of erythropoiesis in early fetal life, by birth the bone marrow has largely taken over. The timing of symptom onset is driven by the hemoglobin switch from gamma to beta chains, not by a transition in the site of red cell production.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. HbS is the hemoglobin variant found in sickle cell disease (caused by a point mutation in the beta-globin gene), not in beta thalassemia trait. Beta thalassemia involves reduced quantity of structurally normal beta chains, while sickle cell disease involves a qualitatively abnormal beta chain.
- **B)** Correct. In beta thalassemia trait (beta thal minor), reduced beta chain production leads to a relative excess of alpha chains. Some of these excess alpha chains pair with delta chains to form HbA2 (alpha2-delta2). Elevated HbA2 (>3.5%) on hemoglobin electrophoresis is the diagnostic hallmark of beta thalassemia trait and is the key feature that distinguishes it from iron deficiency, which can also cause microcytosis.
- **C)** Incorrect. HbH (beta-4 tetramers) is found in HbH disease, which is a form of alpha thalassemia (one functioning alpha gene), not beta thalassemia. In beta thalassemia, beta chains are reduced, so excess beta tetramers would not form.
- **D)** Incorrect. Absent HbA with elevated HbF describes beta thalassemia major (homozygous or compound heterozygous), not beta thalassemia trait. In beta thal major, there is no or severely reduced beta chain production, so HbA (alpha2-beta2) is absent and HbF (alpha2-gamma2) compensatorily predominates. In beta thal trait, HbA is still the dominant hemoglobin.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Valine is actually smaller than glutamic acid, not larger. The pathology is not due to steric clashes within the tetramer but rather due to the change in surface chemistry that promotes intermolecular interactions between hemoglobin molecules.
- **B)** Correct. Glutamic acid at position 6 is a hydrophilic, negatively charged amino acid on the surface of the beta chain. The substitution to valine introduces a hydrophobic, nonpolar residue. In the deoxygenated (T) state, this hydrophobic valine residue is exposed and fits into a complementary hydrophobic pocket on an adjacent hemoglobin molecule, creating intermolecular contacts that drive polymerization of HbS into long, rigid fibers that deform the red cell into a sickle shape.
- **C)** Incorrect. Valine is a nonpolar, uncharged amino acid -- it does not have a positive charge and does not form ionic bonds. The critical property is its hydrophobicity, which creates a "sticky patch" that drives non-covalent hydrophobic interactions between HbS molecules in the deoxy state.
- **D)** Incorrect. The valine substitution does not destabilize the alpha-beta interface or cause hemoglobin to dissociate into monomers. HbS remains a stable tetramer. The polymerization occurs through intermolecular contacts between intact HbS tetramers, driven by the hydrophobic interaction at the beta-6 position.

### Q6. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Vaso-occlusive crisis presents with bone pain, chest pain, or abdominal pain from microvascular occlusion by sickled cells, but it does not typically cause rapid splenic enlargement or an acute drop in hemoglobin of this magnitude. The acute splenic enlargement and severe anemia point specifically to splenic sequestration.
- **B)** Incorrect. Acute chest syndrome presents with fever, respiratory symptoms, chest pain, and a new pulmonary infiltrate on imaging. It does not present with rapid splenic enlargement and a dramatic hemoglobin drop as the primary features. The clinical scenario described is more consistent with a splenic event.
- **C)** Correct. Splenic sequestration crisis is characterized by sudden trapping of large volumes of blood within the spleen, causing rapid splenic enlargement, a precipitous drop in hemoglobin (often >2 g/dL from baseline), and potentially life-threatening hypovolemic shock. The immediate priorities are emergent volume resuscitation and red cell transfusion. This complication is most common in young children with sickle cell disease before autosplenectomy occurs.
- **D)** Incorrect. Aplastic crisis from parvovirus B19 causes severe anemia with reticulocytopenia (the marrow stops making red cells), but it does not cause rapid splenic enlargement. The hallmark of aplastic crisis is an absent reticulocyte response, whereas splenic sequestration shows abrupt splenic enlargement with a preserved or elevated reticulocyte count.

### Q7. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Horizontal membrane protein defects (such as spectrin dimer self-association defects or protein 4.1 deficiency) cause hereditary elliptocytosis, not spherocytosis. Horizontal interactions maintain the structural integrity of the membrane skeleton in the plane of the membrane, and their disruption produces elongated elliptical cells, not spherocytes.
- **B)** Incorrect. Autoantibody-mediated red cell destruction (autoimmune hemolytic anemia) would produce a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs test). This patient's DAT is negative, ruling out an autoimmune etiology. The spherocytes here are from an intrinsic membrane defect, not from antibody coating.
- **C)** Correct. Hereditary spherocytosis is caused by defects in vertical membrane interactions -- proteins that anchor the lipid bilayer to the underlying spectrin skeleton (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, protein 4.2). These defects cause progressive loss of unsupported membrane lipid bilayer (membrane vesiculation) without proportional loss of cell volume, forcing the cell into a sphere with decreased surface area-to-volume ratio. Spherocytes are rigid and are trapped and destroyed in the splenic cords. The negative DAT confirms this is not autoimmune.
- **D)** Incorrect. Pyruvate kinase deficiency causes a chronic hemolytic anemia due to impaired glycolysis and ATP depletion, but the peripheral smear typically shows echinocytes (burr cells) rather than spherocytes. It would also not characteristically produce a positive osmotic fragility test in the way hereditary spherocytosis does.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Heinz bodies do not interfere with the colorimetric G6PD assay. The concern is about the cell population being tested, not assay interference. The technical issue is that the cells with the most severe enzyme deficiency have already been destroyed during hemolysis.
- **B)** Correct. G6PD enzyme activity declines as red cells age because mature red cells cannot synthesize new proteins. During an acute hemolytic episode, the oldest cells with the lowest G6PD activity are preferentially destroyed. The surviving population is enriched with young reticulocytes and newer red cells that have relatively higher G6PD levels. Testing during or immediately after a crisis can therefore yield a falsely normal result, missing the diagnosis.
- **C)** Incorrect. Acute inflammation does not temporarily inhibit G6PD activity across all cells. The issue is selective destruction of the most deficient cells, not a systemic suppression of enzyme activity. The remaining cells test normal because they genuinely have higher enzyme levels.
- **D)** Incorrect. Hemoglobinemia does not cross-react with the G6PD enzyme assay. The diagnostic pitfall is biological (the most deficient cells have been destroyed), not a laboratory artifact from free hemoglobin interfering with the assay methodology.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While the spleen does play a role in clearing damaged red cells (including those with Heinz bodies), splenic clearance is not the reason the hemolytic episode is self-limited. The self-limitation is due to the selective destruction of the most vulnerable (oldest, most enzyme-depleted) cells, leaving behind a more resistant population.
- **B)** Correct. In G6PD A-, the enzyme is synthesized normally but is unstable and loses activity as red cells age (red cells cannot make new protein). During an oxidant challenge, the oldest red cells with the least residual G6PD activity are most vulnerable and are selectively destroyed. Younger red cells and reticulocytes retain sufficient G6PD activity to generate NADPH and maintain reduced glutathione, so they survive the oxidative stress. Once the vulnerable older population is eliminated, hemolysis stops.
- **C)** Incorrect. While removal of the oxidant stimulus does help, the self-limited nature of hemolysis in G6PD A- is primarily explained by the age-dependent enzyme decline. Even with continued oxidant exposure, the episode would slow because the most susceptible cells have already been destroyed. This distinguishes G6PD A- from the Mediterranean variant, where enzyme levels are severely reduced even in young cells.
- **D)** Incorrect. There is no significant compensatory upregulation of glutathione reductase within 48 hours. Mature red cells have limited capacity for new protein synthesis. The self-limitation is due to selective destruction of old, enzyme-depleted cells, not compensatory enzyme induction.

### Q10. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Pyruvate kinase is the rate-limiting enzyme of glycolysis, which is the sole source of ATP in mature red cells (which lack mitochondria). ATP is continuously required for maintaining ion gradients (Na+/K+ ATPase), membrane integrity, and cell shape. Pyruvate kinase deficiency causes ongoing ATP depletion and therefore chronic hemolysis. In contrast, G6PD deficiency affects the hexose monophosphate shunt (pentose phosphate pathway), which generates NADPH for defense against oxidative stress. Hemolysis in G6PD deficiency only occurs when an oxidant challenge overwhelms the cell's diminished protective capacity.
- **B)** Incorrect. While it is true that G6PD activity declines with cell age in certain variants (like G6PD A-), pyruvate kinase activity can also vary with cell age. The key distinction is not age-dependence of the enzyme but rather the continuous need for ATP (glycolysis) versus the episodic need for NADPH (pentose phosphate pathway) only during oxidative stress.
- **C)** Incorrect. While reticulocytes do have mitochondria and can generate ATP via oxidative phosphorylation (which partially compensates in pyruvate kinase deficiency and explains why reticulocyte counts can be very high in PK deficiency), this does not explain why G6PD deficiency is episodic. The episodic nature of G6PD deficiency is because the pentose phosphate pathway is only critically needed during oxidative stress.
- **D)** Incorrect. Pyruvate kinase deficiency does not trigger complement-mediated lysis. The hemolysis is extravascular, primarily occurring in the spleen, where rigid ATP-depleted red cells are unable to navigate the narrow splenic sinusoids. Complement-mediated lysis is associated with conditions like paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, not PK deficiency.
