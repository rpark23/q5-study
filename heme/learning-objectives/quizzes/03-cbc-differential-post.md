## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Oxidative damage to hemoglobin produces Heinz bodies (denatured hemoglobin aggregates) and bite cells (where the spleen removes Heinz bodies), not schistocytes. Schistocytes result from physical fragmentation, not chemical damage.
- **B)** Correct. Schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells including helmet cells and triangular fragments) are the hallmark of mechanical shearing of RBCs, most commonly seen in microangiopathic hemolytic anemias (TTP, HUS, DIC) where fibrin strands deposited in small vessels physically slice passing red blood cells. They also occur with mechanical heart valves.
- **C)** Incorrect. Immune-mediated extravascular hemolysis (as in autoimmune hemolytic anemia) produces spherocytes, not schistocytes. In extravascular hemolysis, antibody-coated RBCs are partially phagocytosed by splenic macrophages, which removes membrane and creates spherocytes.
- **D)** Incorrect. Megaloblastic erythropoiesis from folate deficiency produces macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils on peripheral smear, not schistocytes. While some large, fragile megaloblastic cells can fragment, schistocytes are far more indicative of a microangiopathic process.

### Q2. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Target cells are associated with liver disease, thalassemia, hemoglobin C disease, and post-splenectomy states. They are not the characteristic morphology of autoimmune hemolytic anemia with a positive direct Coombs test.
- **B)** Incorrect. Sickle cells result from polymerization of hemoglobin S under deoxygenated conditions in sickle cell disease. They are not produced by antibody-mediated hemolysis and would not be associated with a positive direct Coombs test.
- **C)** Correct. In autoimmune hemolytic anemia (positive direct Coombs test), antibody-coated red blood cells are partially phagocytosed by splenic macrophages, which remove portions of the cell membrane while the cytoplasm remains. This loss of membrane surface area forces the cell into a spherical shape (spherocyte), which lacks the normal central pallor.
- **D)** Incorrect. Acanthocytes (spur cells) are associated with severe liver disease (spur cell anemia), abetalipoproteinemia, and neuroacanthocytosis. They result from abnormal membrane lipid composition, not from antibody-mediated hemolysis.

### Q3. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Spherocytes result from loss of RBC membrane, typically from immune-mediated partial phagocytosis (autoimmune hemolytic anemia) or inherited membrane defects (hereditary spherocytosis). While spherocytes may be present in any hemolytic process, they are not the specific hallmark of oxidative hemolysis.
- **B)** Incorrect. Target cells are associated with liver disease, thalassemia, hemoglobin C disease, and post-splenectomy states. They result from an increase in the membrane-to-volume ratio and are not related to oxidative damage to hemoglobin.
- **C)** Correct. Bite cells (degmacytes) are the hallmark of oxidative hemolysis. Oxidant drugs like dapsone (especially in G6PD-deficient patients) cause hemoglobin to denature and precipitate as Heinz bodies. Splenic macrophages then "bite out" the Heinz body inclusions from the red blood cell, leaving a characteristic semicircular indentation in the cell membrane.
- **D)** Incorrect. Schistocytes result from mechanical shearing of red blood cells by fibrin strands in microangiopathic hemolytic anemias (TTP, HUS, DIC) or by prosthetic heart valves. They are not caused by oxidative damage to hemoglobin.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Oxidative damage to the RBC membrane (e.g., from iron overload) can cause various morphologic changes, but it does not produce the characteristic teardrop shape. Oxidative damage more typically leads to Heinz bodies, bite cells, or spherocytes.
- **B)** Correct. In myelofibrosis, the bone marrow is replaced by fibrotic tissue. Red blood cells being produced via extramedullary hematopoiesis (primarily in the spleen) are distorted as they squeeze through the fibrotic marrow and spleen, creating the characteristic teardrop shape (dacrocyte) that the cell cannot spring back from.
- **C)** Incorrect. Autoimmune antibodies binding to RBCs cause spherocyte formation through partial phagocytosis by splenic macrophages, not teardrop cell formation. There is no mechanism by which antibody binding to one pole of an RBC would create a teardrop shape.
- **D)** Incorrect. Megakaryocyte fragments are not incorporated into the red blood cell membrane. While megakaryocyte fragments may be seen in the peripheral blood in myelofibrosis (as part of a leukoerythroblastic picture), they are not the cause of dacrocyte formation.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Beta-thalassemia trait causes microcytic anemia but characteristically has a normal or low RDW (uniform microcytosis), normal or elevated iron stores, and a low-normal ferritin rather than the low ferritin and high TIBC seen here. The elevated RDW argues against thalassemia trait.
- **B)** Correct. The constellation of microcytic anemia (MCV 68), low serum iron, low ferritin (indicating depleted iron stores), high TIBC (the body attempting to maximize iron absorption), and elevated RDW (anisocytosis as new iron-deficient smaller cells mix with older normal-sized cells) is the classic laboratory profile of iron deficiency anemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. Anemia of chronic inflammation typically shows low serum iron but normal or elevated ferritin (because iron is trapped in macrophages, not truly depleted) and low TIBC (unlike the high TIBC of iron deficiency). The RDW is usually normal in ACI.
- **D)** Incorrect. Sideroblastic anemia is characterized by ringed sideroblasts on bone marrow biopsy, elevated serum iron, and elevated ferritin due to ineffective iron utilization. The low ferritin and high TIBC in this patient are the opposite of what is expected in sideroblastic anemia.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While reticulocytes are larger than mature RBCs and can raise the MCV, reticulocytosis from GI bleeding alone rarely produces an MCV as high as 110 fL. Additionally, this answer does not account for the stated history of alcohol use and liver disease, which are well-known independent causes of macrocytosis.
- **B)** Correct. Both alcohol and chronic liver disease are common causes of macrocytosis independent of B12 or folate deficiency. Alcohol directly impairs erythroid precursor development, and liver disease causes excess cholesterol and phospholipid to be incorporated into the RBC membrane, increasing the cell's surface area and volume.
- **C)** Incorrect. While it is theoretically possible for B12 levels to be falsely normal, this is uncommon. The clinical scenario clearly provides two well-established causes of non-megaloblastic macrocytosis (alcohol and liver disease), making occult pernicious anemia a much less likely explanation.
- **D)** Incorrect. Concurrent iron deficiency would lower the MCV, not reveal a "masked" higher MCV. If iron deficiency were present alongside a macrocytic process, the MCV might be falsely normal or only mildly elevated, not 110 fL. This answer does not logically explain the macrocytosis.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. This reverses the correct pairing. Beta-thalassemia trait produces uniformly small red cells (normal RDW) because the genetic defect affects all RBCs equally, while iron deficiency produces a mixed population of sizes (elevated RDW) as iron stores gradually deplete.
- **B)** Correct. Iron deficiency anemia causes an elevated RDW because the marrow produces progressively smaller red cells as iron stores deplete, creating a mixed population of old normal-sized cells and new microcytic cells (anisocytosis). Beta-thalassemia trait produces uniformly small cells due to a consistent genetic defect in globin chain production, so the RDW remains normal.
- **C)** Incorrect. Anemia of chronic inflammation typically causes a normocytic or mildly microcytic anemia with a normal RDW, not the elevated RDW seen in Patient A. Sideroblastic anemia can have a dimorphic population, but this pairing does not match the classic clinical distinction being tested.
- **D)** Incorrect. Sideroblastic anemia can cause elevated RDW due to a dimorphic red cell population, but it is not the most common cause of microcytic anemia with elevated RDW. This combination does not represent the classic teaching point of distinguishing iron deficiency from thalassemia trait using RDW.

### Q8. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. The normal MCHC range is approximately 32-36 g/dL. An MCHC of 30 g/dL falls below this range and is classified as hypochromic, meaning the red blood cells contain less hemoglobin concentration per unit volume than normal. Hypochromia is characteristically seen in iron deficiency anemia and thalassemia.
- **B)** Incorrect. The stated normal range of 28-32 g/dL is incorrect. The true normal range for MCHC is approximately 32-36 g/dL. Using the wrong reference range would lead to a false interpretation of 30 g/dL as normochromic.
- **C)** Incorrect. An MCHC of 30 g/dL is below normal, not above it. The upper limit of normal is approximately 36 g/dL, not 28 g/dL. A truly elevated MCHC (>36 g/dL) is seen with spherocytes or may indicate a laboratory error.
- **D)** Incorrect. The MCHC can be interpreted independently of the reticulocyte count. MCHC is calculated from Hgb and Hct (MCHC = Hgb/Hct x 100) and provides information about hemoglobin concentration within red blood cells. The reticulocyte count is relevant for assessing the bone marrow response but is not needed to interpret the MCHC value.

### Q9. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. The Rule of Three states that RBC x 3 should approximate Hgb, and Hgb x 3 should approximate Hct. Here: 4.0 x 3 = 12.0 (matches Hgb), and 12.0 x 3 = 36 (matches Hct). The internally consistent values indicate normocytic, normochromic red cells. The Hgb of 12 g/dL may represent mild anemia depending on sex.
- **B)** Incorrect. The Hct of 36% is exactly 3 times the Hgb of 12 g/dL, which is the expected ratio under the Rule of Three. There is no discrepancy suggesting hemodilution; all three values are proportional.
- **C)** Incorrect. The Hgb of 12 is exactly 3 times the RBC count of 4.0, which is the expected ratio for normocytic cells. If the Hgb were disproportionately high relative to the RBC count, it would suggest macrocytosis (larger cells carrying more hemoglobin per cell), but that is not the case here.
- **D)** Incorrect. The RBC count of 4.0 is appropriate for a Hgb of 12 g/dL under the Rule of Three. Spherocytes would not cause the RBC count to appear low relative to Hgb; rather, spherocytes are associated with a high MCHC because they are dense, compact cells.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Cytopenias must be determined by absolute counts, not by relative percentages. A neutrophil percentage below 50% does not indicate neutropenia if the total WBC count is high enough to produce a normal absolute neutrophil count.
- **B)** Correct. The absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is calculated as WBC x % neutrophils = 12,000 x 0.40 = 4,800/uL. Since the normal ANC range is approximately 1,500-7,000/uL, this patient has a normal ANC. The relatively low neutrophil percentage is due to a relative lymphocytosis, not true neutropenia.
- **C)** Incorrect. "Relative neutropenia" based on percentages is not the same as clinical neutropenia. What matters clinically is the absolute neutrophil count. A lymphocyte percentage exceeding the neutrophil percentage can occur with lymphocytosis (e.g., viral infection) without any reduction in actual neutrophil numbers.
- **D)** Incorrect. Neutropenia is defined by a low absolute neutrophil count (typically ANC < 1,500/uL), not by a low total WBC count. A patient can have a normal WBC count but still have neutropenia if the neutrophil fraction is very low, and conversely, a patient with a low WBC may have adequate neutrophils.
