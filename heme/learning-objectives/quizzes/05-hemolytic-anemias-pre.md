## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. 30 days is far shorter than the normal red blood cell lifespan. This duration is closer to the lifespan of platelets (8-10 days) or the time folate stores last in the body (about 3 weeks).
- **B)** Incorrect. 60 days is approximately half the normal red blood cell lifespan. A significantly shortened lifespan like this would be seen in hemolytic conditions but is not the normal value.
- **C)** Correct. The normal red blood cell lifespan is approximately 120 days. After this period, senescent red cells are recognized and removed by reticuloendothelial macrophages in the spleen and liver. Approximately 1% of circulating red cells are normally destroyed and replaced each day.
- **D)** Incorrect. 180 days exceeds the normal red blood cell lifespan. No normal red blood cells survive this long in circulation; by 120 days, membrane changes and loss of deformability lead to splenic removal.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Iron transport from destroyed red cells back to the bone marrow is accomplished by transferrin, not haptoglobin. Iron released from hemoglobin breakdown in macrophages is exported via ferroportin and loaded onto transferrin for transport.
- **B)** Correct. Haptoglobin is a plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin released during red blood cell destruction. The hemoglobin-haptoglobin complex is then cleared by macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system (primarily in the liver). During active hemolysis, haptoglobin is consumed faster than it can be replenished, leading to characteristically low or undetectable serum levels.
- **C)** Incorrect. The conversion of unconjugated to conjugated bilirubin is performed by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase in hepatocytes, not by haptoglobin. Haptoglobin deals with free hemoglobin, not bilirubin metabolism.
- **D)** Incorrect. Erythropoietin production is stimulated by hypoxia-sensing mechanisms in the kidney, not by haptoglobin. Haptoglobin's role is limited to binding and facilitating clearance of free hemoglobin.

### Q3. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Intravascular hemolysis occurs when red blood cells are destroyed within the blood vessels, releasing free hemoglobin directly into the plasma. This leads to hemoglobinuria (dark urine), hemoglobinemia, and urine hemosiderin. Extravascular hemolysis occurs when macrophages in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow phagocytose and destroy red blood cells. Extravascular hemolysis is the more common mechanism and is associated with spherocytes on peripheral smear.
- **B)** Incorrect. Both intravascular and extravascular hemolysis can be immune-mediated or non-immune. For example, warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia causes primarily extravascular hemolysis (immune-mediated), while mechanical heart valve hemolysis causes intravascular hemolysis (non-immune). The distinction is based on location of destruction, not mechanism.
- **C)** Incorrect. This reverses the typical smear findings. Spherocytes are characteristic of extravascular hemolysis (e.g., hereditary spherocytosis, warm AIHA), while schistocytes (fragmented red cells) are characteristic of intravascular hemolysis from mechanical causes (e.g., TTP, DIC, mechanical valves).
- **D)** Incorrect. Neither intravascular nor extravascular hemolysis is specifically characterized by ferritin changes. Both types of hemolysis show elevated LDH, elevated indirect bilirubin, and decreased haptoglobin. Ferritin levels relate to iron stores and inflammation, not the type of hemolysis.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Two alpha chains and two gamma chains (alpha2-gamma2) describes fetal hemoglobin (HbF), which predominates during fetal life and the first few months after birth. HbF is gradually replaced by HbA during infancy.
- **B)** Correct. Normal adult hemoglobin (HbA) consists of two alpha-globin chains and two beta-globin chains (alpha2-beta2). HbA is the predominant hemoglobin in adults, comprising approximately 95-97% of total hemoglobin.
- **C)** Incorrect. Two alpha chains and two delta chains (alpha2-delta2) describes HbA2, which is a minor adult hemoglobin normally comprising only 2-3.5% of total hemoglobin. Elevated HbA2 is the diagnostic hallmark of beta thalassemia trait.
- **D)** Incorrect. Four beta chains (beta4) describes HbH, an abnormal hemoglobin that forms when only one alpha-globin gene is functioning (HbH disease). It is an unstable hemoglobin, not a component of normal adult hemoglobin.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Polymerization of hemoglobin into rigid fibers that distort red cell shape describes the pathophysiology of sickle cell disease (HbS polymerization), not thalassemia. Thalassemia involves precipitation of excess unpaired globin chains, not ordered polymerization.
- **B)** Correct. In thalassemia, the fundamental problem is an imbalance between alpha and beta globin chain production. The excess unpaired chains (e.g., excess alpha chains in beta-thalassemia) are unstable, precipitate within red cell precursors, and damage their membranes. This leads to destruction of developing red cells within the bone marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) and hemolysis of circulating red cells.
- **C)** Incorrect. Thalassemia involves reduced quantity of structurally normal globin chains, not replacement by abnormal variants. The chains that are produced are normal in structure; the problem is the quantitative imbalance between chain types.
- **D)** Incorrect. Complement activation on the red cell surface is a mechanism seen in conditions like paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) or autoimmune hemolytic anemia, not in thalassemia. Thalassemic red cell destruction is driven by globin chain precipitation, not complement.

### Q6. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Chromosome 11 contains the beta-globin gene cluster (beta, delta, gamma, and epsilon genes), not the alpha-globin genes. There are 2 beta-globin genes (one per chromosome 11), not 2 alpha genes on this chromosome.
- **B)** Incorrect. While the alpha-globin genes are indeed on chromosome 16, there are 4 alpha-globin genes total (2 on each copy of chromosome 16), not 2. This distinction is clinically important because deletion of different numbers of alpha genes produces different clinical severity levels.
- **C)** Correct. A normal individual has 4 alpha-globin genes, with 2 alpha genes located on each copy of chromosome 16. This is important for understanding alpha thalassemia, where deletion of 1, 2, 3, or all 4 genes produces progressively more severe clinical phenotypes (silent carrier, trait, HbH disease, and hydrops fetalis, respectively).
- **D)** Incorrect. This combines two errors: the alpha genes are on chromosome 16, not chromosome 11, and there are 4 total alpha genes, not 4 on chromosome 11. Chromosome 11 houses the beta-globin gene cluster.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Deletion of the entire beta-globin gene describes beta-zero thalassemia, not sickle cell disease. Sickle cell disease involves a qualitative change (point mutation) in the beta-globin gene, not a quantitative deletion.
- **B)** Correct. Sickle cell disease results from a single nucleotide change (GAG to GTG) in the beta-globin gene, causing substitution of the hydrophobic amino acid valine for the hydrophilic glutamic acid at position 6 of the beta chain. This creates hemoglobin S (HbS), which polymerizes when deoxygenated due to hydrophobic interactions between the valine residue and a complementary pocket on adjacent HbS molecules.
- **C)** Incorrect. Sickle cell disease is caused by a point mutation in the beta-globin gene, not a frameshift mutation in the alpha-globin gene. The alpha-globin genes are normal in sickle cell disease.
- **D)** Incorrect. A promoter mutation reducing beta-globin transcription would cause beta-plus thalassemia (reduced but not absent beta chain production), not sickle cell disease. Sickle cell disease involves a structural change in the beta chain, not reduced production.

### Q8. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Defects in horizontal membrane protein interactions describe hereditary elliptocytosis, not hereditary spherocytosis. Horizontal interactions maintain the structural integrity of the spectrin-actin cytoskeletal lattice in the plane of the membrane; their disruption produces elliptical cells.
- **B)** Incorrect. G6PD deficiency causes episodic hemolysis triggered by oxidative stress, producing bite cells and Heinz bodies. It is an enzymopathy, not a membrane structural defect, and does not produce spherocytes.
- **C)** Correct. Hereditary spherocytosis results from defects in vertical membrane protein interactions -- the connections between the spectrin-actin cytoskeleton and the overlying lipid bilayer, involving proteins such as spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, and protein 4.2. Disruption of these vertical linkages causes progressive loss of unsupported membrane, reducing the surface area-to-volume ratio and forcing the cell into a spherical shape.
- **D)** Incorrect. Abnormal hemoglobin polymerization causing membrane rigidity describes sickle cell disease (HbS polymerization), not hereditary spherocytosis. Spherocytosis is caused by a primary membrane skeleton defect, not a hemoglobin abnormality.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The final enzyme of glycolysis that generates ATP is pyruvate kinase, not G6PD. Pyruvate kinase deficiency causes chronic hemolytic anemia due to ATP depletion and impaired red cell membrane function.
- **B)** Correct. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the first and rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (also called the hexose monophosphate shunt). It catalyzes the oxidation of glucose-6-phosphate, generating NADPH. NADPH is essential for maintaining reduced glutathione (GSH), which protects red cell proteins and membranes from oxidative damage.
- **C)** Incorrect. Membrane transporters that maintain intracellular ion concentrations include the Na+/K+ ATPase, which is powered by ATP from glycolysis. G6PD is an enzymatic component of the pentose phosphate pathway, not a membrane transporter.
- **D)** Incorrect. The enzyme that converts methemoglobin back to functional hemoglobin is methemoglobin reductase (NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase), not G6PD. While NADPH generated by G6PD can serve as a backup pathway for methemoglobin reduction, this is not its primary role.

### Q10. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. G6PD deficiency is not autosomal dominant. The G6PD gene is located on the X chromosome, meaning the inheritance pattern is sex-linked, not autosomal.
- **B)** Incorrect. G6PD deficiency is not autosomal recessive. Since the gene is X-linked, males (XY) who inherit one mutant copy are hemizygous and fully affected. Autosomal recessive conditions require two mutant copies for disease expression.
- **C)** Correct. G6PD deficiency follows an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern. The G6PD gene is located on the X chromosome. Males with a single mutant allele are hemizygous and clinically affected. Heterozygous females are usually carriers with variable expression due to X-inactivation (lyonization), while homozygous females are fully affected.
- **D)** Incorrect. Mitochondrial inheritance involves transmission through maternal mitochondrial DNA and affects both sexes equally. G6PD deficiency is encoded by a nuclear gene on the X chromosome, not by mitochondrial DNA.
