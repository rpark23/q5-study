## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Ferroportin is the iron exporter on the basolateral membrane of enterocytes and macrophages. It moves iron out of cells into the circulation, not into enterocytes from the gut lumen.
- **B)** Incorrect. Transferrin is a plasma protein that carries iron through the bloodstream to cells with transferrin receptors. It does not function at the apical membrane of enterocytes.
- **C)** Correct. DMT1 (divalent metal transporter 1) is located on the apical membrane of duodenal enterocytes and is responsible for importing dietary ferrous iron (Fe2+) from the intestinal lumen into the cell. Low gastric pH and ascorbate enhance iron solubility to facilitate this transport.
- **D)** Incorrect. Cubulin is the receptor in the terminal ileum that binds the intrinsic factor-vitamin B12 complex. It plays no role in iron transport.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Iron delivery to erythroid precursors is mediated by transferrin binding to transferrin receptors on cell surfaces, not by ferroportin. Ferroportin is an exporter, not an importer.
- **B)** Correct. Ferroportin is the only known cellular iron exporter in mammals. It is located on the basolateral membrane of duodenal enterocytes and on the surface of reticuloendothelial macrophages, where it exports iron into the plasma for loading onto transferrin.
- **C)** Incorrect. The conversion of ferrous to ferric iron in the stomach relates to gastric acid and oxidation states. Ferroportin does not perform this function; rather, after ferroportin exports ferrous iron, it is oxidized to ferric iron before binding transferrin.
- **D)** Incorrect. The plasma protein that binds and transports iron is transferrin, not ferroportin. Ferroportin is a membrane channel that exports iron from inside cells.

### Q3. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. 0.3-0.5 grams is far below normal total body iron. This amount is closer to the iron found in the bone marrow alone (approximately 0.3 g).
- **B)** Incorrect. 1-2 grams underestimates total body iron. The liver alone stores approximately 1.0 g, and hemoglobin in red blood cells contains about 1.8 g.
- **C)** Correct. Total body iron in a healthy adult is approximately 3-4 grams, distributed among hemoglobin in red blood cells (1.8 g), liver stores (1.0 g), reticuloendothelial macrophages (0.6 g), bone marrow (0.3 g), myoglobin in muscles (0.3 g), and transferrin in plasma (0.003 g).
- **D)** Incorrect. 8-10 grams would represent iron overload, well above the normal 3-4 gram total body iron content.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Microcytic red blood cells are a laboratory finding on CBC/smear in iron deficiency, but this is not what the term "pica" refers to. Pica is a clinical symptom, not a morphologic finding.
- **B)** Correct. Pica is defined as a craving for and consumption of non-food substances, such as ice (pagophagia), clay (geophagia), or starch. It is a clinical symptom specifically associated with iron deficiency and often resolves with iron repletion.
- **C)** Incorrect. Spoon-shaped deformity of the fingernails is called koilonychia, not pica. Koilonychia is another clinical finding specific to iron deficiency but is a separate entity from pica.
- **D)** Incorrect. Inflammation of the corners of the mouth is called angular stomatitis (or angular cheilitis). While it can occur in iron deficiency, this is not the definition of pica.

### Q5. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. TNF-alpha is an inflammatory cytokine that contributes to the pathogenesis of anemia of inflammation through bone marrow suppression and reduced RBC lifespan, but it is not the primary cytokine driving hepcidin production.
- **B)** Incorrect. IFN-gamma is involved in the inflammatory response and can contribute to marrow suppression, but it is not the primary stimulator of hepatic hepcidin synthesis. IL-6 is the principal cytokine for this function.
- **C)** Correct. IL-6 (interleukin-6) is the primary cytokine that stimulates hepatic production of hepcidin during inflammation. IL-6 signals through the JAK-STAT pathway in hepatocytes to upregulate hepcidin transcription, which then causes ferroportin degradation and iron sequestration.
- **D)** Incorrect. IL-1beta is another inflammatory mediator that can modulate hepcidin expression, but it is not the primary driver. IL-6 is the key cytokine recognized as the principal inducer of hepcidin in inflammatory states.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Hepcidin does not stimulate DMT1 expression. Its mechanism of action is through ferroportin, not DMT1. Iron absorption is reduced by hepcidin because iron cannot exit enterocytes into the bloodstream, not because apical uptake is increased.
- **B)** Correct. Hepcidin binds to ferroportin on the surface of enterocytes and macrophages, triggering ferroportin internalization and lysosomal degradation. This traps iron inside these cells, preventing dietary iron absorption and blocking release of recycled iron from macrophages, resulting in functional iron deficiency.
- **C)** Incorrect. Hepcidin does not directly inhibit transferrin receptor expression on erythroid precursors. Its target is ferroportin on iron-exporting cells (enterocytes and macrophages), not the iron-importing machinery on erythroid cells.
- **D)** Incorrect. Hepcidin does not promote the release of iron from ferritin. In fact, by blocking ferroportin, hepcidin increases intracellular iron storage as ferritin, keeping iron trapped and unavailable for erythropoiesis.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Anemia caused by chronic blood loss leading to iron depletion describes iron deficiency anemia, which is microcytic and hypochromic. Megaloblastic anemia has a distinct mechanism involving impaired DNA synthesis, not iron depletion.
- **B)** Correct. Megaloblastic anemia is characterized by impaired DNA synthesis (most commonly from B12 or folate deficiency) that slows nuclear maturation while cytoplasmic maturation proceeds normally. This nuclear-cytoplasmic dyssynchrony produces abnormally large cells (megaloblasts) with immature-appearing nuclei.
- **C)** Incorrect. Antibody-mediated destruction of red blood cells describes autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), which is a hemolytic anemia, not a megaloblastic anemia. Hemolysis and megaloblastic change are fundamentally different pathologic processes.
- **D)** Incorrect. Defective hemoglobin structure causing red cell sickling describes sickle cell disease, a hemoglobinopathy. This is unrelated to the impaired DNA synthesis that defines megaloblastic anemia.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Converting methylmalonic acid-related substrates (methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA) is the role of B12 as adenosyl-cobalamin in the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase reaction, not the methionine synthase reaction. These are two separate B12-dependent reactions.
- **B)** Correct. In the methionine synthase reaction, vitamin B12 (as methylcobalamin) serves as a cofactor that transfers a methyl group from methyl-THF to homocysteine, producing methionine and regenerating tetrahydrofolate (THF). This reaction is critical because THF is needed for thymidylate synthesis and DNA replication.
- **C)** Incorrect. B12 does not directly synthesize thymidylate. Thymidylate synthase uses a folate derivative (methylene-THF) for this reaction. B12's role is upstream -- regenerating THF from methyl-THF so that folate can be used for thymidylate synthesis.
- **D)** Incorrect. Folate absorption in the duodenum and jejunum does not require B12. Folate is absorbed after deglutamylation and transported across the brush border without a special transport protein like intrinsic factor.

### Q9. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. The duodenum is the site where pancreatic proteases transfer B12 from dietary binding proteins (R-binders) to intrinsic factor. Absorption of the B12-IF complex does not occur here.
- **B)** Incorrect. The jejunum is the primary site of folate absorption, not B12 absorption. The B12-IF complex passes through the jejunum but is not absorbed until it reaches the terminal ileum.
- **C)** Correct. The terminal ileum contains cubulin receptors that specifically bind the intrinsic factor-B12 complex and mediate its endocytosis into ileal enterocytes. After uptake, B12 is released and bound to transcobalamin for transport into the bloodstream.
- **D)** Incorrect. The ascending colon does not absorb the B12-IF complex. While colonic bacteria can produce some B12, it is produced too distally to be absorbed by the cubulin receptors in the terminal ileum.

### Q10. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Target cells are characteristically seen in thalassemia, liver disease, and hemoglobin C disease, where there is excess membrane relative to cytoplasm. They are not a hallmark of megaloblastic anemia.
- **B)** Incorrect. Schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells) are the hallmark of microangiopathic hemolytic anemias such as TTP, HUS, and DIC. They indicate mechanical shearing of red cells, not impaired DNA synthesis.
- **C)** Correct. Hypersegmented neutrophils (defined as neutrophils with 5 or more nuclear lobes) are the hallmark peripheral smear finding of megaloblastic anemia. They result from impaired DNA synthesis in neutrophil precursors, leading to abnormal nuclear maturation and excessive nuclear lobulation.
- **D)** Incorrect. Spherocytes are the hallmark of hereditary spherocytosis and autoimmune hemolytic anemia, where membrane loss produces small, dense, round red cells. They are not associated with megaloblastic anemia.
