## Platelets: Physiology and Quantitative Disorders (Clara Lo)

**Q1.** A patient with a platelet disorder is found to have impaired platelet adhesion to exposed subendothelial collagen at sites of vascular injury. Which of the following platelet surface receptors is most directly responsible for mediating this initial adhesion step via binding to von Willebrand factor?

- A) GP-IIb/IIIa
- B) GPIb-IX-V
- C) P-selectin
- D) Thromboxane A2 receptor

**Q2.** A hematologist explains to a medical student that a single megakaryocyte can produce thousands of platelets. Which of the following features of megakaryocyte biology makes this possible?

- A) Megakaryocytes undergo rapid mitotic divisions to expand their number before producing platelets
- B) Megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis to become polyploid, increasing cytoplasmic volume for platelet production
- C) Megakaryocytes fuse with neighboring cells to increase their size
- D) Megakaryocytes produce platelets by budding from the cell membrane in a process independent of ploidy

**Q3.** A patient with cirrhosis and portal hypertension has a platelet count of 72,000/uL. The bone marrow is normal with adequate megakaryocytes. Which of the following best explains this patient's thrombocytopenia?

- A) Decreased thrombopoietin production and increased splenic sequestration
- B) Immune-mediated platelet destruction by anti-platelet antibodies
- C) Disseminated intravascular coagulation consuming platelets
- D) Bone marrow infiltration by hepatic fibrotic tissue

**Q4.** A 30-year-old woman presents with petechiae on her lower extremities and gingival bleeding. Her CBC shows isolated thrombocytopenia (platelet count 15,000/uL) with normal hemoglobin and WBC. Peripheral smear shows large platelets but is otherwise unremarkable. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
- B) Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)
- C) Aplastic anemia
- D) Acute leukemia

**Q5.** A child develops thrombocytopenia two weeks after a viral upper respiratory infection. He is diagnosed with ITP. Compared to ITP in adults, which of the following statements about childhood ITP is most accurate?

- A) Childhood ITP is more likely to become chronic and require long-term treatment
- B) Childhood ITP typically follows a viral infection and often resolves spontaneously
- C) Childhood ITP is usually associated with an underlying autoimmune disease
- D) Childhood ITP has a worse overall prognosis than adult ITP

**Q6.** A patient with severe ITP (platelet count 5,000/uL) is actively bleeding and needs a rapid rise in platelet count. Which of the following treatments has the fastest onset of action?

- A) Oral prednisone (expected response in 5-7 days)
- B) Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (expected response in 24-48 hours)
- C) Thrombopoietin receptor agonist (expected response in 1-2 weeks)
- D) Rituximab (expected response in 2-4 weeks)

**Q7.** A 35-year-old woman presents with confusion, fever, thrombocytopenia (platelet count 22,000/uL), and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. Peripheral smear shows numerous schistocytes. ADAMTS13 activity is <5%. Which of the following best describes the pathophysiology of this condition?

- A) Autoantibodies destroy platelets in the spleen, causing isolated thrombocytopenia
- B) Deficiency of ADAMTS13 leads to accumulation of ultra-large VWF multimers, causing platelet microthrombi in small vessels
- C) Complement-mediated destruction of red blood cells and platelets in the vasculature
- D) Disseminated intravascular coagulation with consumption of clotting factors and platelets

**Q8.** A patient is diagnosed with acquired TTP. Which of the following is the most critical initial treatment?

- A) Platelet transfusion to correct the thrombocytopenia
- B) Heparin anticoagulation to prevent further thrombosis
- C) Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis) to remove ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replenish the enzyme
- D) High-dose corticosteroids alone

**Q9.** A male infant presents with thrombocytopenia, eczema, and recurrent infections. Peripheral smear shows abnormally small platelets. Which of the following congenital disorders is most consistent with this presentation?

- A) Bernard-Soulier syndrome
- B) Glanzmann thrombasthenia
- C) Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome
- D) Thrombocytopenia with absent radii (TAR)

**Q10.** A patient with a suspected platelet function disorder undergoes platelet aggregometry. Aggregation is absent in response to ADP, collagen, epinephrine, and thrombin, but aggregation to ristocetin is normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- A) Bernard-Soulier syndrome
- B) Glanzmann thrombasthenia
- C) Von Willebrand disease
- D) Aspirin effect

**Q11.** A patient has a normal platelet count but absent ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation with normal aggregation to all other agonists. She also has macrothrombocytopenia. Which platelet receptor is most likely defective?

- A) GP-IIb/IIIa
- B) Thromboxane A2 receptor
- C) GPIb-IX-V
- D) Collagen receptor (GPVI)

**Q12.** A clinician is evaluating a patient with mucocutaneous bleeding. The patient has petechiae and epistaxis but no hemarthroses or deep muscle hematomas. This bleeding pattern is most consistent with a defect in which of the following?

- A) The intrinsic coagulation pathway (factor VIII or IX deficiency)
- B) Primary hemostasis (platelet number or function, or von Willebrand factor)
- C) The extrinsic coagulation pathway (factor VII deficiency)
- D) The fibrinolytic system (excess plasmin activity)

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. GP-IIb/IIIa (integrin alphaIIb-beta3) is the receptor responsible for platelet aggregation, not initial adhesion. It mediates platelet-platelet cross-linking by binding fibrinogen and von Willebrand factor after platelet activation, which is a later step in primary hemostasis.
- **B)** Correct. GPIb-IX-V is the platelet surface receptor that binds von Willebrand factor (VWF) anchored to exposed subendothelial collagen, mediating the initial adhesion of platelets at sites of vascular injury. This is the critical first step in primary hemostasis and is deficient in Bernard-Soulier syndrome.
- **C)** Incorrect. P-selectin is a cell adhesion molecule stored in platelet alpha-granules that is expressed on the platelet surface upon activation. It mediates platelet interactions with leukocytes and endothelial cells but is not responsible for the initial adhesion step to subendothelial VWF.
- **D)** Incorrect. The thromboxane A2 (TXA2) receptor mediates platelet activation and amplification of the platelet response after initial adhesion has occurred. TXA2 is synthesized by activated platelets and promotes further platelet recruitment, but it does not mediate the initial adhesion to VWF.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not undergo conventional mitotic cell division to expand their numbers. Instead, they replicate their DNA without dividing, a process called endomitosis. This is a fundamental distinction in megakaryocyte biology and explains how one cell can grow large enough to produce thousands of platelets.
- **B)** Correct. Megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis, a process of DNA replication without cell division that produces polyploid cells (typically reaching a mean ploidy of 16N). This massive increase in DNA content drives cytoplasmic expansion, allowing a single megakaryocyte to extend long proplatelet processes into bone marrow sinusoids and release thousands of platelets.
- **C)** Incorrect. Megakaryocytes do not fuse with neighboring cells to increase their size. Their large size results from endomitosis (internal DNA replication without cytokinesis), not from cell fusion. Cell fusion is a feature of other biological processes such as skeletal muscle fiber formation.
- **D)** Incorrect. While platelets are indeed formed by fragmentation of megakaryocyte cytoplasm (through proplatelet extensions), this process is directly dependent on ploidy. The degree of polyploidy determines the cytoplasmic volume available for platelet production, making endomitosis essential for high platelet output.

### Q3. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Cirrhosis causes thrombocytopenia through two complementary mechanisms: decreased hepatic production of thrombopoietin (TPO), the primary growth factor for megakaryocyte development, and hypersplenism from portal hypertension causing increased splenic sequestration of platelets. Normally about one-third of platelets reside in the spleen, but with splenomegaly this fraction increases substantially.
- **B)** Incorrect. Immune-mediated platelet destruction (as in ITP) involves autoantibodies against platelet surface glycoproteins, leading to splenic clearance of opsonized platelets. While autoimmune thrombocytopenia can occur in liver disease (e.g., hepatitis C-associated ITP), the primary mechanism in cirrhosis with portal hypertension is decreased TPO and splenic sequestration.
- **C)** Incorrect. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) can occur in liver disease, but it consumes both platelets and coagulation factors and would present with additional findings such as elevated D-dimer, prolonged PT/PTT, and low fibrinogen. The clinical scenario describes isolated thrombocytopenia with a normal marrow, more consistent with sequestration and decreased TPO.
- **D)** Incorrect. Hepatic fibrotic tissue does not infiltrate the bone marrow. The bone marrow in this patient is described as normal with adequate megakaryocytes, which rules out a marrow infiltrative process. The thrombocytopenia is caused by peripheral mechanisms (splenic sequestration) and decreased TPO production.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) would present with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes on smear, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin) in addition to thrombocytopenia. This patient has isolated thrombocytopenia with an otherwise unremarkable smear, which is inconsistent with TTP.
- **B)** Correct. Isolated thrombocytopenia with large platelets on smear and an otherwise normal CBC in a young woman is the classic presentation of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). The large platelets indicate active bone marrow compensation, as the marrow is producing young, large platelets in response to peripheral immune-mediated destruction. ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **C)** Incorrect. Aplastic anemia causes pancytopenia (decreased red cells, white cells, and platelets) due to bone marrow failure. This patient has normal hemoglobin and WBC with only isolated thrombocytopenia, which does not fit aplastic anemia.
- **D)** Incorrect. Acute leukemia would typically present with abnormal cells on the peripheral smear (blasts), and often with anemia and/or leukocytosis or leukopenia in addition to thrombocytopenia. This patient's smear shows only large platelets with no abnormal cells, making leukemia very unlikely.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Childhood ITP is actually less likely to become chronic compared to adult ITP. The majority of children with ITP (approximately 70-80%) experience spontaneous resolution within 6 months. It is adult ITP that more commonly follows a chronic course requiring long-term management.
- **B)** Correct. Childhood ITP typically occurs following a viral infection (often an upper respiratory infection), is self-limited, and resolves spontaneously in the majority of cases. The immune stimulus from the viral infection triggers cross-reactive antiplatelet antibodies that are usually transient, unlike the persistent autoimmunity more common in adult ITP.
- **C)** Incorrect. Childhood ITP is usually a primary (idiopathic) immune process triggered by a preceding viral infection, not a manifestation of an underlying systemic autoimmune disease. In contrast, adult ITP is more commonly associated with other autoimmune conditions (e.g., SLE) or chronic infections (e.g., hepatitis C, H. pylori).
- **D)** Incorrect. Childhood ITP actually has a better overall prognosis than adult ITP precisely because it is typically self-limited and resolves spontaneously. Serious bleeding complications in childhood ITP are rare, and most children recover fully without long-term sequelae.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While oral prednisone is a standard first-line treatment for ITP, its onset of action is 5-7 days, which is too slow for a patient with severe thrombocytopenia who is actively bleeding. Steroids work by reducing antibody production and decreasing splenic macrophage activity, but this takes several days to achieve a meaningful platelet rise.
- **B)** Correct. IVIG has the fastest onset of action among ITP treatments, typically raising the platelet count within 24-48 hours. It works primarily by saturating Fc-gamma receptors on splenic macrophages, competitively inhibiting their ability to phagocytose antibody-coated platelets. This rapid mechanism makes IVIG the treatment of choice in emergency situations with active bleeding.
- **C)** Incorrect. Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (e.g., romiplostim, eltrombopag) stimulate megakaryocyte proliferation and platelet production, but they take 1-2 weeks to achieve a response because new platelets must be generated from the marrow. This timeframe is too slow for an actively bleeding patient.
- **D)** Incorrect. Rituximab (anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody) depletes B-cells that produce antiplatelet antibodies, but the onset of response is 2-4 weeks because it takes time to eliminate the antibody-producing B-cell clones and for circulating autoantibody levels to fall. This is a second-line therapy, not an emergency treatment.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. This describes the pathophysiology of ITP, not TTP. ITP involves autoantibody-mediated splenic destruction of platelets causing isolated thrombocytopenia without hemolysis or schistocytes. TTP has a fundamentally different mechanism involving VWF-mediated microvascular thrombosis.
- **B)** Correct. In TTP, severely deficient ADAMTS13 activity (either from autoantibodies in acquired TTP or mutations in congenital TTP) prevents the cleavage of ultra-large VWF multimers. These uncleaved multimers spontaneously bind and activate platelets in the microvasculature, forming platelet-rich microthrombi that consume platelets (causing thrombocytopenia) and shear red blood cells passing through the partially occluded vessels (causing schistocytes and hemolytic anemia).
- **C)** Incorrect. Complement-mediated destruction describes the pathophysiology of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), which involves uncontrolled complement activation on endothelial surfaces. While aHUS can also cause thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, the severely deficient ADAMTS13 (<5%) in this patient specifically indicates TTP, not complement-mediated disease.
- **D)** Incorrect. DIC involves widespread activation of the coagulation cascade with consumption of both clotting factors and platelets. DIC would present with prolonged PT and PTT, low fibrinogen, and elevated D-dimer. TTP, by contrast, involves platelet-mediated microvascular thrombosis with normal or near-normal coagulation studies, and the key deficiency is in ADAMTS13.

### Q8. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Platelet transfusion in TTP is generally contraindicated (or used only as a last resort in life-threatening bleeding) because transfused platelets can be incorporated into the existing microthrombi, potentially worsening the thrombotic process and causing further organ damage. This is a critical teaching point -- "fuel on the fire."
- **B)** Incorrect. Heparin anticoagulation does not address the underlying pathophysiology of TTP (ultra-large VWF multimer accumulation due to ADAMTS13 deficiency). The microthrombi in TTP are platelet-rich (white thrombi), not fibrin-rich, so anticoagulation with heparin is not effective and does not treat the root cause.
- **C)** Correct. Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis) is the cornerstone of TTP treatment and should be initiated emergently. It serves a dual therapeutic purpose: removing the circulating autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 (and the ultra-large VWF multimers) while simultaneously replenishing functional ADAMTS13 enzyme through the replacement plasma. Before plasma exchange was available, TTP had a mortality rate exceeding 90%.
- **D)** Incorrect. High-dose corticosteroids are used as adjunctive therapy in acquired TTP (to suppress autoantibody production), but they are not sufficient as the sole treatment. Steroids take days to weeks to suppress the immune response, while plasma exchange provides immediate removal of pathologic antibodies and replacement of ADAMTS13. Steroids alone would not adequately address the acute crisis.

### Q9. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Bernard-Soulier syndrome is characterized by thrombocytopenia with abnormally large platelets (macrothrombocytopenia) due to GPIb-IX-V deficiency. It does not cause eczema or immunodeficiency. The small platelet size and triad of symptoms in this patient are inconsistent with Bernard-Soulier.
- **B)** Incorrect. Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a qualitative platelet disorder caused by GP-IIb/IIIa deficiency. It presents with mucocutaneous bleeding but characteristically has a normal platelet count and normal platelet size. It does not cause eczema, immunodeficiency, or thrombocytopenia.
- **C)** Correct. Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome is an X-linked recessive disorder caused by mutations in the WAS gene. It presents with the classic triad of thrombocytopenia with characteristically small platelets (microthrombocytopenia), eczema, and immunodeficiency (recurrent infections). The small platelet size is a distinguishing feature that differentiates it from other congenital thrombocytopenias.
- **D)** Incorrect. Thrombocytopenia with absent radii (TAR) syndrome presents with thrombocytopenia and bilateral absence of the radius bones, a skeletal abnormality not mentioned in this patient. TAR does not cause eczema or immunodeficiency, and the characteristic small platelets described here are specific to Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Bernard-Soulier syndrome (GPIb-IX-V deficiency) would show absent ristocetin-induced aggregation because ristocetin works by promoting GPIb-VWF interaction. In this patient, ristocetin aggregation is normal, ruling out Bernard-Soulier. Bernard-Soulier would also present with macrothrombocytopenia.
- **B)** Correct. Glanzmann thrombasthenia is caused by deficiency or dysfunction of GP-IIb/IIIa, the receptor that mediates platelet aggregation by binding fibrinogen to cross-link adjacent platelets. Since all agonist-induced aggregation (ADP, collagen, epinephrine, thrombin) ultimately depends on GP-IIb/IIIa for the final aggregation step, all are absent. Ristocetin-induced agglutination is preserved because it depends on GPIb-VWF interaction, which is independent of GP-IIb/IIIa.
- **C)** Incorrect. Von Willebrand disease would characteristically show decreased or absent ristocetin-induced aggregation (because ristocetin depends on VWF to mediate GPIb binding), but responses to other agonists like ADP and collagen would remain intact. The pattern of absent aggregation to all agonists except ristocetin is the opposite of what VWD would produce.
- **D)** Incorrect. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase, blocking thromboxane A2 production. This impairs aggregation responses to weak agonists (ADP, collagen, epinephrine) but does not abolish them completely, and aggregation to strong agonists like thrombin remains intact. The complete absence of aggregation to all agonists including thrombin indicates a structural receptor deficiency, not an aspirin effect.

### Q11. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. GP-IIb/IIIa deficiency (Glanzmann thrombasthenia) would cause absent aggregation to all agonists (ADP, collagen, epinephrine, thrombin) but preserved ristocetin-induced agglutination -- the opposite of this patient's pattern. GP-IIb/IIIa deficiency also does not cause macrothrombocytopenia.
- **B)** Incorrect. Thromboxane A2 receptor deficiency would impair platelet activation responses but would not selectively abolish ristocetin-induced aggregation. Ristocetin agglutination is independent of the TXA2 pathway and depends solely on GPIb-VWF interaction.
- **C)** Correct. Absent ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation with normal responses to all other agonists, combined with macrothrombocytopenia, is the classic pattern of Bernard-Soulier syndrome (GPIb-IX-V deficiency). Ristocetin induces platelet agglutination by promoting VWF binding to GPIb, so a defective GPIb-IX-V complex abolishes this response. The large platelets are a characteristic morphologic feature of Bernard-Soulier.
- **D)** Incorrect. Collagen receptor (GPVI) deficiency would specifically impair collagen-induced platelet activation and aggregation but would not affect ristocetin-induced agglutination (which depends on GPIb-VWF interaction). The selective loss of ristocetin response with macrothrombocytopenia points to GPIb-IX-V deficiency, not GPVI.

### Q12. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Intrinsic pathway deficiencies (factor VIII or IX deficiency, i.e., hemophilia A or B) cause deep tissue bleeding -- hemarthroses (bleeding into joints), deep muscle hematomas, and delayed bleeding after surgery or trauma. They do not typically cause petechiae or superficial mucocutaneous bleeding, which are hallmarks of platelet/primary hemostasis disorders.
- **B)** Correct. Mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, menorrhagia) is the hallmark of defects in primary hemostasis, which includes platelet quantity disorders, platelet function disorders, and von Willebrand factor deficiency. Petechiae, in particular, are virtually pathognomonic for a primary hemostasis defect and are not seen in coagulation factor deficiencies.
- **C)** Incorrect. Extrinsic pathway deficiency (factor VII deficiency) causes a coagulation disorder that would present with deep bleeding and prolonged PT, not with the petechiae and mucocutaneous bleeding pattern described. Isolated factor VII deficiency is rare and does not produce petechiae.
- **D)** Incorrect. Excess fibrinolytic activity (e.g., from excess plasmin) would cause dissolution of already-formed clots, leading to delayed or recurrent bleeding after initial hemostasis. It would not produce the classic petechiae and mucocutaneous bleeding pattern, which specifically indicates a defect in the initial platelet plug formation (primary hemostasis).
