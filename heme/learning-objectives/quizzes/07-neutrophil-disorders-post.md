## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Neutrophils and eosinophils are terminally differentiated granulocytes that arise from myeloid progenitors in the bone marrow. Monocytes do not differentiate into granulocytes; rather, both monocytes and granulocytes share a common myeloid progenitor but diverge early in their development.
- **B)** Correct. The cell described is a monocyte, identified by its kidney-shaped (reniform) nucleus, abundant gray-blue cytoplasm, and fine azurophilic granules. Monocytes circulate in the blood for 1-3 days, then migrate into tissues where they differentiate into macrophages (tissue-resident phagocytes for innate immunity, antigen presentation, and debris clearance) or dendritic cells (professional antigen-presenting cells that activate naive T cells).
- **C)** Incorrect. Plasma cells and memory B cells are differentiated forms of B lymphocytes, arising through antigen stimulation and T-cell help in the adaptive immune response. Monocytes belong to the myeloid lineage and do not give rise to any lymphoid cells.
- **D)** Incorrect. Natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells are lymphoid lineage cells. NK cells arise from lymphoid progenitors in the bone marrow, and cytotoxic T cells differentiate from naive CD8+ T cells in secondary lymphoid organs. Monocytes cannot differentiate into lymphoid cells.

### Q2. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. ICAM-1 (intercellular adhesion molecule 1) is the endothelial ligand for integrins (CD11a/CD18 and CD11b/CD18) and is involved in the firm adhesion step -- the step that comes after rolling. It does not mediate rolling.
- **B)** Incorrect. CD18 (beta-2 integrin) pairs with CD11a or CD11b to form the integrins LFA-1 and Mac-1, which mediate firm adhesion to ICAM-1 on endothelium. Integrins are responsible for the arrest (firm adhesion) step, not the rolling step.
- **C)** Correct. The rolling step of neutrophil extravasation is mediated by selectins: E-selectin and P-selectin on activated endothelium bind to sialyl-Lewis X and PSGL-1 on neutrophils, while L-selectin on neutrophils binds to endothelial ligands. These low-affinity, high-off-rate interactions allow the characteristic rolling movement.
- **D)** Incorrect. C3b is an opsonin generated by complement activation. It coats pathogens and facilitates phagocytosis via complement receptors on neutrophils. It plays no role in neutrophil rolling along the endothelium during extravasation.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Chemotaxis (directed migration toward chemoattractants) is a separate step that occurs after neutrophils have already exited the vasculature. LAD type I impairs the step before transmigration -- firm adhesion -- which prevents neutrophils from ever leaving the blood.
- **B)** Correct. LAD type I is caused by deficiency of CD18 (the beta-2 integrin subunit), which prevents firm adhesion of neutrophils to ICAM-1 on activated endothelium. Without firm adhesion, neutrophils cannot transmigrate into tissues, explaining the leukocytosis (neutrophils trapped in blood), absent pus formation, and delayed umbilical cord separation.
- **C)** Incorrect. Opsonization by complement receptors (e.g., CR3/CD11b-CD18) is affected in LAD I, but the primary and most clinically significant defect is failure of firm adhesion and transmigration, not opsonization per se. The clinical hallmarks described (absent pus, leukocytosis, delayed cord separation) all point to a trafficking defect.
- **D)** Incorrect. The oxidative respiratory burst is mediated by NADPH oxidase, which is intact in LAD I. A defect in the respiratory burst characterizes chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), not LAD I.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Failure of NADPH oxidase assembly describes chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), which presents with granuloma formation and infections by catalase-positive organisms. CGD does not feature giant granules or albinism.
- **B)** Correct. Chediak-Higashi syndrome is caused by a mutation in the LYST gene, which regulates vesicle trafficking and granule fusion. Abnormal vesicle trafficking produces pathognomonic giant azurophilic granules. The classic triad is recurrent pyogenic infections, partial oculocutaneous albinism (from defective melanin granule trafficking), and neurologic dysfunction.
- **C)** Incorrect. Inability to adhere to endothelium describes leukocyte adhesion deficiency (LAD), which presents with leukocytosis and absent pus but does not involve giant granules or albinism.
- **D)** Incorrect. MPO deficiency is the most common inherited phagocyte disorder but is usually clinically silent. It does not produce giant granules, albinism, or significant recurrent infections in most patients.

### Q5. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. CGD neutrophils do not produce H2O2 at all (or only in negligible amounts) because NADPH oxidase is non-functional. The key concept is not about residual H2O2 production by the neutrophil, but rather about bacterial-derived H2O2 that can serve as a substrate for MPO-mediated killing.
- **B)** Incorrect. Catalase-positive organisms can be opsonized normally, and CGD patients do not have complement deficiency. The defect in CGD is specifically in oxidative killing, not in opsonization or complement pathways.
- **C)** Correct. Catalase-negative bacteria (e.g., Streptococci) produce H2O2 as a metabolic byproduct and lack catalase to degrade it. CGD neutrophils can co-opt this bacterial-derived H2O2, using it with their intact MPO to generate hypochlorous acid for killing. Catalase-positive organisms (e.g., Staphylococcus, Aspergillus, Serratia) destroy their own H2O2, removing this backup killing mechanism entirely.
- **D)** Incorrect. Catalase-positive organisms are not inherently resistant to non-oxidative killing mechanisms (defensins, lysozyme, lactoferrin). The vulnerability in CGD is specifically about the loss of the oxidative killing pathway and the inability to co-opt bacterial-derived H2O2 from catalase-positive organisms.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. While non-oxidative mechanisms (defensins, lysozyme, lactoferrin) do exist, they are not significantly upregulated in MPO deficiency. The clinical silence of MPO deficiency is primarily explained by the intact NADPH oxidase system, which generates sufficient reactive oxygen species through MPO-independent pathways.
- **B)** Correct. In MPO deficiency, NADPH oxidase remains fully functional, so neutrophils still generate superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and other reactive oxygen species. These MPO-independent oxidative pathways (including direct toxicity of H2O2 and superoxide) provide sufficient microbicidal activity for most clinical situations, making MPO deficiency usually asymptomatic.
- **C)** Incorrect. MPO deficiency can be complete (homozygous), not always partial. Even patients with complete MPO deficiency are typically asymptomatic because NADPH oxidase-generated reactive oxygen species compensate for the loss of the MPO-halide system.
- **D)** Incorrect. Eosinophils and basophils do not compensate meaningfully for a neutrophil killing defect. Neutrophils are the primary phagocytic cells, and compensation comes from within the neutrophil itself -- specifically from its intact NADPH oxidase system and MPO-independent oxidative pathways.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Autoimmune neutropenia is possible but would not be the most likely explanation in an asymptomatic individual of Sub-Saharan African descent with an isolated, mildly low ANC and no history of recurrent infections. Autoimmune neutropenia is more commonly associated with other autoimmune conditions and may present with more severe neutropenia.
- **B)** Correct. The Duffy-null (ACKR1/DARC-negative) phenotype is extremely common in people of Sub-Saharan African descent (up to 70%) and is associated with benign ethnic neutropenia -- a lower circulating ANC baseline that does NOT confer increased infection risk. The Duffy antigen on red cells also plays a role in neutrophil margination, so its absence alters the distribution of neutrophils between the circulating and marginated pools.
- **C)** Incorrect. MDS is predominantly a disease of older adults (median age ~70) and is characterized by dysplastic morphologic changes, cytopenias, and often clonal cytogenetic or molecular abnormalities. An isolated mild neutropenia with normal smear morphology in a 35-year-old is far more consistent with benign ethnic neutropenia.
- **D)** Incorrect. Cyclic neutropenia (caused by ELANE mutations) presents with periodic nadirs every ~21 days, often accompanied by recurrent fevers, mouth ulcers, and infections at the nadir. A single low ANC reading in an asymptomatic patient is not suggestive of this diagnosis.

### Q8. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Febrile neutropenia (ANC <500 with fever >=38.3 C or sustained >=38.0 C) is an oncologic emergency. With an ANC of 80, the patient cannot mount a normal inflammatory response, so even minor infections can rapidly become fatal. Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam, or meropenem) must be started immediately, before culture results return.
- **B)** Incorrect. In the setting of severe neutropenia, fever must be assumed to represent life-threatening infection until proven otherwise. Withholding antibiotics pending cultures can be fatal, as these patients can progress to septic shock rapidly due to their inability to contain bacterial spread.
- **C)** Incorrect. Granulocyte transfusions are not first-line therapy and are reserved for rare, refractory cases of severe neutropenia with documented infections unresponsive to antibiotics. They are logistically challenging, carry significant risks, and are not standard initial management.
- **D)** Incorrect. While G-CSF can be used as an adjunct to shorten the duration of neutropenia, it takes days to raise the neutrophil count and does not replace the immediate need for empiric antibiotics. Antibiotics must be started first; G-CSF is a supportive measure, not a substitute.

### Q9. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Severe congenital neutropenia (Kostmann syndrome, caused by ELANE mutations) carries a 2-8% lifetime risk of transformation to MDS or AML. This risk requires ongoing monitoring with periodic bone marrow evaluations, particularly since many of these patients receive long-term G-CSF therapy, which may contribute to clonal evolution.
- **B)** Incorrect. Oculocutaneous albinism is a feature of Chediak-Higashi syndrome (LYST gene mutation), not Kostmann syndrome. Kostmann syndrome involves a defect in neutrophil production, not in granule or melanin trafficking.
- **C)** Incorrect. Recurrent episodes of neutropenia every 21 days describes cyclic neutropenia, which also involves ELANE mutations but presents with a different clinical pattern -- periodic nadirs rather than constant severe neutropenia. Importantly, cyclic neutropenia does NOT carry the same leukemia transformation risk as severe congenital neutropenia.
- **D)** Incorrect. Severe congenital neutropenia does not spontaneously resolve. Without treatment (G-CSF or stem cell transplant), these patients remain severely neutropenic and are at high risk for life-threatening infections throughout life.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Release of band forms from the marrow storage pool (left shift) does contribute to neutrophilia during infection, but it takes somewhat longer than demargination. The question asks about the fastest mechanism, occurring within minutes to hours -- demargination is the most immediate response.
- **B)** Correct. Approximately half of circulating neutrophils are marginated along vessel walls at any given time, forming the "marginated pool." Stress, catecholamines, and endotoxins cause rapid demargination, effectively doubling the circulating neutrophil count within minutes -- far faster than any mechanism requiring marrow release or new production.
- **C)** Incorrect. G-CSF-driven acceleration of myeloid progenitor differentiation takes days, not hours. While G-CSF levels rise during infection, this mechanism contributes to sustained neutrophilia over days, not the rapid initial rise seen within hours.
- **D)** Incorrect. Decreased apoptosis of circulating neutrophils does occur during infection (mediated by cytokines like G-CSF and GM-CSF) and contributes to prolonged neutrophilia, but this is a slower mechanism that does not explain the rapid initial rise within hours.

### Q11. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. CML presents with leukocytosis and left shift but is characterized by a low LAP score (because the alkaline phosphatase activity in leukemic neutrophils is reduced), basophilia, splenomegaly, and the presence of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene. This patient has a high LAP score, no basophilia, no splenomegaly, and an identifiable infection -- all pointing away from CML.
- **B)** Correct. A leukemoid reaction is a reactive leukocytosis (typically WBC >50,000) with a left shift, driven by infection or severe inflammation. Key features distinguishing it from CML include a high LAP score (reactive neutrophils are activated), an identifiable infectious or inflammatory cause, absence of basophilia, and absence of splenomegaly. There is no BCR::ABL1 fusion.
- **C)** Incorrect. AML typically presents with blasts (not mature granulocytes with a left shift), cytopenias from marrow failure, and would not show a high LAP score. Leukostasis in AML occurs with very high blast counts and presents with respiratory distress and neurologic symptoms, not an infectious picture with mature left-shifted cells.
- **D)** Incorrect. Myelofibrosis with extramedullary hematopoiesis presents with massive splenomegaly, teardrop cells, a leukoerythroblastic picture, and marrow fibrosis. This patient lacks splenomegaly and has a clear infectious cause for the leukocytosis.

### Q12. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. IL-2 is primarily a T-cell growth factor that drives T-cell proliferation and activation. It plays a central role in adaptive immunity but is not the primary regulator of eosinophil biology.
- **B)** Correct. IL-5 is the key cytokine governing eosinophil biology -- it stimulates eosinophil production in the bone marrow, activates mature eosinophils, and prolongs their survival by inhibiting apoptosis. This is clinically relevant: anti-IL-5 therapies such as mepolizumab and reslizumab are used to treat eosinophilic disorders including severe eosinophilic asthma and hypereosinophilic syndrome.
- **C)** Incorrect. G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) is the primary growth factor for neutrophil production, driving proliferation and differentiation of neutrophil precursors. It does not play a major role in eosinophil regulation.
- **D)** Incorrect. TNF-alpha is a pro-inflammatory cytokine produced by macrophages and other immune cells. While it contributes to inflammation broadly, it is not the primary regulator of eosinophil production, activation, or survival.
