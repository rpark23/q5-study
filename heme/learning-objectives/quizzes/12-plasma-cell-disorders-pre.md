## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Phagocytosis is a function of macrophages and neutrophils, not plasma cells. Plasma cells are terminally differentiated B-cells that have specialized in antibody secretion.
- **B)** Correct. Plasma cells are the antibody-producing cells of the adaptive immune system. Each plasma cell produces a specific immunoglobulin consisting of a heavy chain (G, A, M, E, or D) and a light chain (kappa or lambda). In a normal polyclonal state, diverse plasma cell clones produce a variety of antibodies.
- **C)** Incorrect. Antigen presentation to T-cells is the function of dendritic cells, macrophages, and B-cells (as antigen-presenting cells), not plasma cells. Plasma cells are terminally differentiated and focused on antibody secretion.
- **D)** Incorrect. Direct cytotoxic killing of virus-infected cells is the function of CD8+ cytotoxic T-lymphocytes and natural killer cells, not plasma cells.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A deficiency of all immunoglobulin classes describes hypogammaglobulinemia or agammaglobulinemia, not monoclonal gammopathy. Monoclonal gammopathy involves overproduction of a single immunoglobulin type, not deficiency.
- **B)** Correct. Monoclonal gammopathy refers to the state in which a single expanded clone of plasma cells produces identical antibodies, creating a detectable M-spike on serum protein electrophoresis. This is the hallmark of plasma cell neoplasms including MGUS, smoldering myeloma, and active multiple myeloma.
- **C)** Incorrect. Overproduction of polyclonal immunoglobulins from multiple clones describes a polyclonal gammopathy, which is a reactive process often seen in chronic infections, autoimmune diseases, and liver disease. It is not monoclonal gammopathy.
- **D)** Incorrect. An inherited deficiency of gamma globulins describes primary immunodeficiency syndromes such as X-linked agammaglobulinemia, not monoclonal gammopathy, which involves clonal overproduction of a single immunoglobulin.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A rate of 0.01% per year greatly underestimates the progression risk of MGUS. At this rate, progression would be exceedingly rare and monitoring would have little clinical value.
- **B)** Correct. MGUS progresses to a malignant plasma cell disorder (multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, or related conditions) at a rate of approximately 1% per year. This risk persists indefinitely, which is why lifelong monitoring is recommended.
- **C)** Incorrect. A 10% per year progression rate describes the early years of smoldering myeloma (years 1-5), not MGUS. MGUS has a much lower annual risk of transformation.
- **D)** Incorrect. A 25% annual rate would make MGUS a high-risk condition requiring early intervention, which does not match clinical reality. MGUS is managed with observation because of its low but persistent annual progression risk.

### Q4. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. MGUS is defined by an M-spike less than 3 g/dL, fewer than 10% clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow, and the absence of end-organ damage (no CRAB features or SLiM criteria). If either the M-spike is 3 g/dL or higher, or marrow plasma cells reach 10% or more (without end-organ damage), the diagnosis becomes smoldering myeloma.
- **B)** Incorrect. MGUS does not have bone lesions. The absence of end-organ damage, including bone lesions, is a defining requirement for the MGUS diagnosis. Bone lesions would indicate active myeloma.
- **C)** Incorrect. Hypercalcemia is a CRAB criterion indicating end-organ damage, which must be absent to diagnose MGUS. The presence of hypercalcemia from a plasma cell disorder would indicate active myeloma, not MGUS.
- **D)** Incorrect. While the presence of a detectable M-spike is necessary for MGUS, it is not sufficient. MGUS requires the M-spike to be below 3 g/dL and marrow plasma cells below 10%, with no related end-organ damage.

### Q5. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. CRAB stands for Calcium elevation (hypercalcemia), Renal failure (renal insufficiency), Anemia, and Bone lesions (lytic bone lesions). These represent the four traditional criteria for end-organ damage that define active multiple myeloma requiring treatment.
- **B)** Incorrect. Coagulopathy, Rash, Ascites, and Bleeding are not components of the CRAB criteria for myeloma. The CRAB acronym specifically refers to the four cardinal manifestations of myeloma-related organ damage.
- **C)** Incorrect. Cytopenia, Respiratory failure, Adenopathy, and B symptoms are not the CRAB criteria. While anemia (a cytopenia) is a CRAB feature, the other three are not part of this specific mnemonic.
- **D)** Incorrect. Complement activation, Renal tubular acidosis, Autoimmunity, and Bone marrow fibrosis do not correspond to the CRAB criteria. The correct definitions are hypercalcemia, renal failure, anemia, and bone lesions.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Identifying the specific heavy and light chain type of the monoclonal protein is the role of immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE), not SPEP. SPEP detects and quantifies the M-spike but does not determine its isotype.
- **B)** Correct. Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) separates serum proteins by size and charge, producing bands in the albumin, alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, and gamma regions. A monoclonal protein appears as a discrete M-spike in the gamma region, and SPEP quantifies the amount of this monoclonal protein.
- **C)** Incorrect. Measuring total serum immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM) is performed by quantitative immunoglobulin testing, not SPEP. While SPEP can suggest immunoparesis, it is not designed for this purpose.
- **D)** Incorrect. Detecting free light chains in the urine is the purpose of urine protein electrophoresis and immunofixation (UPIE), not serum protein electrophoresis. SPEP analyzes serum, not urine.

### Q7. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Myeloma cells do not directly digest bone through their own enzymatic activity. Bone destruction in myeloma is indirect, mediated by myeloma cell interactions with bone-remodeling cells in the marrow microenvironment.
- **B)** Correct. Myeloma cells interact with the bone marrow microenvironment to activate osteoclasts while simultaneously suppressing osteoblast function. This uncoupled bone remodeling produces purely lytic lesions, leading to pathologic fractures, bone pain, and hypercalcemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. Amyloid deposition can occur in some plasma cell disorders (AL amyloidosis) but is not the mechanism of typical myeloma lytic bone lesions. Lytic lesions result from osteoclast activation, not amyloid deposition.
- **D)** Incorrect. Myeloma cells do not secrete parathyroid hormone. PTH-related protein (PTHrP) secretion is the mechanism of humoral hypercalcemia in solid tumors, not myeloma. Myeloma causes bone destruction through local osteoclast activation.

### Q8. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. IgG is the most common immunoglobulin isotype produced in multiple myeloma, not Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia. WM is defined by the production of an IgM paraprotein.
- **B)** Incorrect. IgA is the second most common immunoglobulin isotype in multiple myeloma. It is not characteristic of Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia.
- **C)** Correct. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia is defined by the production of an IgM paraprotein. The "macro" in the name refers to the large pentameric IgM molecule, which can cause hyperviscosity syndrome at high concentrations. All patients with WM have an IgM paraprotein.
- **D)** Incorrect. IgE-producing plasma cell neoplasms are extremely rare. Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia specifically produces IgM, not IgE.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Surgery and radiation are not the primary treatment modalities for multiple myeloma, which is a systemic disease requiring systemic therapy. Radiation may play a limited palliative role for localized bone lesions but is not a phase of standard initial treatment.
- **B)** Correct. The three phases of initial myeloma therapy are: (1) induction chemotherapy to induce remission using combinations of steroids, immunomodulators, proteasome inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies; (2) consolidation with high-dose melphalan followed by autologous stem cell transplant to deepen the remission; and (3) maintenance therapy (typically lenalidomide) to sustain the remission as long as possible.
- **C)** Incorrect. Antibiotics, antifungals, and antivirals are supportive care measures that may be needed during myeloma treatment but do not constitute the three phases of myeloma therapy.
- **D)** Incorrect. Plasmapheresis, splenectomy, and immunosuppression are not the standard phases of myeloma treatment. Plasmapheresis may be used emergently for hyperviscosity (more relevant to WM), but the standard approach involves chemotherapy, transplant, and maintenance.

### Q10. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. CD20 is a B-cell marker targeted by rituximab in B-cell lymphomas and CLL, but it is not expressed on terminally differentiated plasma cells or myeloma cells. CAR-T therapy for myeloma does not target CD20.
- **B)** Incorrect. CD38 is highly expressed on myeloma cells and is the target of the monoclonal antibody daratumumab, a mainstay of myeloma therapy. However, CD38 is not the target of the currently approved CAR-T constructs for myeloma.
- **C)** Correct. BCMA (B-cell maturation antigen) is the target of the CAR-T therapies approved for relapsed/refractory myeloma, including idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel/Abecma) and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel/Carvykti). BCMA is highly expressed on myeloma cell surfaces and plays a role in plasma cell survival.
- **D)** Incorrect. CD19 is a pan-B-cell marker targeted by CAR-T therapy for B-ALL and certain B-cell lymphomas, but myeloma cells generally lose CD19 expression during plasma cell differentiation, making it an ineffective target for myeloma.
