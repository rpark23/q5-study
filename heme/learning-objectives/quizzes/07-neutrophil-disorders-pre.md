## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Gelatinase and CD11b/CD18 are found in tertiary (gelatinase) granules, not primary granules. Tertiary granules are involved in neutrophil adhesion and migration through tissue.
- **B)** Correct. Primary (azurophilic) granules are the first granules formed during neutrophil development and contain myeloperoxidase (MPO) and lysozyme as key contents. MPO catalyzes the formation of microbicidal hypochlorous acid from hydrogen peroxide and halide ions, while lysozyme degrades bacterial cell walls.
- **C)** Incorrect. Apolactoferrin and vitamin B12 binding protein are characteristic contents of specific (secondary) granules, not primary granules. Specific granules also contain lysozyme and collagenase.
- **D)** Incorrect. Histamine and heparin are contents of basophil granules, not neutrophil primary granules. Basophils and mast cells store these mediators for release during allergic and inflammatory responses.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Immunoglobulin production is the function of B-lymphocytes and plasma cells, not neutrophils. Neutrophils are part of the innate immune system and do not produce antibodies.
- **B)** Correct. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells and serve as the primary effectors of innate immunity. They migrate to sites of infection, phagocytize microorganisms, and kill them using both oxidative (respiratory burst) and non-oxidative mechanisms.
- **C)** Incorrect. Antigen presentation to naive T-cells is the primary function of dendritic cells and macrophages, not neutrophils. While neutrophils can interact with the adaptive immune system, antigen presentation is not their principal role.
- **D)** Incorrect. Histamine release during allergic reactions is the function of basophils and mast cells. Neutrophils do not contain significant stores of histamine in their granules.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Thirty minutes is too short even for the circulating half-life of neutrophils. Neutrophils remain in circulation for hours before entering tissues, where they survive for an additional 2-3 days.
- **B)** Correct. Neutrophils circulate in the peripheral blood with a half-life of approximately 6 hours. After leaving the circulation, they enter tissues where they survive for an additional 2-3 days performing their immune functions before undergoing apoptosis and being cleared by macrophages.
- **C)** Incorrect. Three days is closer to the total tissue lifespan of a neutrophil after it has already left the circulation. The circulating half-life in the blood itself is much shorter, approximately 6 hours.
- **D)** Incorrect. Two weeks is far too long for neutrophil survival. Unlike some lymphocytes that can persist for years, neutrophils are short-lived cells with a circulating half-life of about 6 hours and a total tissue survival of only 2-3 additional days.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The release of granule contents into the extracellular space is called degranulation or exocytosis. Opsonization is a process that occurs before phagocytosis, not after it, and involves marking pathogens for recognition.
- **B)** Correct. Opsonization is the process of coating pathogens with molecules such as immunoglobulins (whose Fc regions bind Fc receptors on neutrophils) or complement component C3b (which binds complement receptors on neutrophils). This coating greatly enhances the ability of neutrophils to recognize, bind, and phagocytize the pathogen.
- **C)** Incorrect. The formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) is a distinct antimicrobial process called NETosis, in which neutrophils release decondensed chromatin and granule proteins to trap and kill pathogens. It is separate from opsonization.
- **D)** Incorrect. The conversion of superoxide to hydrogen peroxide is a step in the respiratory burst pathway catalyzed by superoxide dismutase. This is part of the oxidative killing mechanism, not the opsonization process.

### Q5. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Mutations in the LYST gene cause Chediak-Higashi syndrome, which is characterized by defective vesicle trafficking, giant granules, and oculocutaneous albinism. CGD involves a different gene and mechanism.
- **B)** Incorrect. Deficiency of CD18 (beta-2 integrin) causes leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I (LAD I), not CGD. LAD I impairs neutrophil adhesion and transmigration, whereas CGD impairs intracellular killing.
- **C)** Correct. Chronic granulomatous disease is caused by mutations in one of the five genes encoding components of the NADPH oxidase complex. This enzyme is essential for generating superoxide during the respiratory burst. Without functional NADPH oxidase, neutrophils cannot produce the reactive oxygen species needed to kill ingested microorganisms, particularly catalase-positive bacteria and fungi.
- **D)** Incorrect. Defective post-translational processing of the MPO polypeptide causes myeloperoxidase deficiency, the most common inherited phagocyte disorder. MPO deficiency is usually clinically silent, unlike CGD which causes severe recurrent infections.

### Q6. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The total number of white blood cells per microliter is the WBC count, not the ANC. The ANC specifically represents the neutrophil component calculated from the WBC count multiplied by the percentage of neutrophils (segments plus bands).
- **B)** Correct. The ANC is calculated as WBC x (% segmented neutrophils + % band neutrophils). It quantifies the absolute number of neutrophils available for immune defense and is the key value used to assess infection risk in neutropenic patients.
- **C)** Incorrect. The ANC is a peripheral blood measurement, not a bone marrow ratio. It does not compare mature to immature neutrophils in the marrow but rather provides the total circulating neutrophil number.
- **D)** Incorrect. The ANC is a quantitative measurement derived from a standard CBC and differential, not a functional assessment by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry is used for other purposes such as diagnosing leukocyte adhesion deficiency or chronic granulomatous disease.

### Q7. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. An ANC less than 1,500/uL defines neutropenia in general but represents only mild neutropenia (1,000-1,500/uL). The threshold for severe neutropenia is lower than this.
- **B)** Incorrect. An ANC of 500-1,000/uL defines moderate neutropenia. While patients at this level have increased infection risk, it does not meet the threshold for severe neutropenia.
- **C)** Correct. Severe neutropenia is defined as an ANC less than 500/uL. At this level, patients are at significantly increased risk for life-threatening bacterial infections, and the risk escalates further as the ANC approaches zero.
- **D)** Incorrect. While an ANC less than 100/uL represents an extremely dangerous degree of neutropenia, the standard definition of severe neutropenia uses the threshold of less than 500/uL. Counts below 100/uL are sometimes called "profound" neutropenia.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Severe congenital neutropenia (e.g., Kostmann syndrome from HAX-1 mutations or severe congenital neutropenia from ELANE mutations) is a distinct condition from the Duffy-null phenotype. The Duffy-null phenotype does not cause severe neutropenia or increased infection risk.
- **B)** Correct. The Duffy-null phenotype results from SNPs in the DARC (Duffy Antigen Receptor for Chemokines) gene, leading to the Fy(a-b-) phenotype. It is common in people of Sub-Saharan African descent (80-100%) and the Arabian peninsula (50-70%). This variant causes a lower baseline circulating ANC but is NOT associated with increased infection risk. It is important to recognize this as a normal variant to avoid unnecessary workup.
- **C)** Incorrect. Cyclic fluctuations in the neutrophil count every 21 days describe cyclic neutropenia, which is caused by ELANE mutations. The Duffy-null phenotype causes a stable, mildly lower ANC baseline rather than periodic cycling.
- **D)** Incorrect. Giant azurophilic granules are the hallmark of Chediak-Higashi syndrome, caused by LYST gene mutations. The Duffy-null phenotype does not affect granule formation.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A decrease in the total WBC below normal is called leukopenia. A left shift refers to a qualitative change in the types of neutrophils present in the blood, not a quantitative decrease in total WBC.
- **B)** Correct. A "left shift" refers to the release of immature neutrophil precursors (bands, metamyelocytes, myelocytes, and occasionally promyelocytes) from the bone marrow into the peripheral blood. This typically occurs during infection or severe inflammation when demand for neutrophils exceeds the available supply of mature cells. The term derives from the historical practice of listing cell maturation stages from left (immature) to right (mature) on laboratory result forms.
- **C)** Incorrect. An increase in the proportion of lymphocytes relative to neutrophils is called a relative lymphocytosis. A left shift specifically refers to the presence of immature granulocyte precursors, not to changes in the lymphocyte-to-neutrophil ratio.
- **D)** Incorrect. A leftward shift in the hemoglobin-oxygen dissociation curve is a concept from respiratory physiology related to hemoglobin oxygen affinity. It is unrelated to the hematologic concept of a "left shift" in the WBC differential.

### Q10. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. A malignant proliferation of immature blast cells in the bone marrow describes acute leukemia (AML or ALL), not a leukemoid reaction. In a leukemoid reaction, the cells are predominantly mature neutrophils and their precursors, not blasts, and the process is reactive rather than neoplastic.
- **B)** Correct. A leukemoid reaction is a reactive, non-neoplastic leukocytosis with a WBC exceeding 50,000/uL, composed predominantly of mature neutrophils with a left shift (increased bands, metamyelocytes, and myelocytes). It is driven by severe infection, inflammation, or other stressors. Key distinguishing features from CML include a high leukocyte alkaline phosphatase (LAP) score, absence of basophilia, absence of splenomegaly, and an identifiable underlying cause.
- **C)** Incorrect. Autoimmune destruction of white blood cells causing severe leukopenia describes autoimmune neutropenia or other immune-mediated cytopenias. A leukemoid reaction is characterized by markedly elevated (not decreased) white blood cell counts.
- **D)** Incorrect. The release of red blood cell precursors into the peripheral blood due to bone marrow fibrosis describes a leukoerythroblastic picture, which is characteristic of myelofibrosis. A leukemoid reaction specifically involves an extreme elevation in mature neutrophils and their precursors.
