**Q1.** The body recycles approximately 20-25 mg of iron per day from senescent red blood cells, yet dietary absorption accounts for only 1-2 mg/day. What is the primary physiologic reason the body relies so heavily on recycling rather than absorption?

- B) Normal ferritin with low serum iron -- the two conditions cancel each other out
- C) A ferritin that is lower than expected for the degree of inflammation, potentially in the low-normal range, with a low serum iron

## Answer Key

### Q1. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. The gut epithelium does express transport proteins for iron absorption (such as DMT1 and ferroportin), and the capacity for absorption is not the primary limiting factor. The body deliberately limits absorption because it lacks an excretion mechanism, not because transport proteins are insufficient.
- **B)** Correct. The body has no regulated mechanism for actively excreting iron. Therefore, iron homeostasis is maintained almost entirely at the level of absorption, which is tightly controlled by hepcidin. Recycling from senescent red cells by macrophages (20-25 mg/day) provides the vast majority of iron needs, because unrestricted absorption would risk dangerous iron overload with no way to remove the excess.
- **C)** Incorrect. While dietary iron exists in different forms (heme and non-heme), the gut can convert ferric iron (Fe3+) to ferrous iron (Fe2+) via duodenal cytochrome B and absorb it through DMT1. The limited absorption is a regulated process, not a consequence of iron being in a non-absorbable form.
- **D)** Incorrect. While excess iron is indeed toxic (it generates reactive oxygen species via Fenton chemistry), the fundamental reason for limited absorption is the absence of an active excretion pathway. The body limits uptake because any iron absorbed is essentially permanent, not specifically because of hepatocyte toxicity.

### Q2. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Glossitis can occur in iron deficiency, but peripheral neuropathy is a hallmark of B12 deficiency, not iron deficiency. This combination points to a megaloblastic anemia rather than the microcytic, iron-deficient pattern described in the lab values.
- **B)** Correct. The lab pattern (low serum iron, high TIBC, low ferritin, low transferrin saturation) is classic for iron deficiency anemia. Koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails) and pica (craving non-food substances such as ice or dirt) are clinical findings specifically associated with iron deficiency, making this the most fitting answer for the given laboratory profile.
- **C)** Incorrect. Jaundice and splenomegaly suggest hemolytic anemia, in which red cells are being destroyed at an accelerated rate. Hemolysis would show elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin, and low haptoglobin -- a very different lab profile from iron deficiency.
- **D)** Incorrect. Petechiae and easy bruising are signs of thrombocytopenia (low platelets) or platelet dysfunction, not iron deficiency anemia. Iron deficiency primarily affects red blood cell production and would not typically cause these bleeding manifestations.

### Q3. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Autoantibodies against transferrin are not a recognized mechanism in anemia of inflammation. The pathophysiology is driven by hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration, not antibody-mediated destruction of iron transport proteins.
- **B)** Correct. In anemia of inflammation (also called anemia of chronic disease), IL-6 produced during chronic inflammation stimulates hepatic production of hepcidin. Hepcidin binds ferroportin on macrophages and enterocytes, causing its internalization and degradation. This traps recycled iron inside macrophages (reflected by elevated ferritin) and blocks dietary iron absorption, creating a functional iron deficiency despite adequate total body iron stores.
- **C)** Incorrect. While hepcidin does reduce iron absorption from the duodenum by targeting ferroportin on enterocytes, the primary mechanism of iron sequestration in anemia of inflammation is the trapping of iron within macrophages. Direct suppression of DMT1 expression by inflammatory cytokines is not the dominant pathway.
- **D)** Incorrect. Rheumatoid factor does not cross-react with transferrin receptors on erythroid precursors. This is not a recognized mechanism. The iron restriction in anemia of inflammation is mediated through the IL-6-hepcidin-ferroportin axis, not through autoantibody interference with erythroid iron uptake.

### Q4. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Serum iron is low in both iron deficiency anemia and anemia of inflammation, so it cannot differentiate between the two conditions. In iron deficiency, iron is depleted; in anemia of inflammation, iron is sequestered and unavailable. Either way, serum iron is reduced.
- **B)** Correct. Ferritin is the single most useful test for distinguishing iron deficiency anemia from anemia of inflammation. In iron deficiency, ferritin is low because it reflects truly depleted iron stores. In anemia of inflammation, ferritin is normal or elevated because iron is present in the body but sequestered inside macrophages by the hepcidin-ferroportin mechanism. Ferritin is also an acute phase reactant, further elevating it in inflammatory states.
- **C)** Incorrect. Hemoglobin tells you that anemia is present but provides no information about the cause. Both iron deficiency anemia and anemia of inflammation cause a reduced hemoglobin, so this test cannot distinguish between them.
- **D)** Incorrect. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) can be low in both conditions, particularly in chronic cases. Iron deficiency classically causes microcytosis, and anemia of inflammation can also produce microcytic or normocytic red cells. MCV alone is not reliable for distinguishing the two.

### Q5. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Elevated homocysteine is found in both B12 deficiency and folate deficiency, because both vitamins are involved in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine. Therefore, homocysteine cannot distinguish between the two deficiencies.
- **B)** Correct. Methylmalonic acid (MMA) is elevated only in B12 deficiency because B12 is a required cofactor for the enzyme methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, which converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA. Folate plays no role in this reaction, so MMA remains normal in isolated folate deficiency. This makes MMA the key distinguishing test.
- **C)** Incorrect. A low reticulocyte count can be seen in both B12 and folate deficiency, as both cause megaloblastic anemia with ineffective erythropoiesis. It does not help differentiate between the two conditions.
- **D)** Incorrect. Elevated LDH occurs in both B12 and folate deficiency as a marker of ineffective erythropoiesis and intramedullary hemolysis of megaloblastic precursors. It is not specific to either deficiency alone.

### Q6. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Body stores of folate last only approximately 3 weeks, making deficiency a rapid risk during the increased demands of pregnancy (the growing fetus requires folate for neural tube closure and rapid cell division). In contrast, hepatic B12 stores last 3-4 years, so B12 deficiency develops much more slowly even if intake is reduced.
- **B)** Incorrect. The fetus requires both folate and B12 for proper development. B12 is essential for normal neurologic development and DNA synthesis in the fetus. The difference in urgency is about maternal store depletion rates, not fetal requirements.
- **C)** Incorrect. The speed of absorption is not the relevant factor. The critical difference is how quickly stores become depleted: folate stores are exhausted in weeks, whereas B12 stores last years. This determines the urgency of supplementation timing.
- **D)** Incorrect. Gut bacteria do produce some B12, but this is synthesized too distally in the colon to be meaningfully absorbed (B12 absorption requires intrinsic factor and occurs in the terminal ileum). Humans rely on dietary B12 intake, not bacterial synthesis.

### Q7. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. Intrinsic factor is produced by parietal cells in the stomach, not in the duodenum. The duodenum is the site of iron absorption, not intrinsic factor secretion. Confusing the site of IF production with the site of nutrient absorption is a common error.
- **B)** Incorrect. B12-intrinsic factor complexes are absorbed in the terminal ileum, not the jejunum. The terminal ileum contains cubilin/amnionless receptors that specifically bind and internalize the B12-IF complex. However, the primary defect in pernicious anemia is not at the absorption site.
- **C)** Correct. Pernicious anemia is an autoimmune condition in which antibodies (anti-parietal cell and anti-intrinsic factor antibodies) destroy gastric parietal cells. Since parietal cells produce intrinsic factor, their destruction eliminates IF production. Without intrinsic factor, B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum. The primary defect is in the stomach.
- **D)** Incorrect. While B12-IF complexes are indeed absorbed in the terminal ileum, the autoantibodies in pernicious anemia target parietal cells and intrinsic factor itself, not receptors in the terminal ileum. The ileal cubam receptor is intact; the problem is that B12 never arrives there complexed with IF.

### Q8. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Folate supplementation does not cause B12 levels to drop through competitive inhibition. B12 and folate have separate absorption and metabolic pathways. The danger is not a pharmacologic interaction but rather a masking effect on hematologic findings.
- **B)** Correct. Folate can correct the megaloblastic anemia caused by B12 deficiency because both vitamins are involved in DNA synthesis (folate through the "methyl-folate trap" depends on B12 for recycling). High-dose folate can normalize the CBC, masking the B12 deficiency. However, folate does not address the neurologic damage of B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord), which continues to progress silently. This is why B12 must always be checked before treating macrocytic anemia with folate alone.
- **C)** Incorrect. Folate supplementation does not cause an acute hemolytic crisis in the setting of B12 deficiency. This is not a recognized adverse effect. The concern is the insidious masking of hematologic abnormalities while neurologic damage progresses.
- **D)** Incorrect. Folate and B12 together are not toxic to the nervous system. In fact, both are essential for normal neurologic function. The danger is specifically that folate alone masks the anemia of B12 deficiency, allowing irreversible neurologic damage to occur undetected.

### Q9. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Oxidative damage to red cell membranes is more characteristic of G6PD deficiency or conditions with reactive oxygen species. Iron deficiency causes red cells to be smaller due to insufficient hemoglobin, not damaged due to oxidative injury to the membrane.
- **B)** Correct. Red cell distribution width (RDW) measures the variability in red cell size (anisocytosis). In iron deficiency, as stores become progressively depleted, newly produced red cells are smaller (microcytic) because there is insufficient iron for normal hemoglobin synthesis. Meanwhile, older red cells produced before iron stores were depleted remain normal-sized. This coexistence of two populations -- normocytic older cells and microcytic newer cells -- creates increased size variation, elevating the RDW.
- **C)** Incorrect. Extramedullary hematopoiesis (production of blood cells outside the bone marrow, typically in the spleen or liver) is associated with severe marrow failure states or myelofibrosis, not with simple iron deficiency anemia.
- **D)** Incorrect. While iron deficiency does cause some degree of ineffective erythropoiesis, the release of cell fragments from apoptotic precursors is not the primary mechanism for elevated RDW. The elevated RDW reflects a dimorphic red cell population, not fragmented cells.

### Q10. Answer: A

- **A)** Correct. Iron is primarily absorbed in the duodenum via DMT1 in the ferrous (Fe2+) form. Gastric acid is essential for converting dietary ferric iron (Fe3+) to the absorbable ferrous form, and ascorbic acid in the stomach further facilitates this reduction. Roux-en-Y gastric bypass both reduces acid production (smaller gastric pouch) and anatomically bypasses the duodenum, impairing both the reduction and absorption steps of iron uptake. This explains the poor response to oral iron.
- **B)** Incorrect. Blind loop syndrome with bacterial overgrowth can occur after certain GI surgeries and may impair absorption of nutrients, but this is more classically associated with B12 deficiency (bacteria consume B12). It is not the primary mechanism of iron malabsorption after Roux-en-Y bypass.
- **C)** Incorrect. Intrinsic factor is relevant to B12 absorption, not iron absorption. While Roux-en-Y can also affect B12 status, the question specifically asks about iron deficiency, and intrinsic factor plays no role in iron uptake.
- **D)** Incorrect. While GI transit time can be altered after surgery, ferroportin exports iron from the basolateral side of enterocytes into the bloodstream and is not directly affected by transit time. The primary problem is bypassing the duodenum and reducing acid-mediated iron conversion upstream.

### Q11. Answer: B

- **A)** Incorrect. Hemolytic anemia causes macrocytosis due to reticulocytosis (reticulocytes are larger than mature red cells), but hemolysis does not cause hypersegmented neutrophils. Hypersegmented neutrophils are specifically a sign of impaired DNA synthesis, not hemolysis.
- **B)** Correct. Drugs that impair DNA synthesis -- such as methotrexate (a folate antagonist), hydroxyurea, 5-fluorouracil, and certain other chemotherapeutic agents -- can cause megaloblastic changes identical to B12/folate deficiency, including macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils, even when vitamin levels are normal. This is a critical consideration in the differential diagnosis of megaloblastic anemia.
- **C)** Incorrect. Hypersegmented neutrophils are a highly specific finding for impaired DNA synthesis (megaloblastic change) and should never be dismissed as nonspecific. Their presence always warrants investigation into the cause, whether nutritional or drug-related.
- **D)** Incorrect. Iron deficiency causes microcytic anemia, not macrocytic anemia. Iron deficiency does not produce hypersegmented neutrophils. Testing for iron deficiency would not explain the macrocytosis and megaloblastic changes seen on the smear.

### Q12. Answer: C

- **A)** Incorrect. High ferritin and low TIBC is the pattern of pure anemia of inflammation, where iron is sequestered by hepcidin-mediated ferroportin degradation. This pattern would not be expected if the patient also has true iron deficiency, which would pull ferritin downward and TIBC upward.
- **B)** Incorrect. It is an oversimplification to say the two conditions "cancel each other out" to produce normal values. The ferritin does not simply normalize; rather, it is lower than expected for the degree of inflammation but may not reach the frankly low levels seen in pure iron deficiency.
- **C)** Correct. When iron deficiency and inflammation coexist, ferritin is pulled in opposite directions: inflammation elevates it as an acute phase reactant, while true iron depletion lowers it. The result is typically a ferritin that is lower than expected for the degree of inflammation -- possibly in the low-normal range rather than frankly low or markedly elevated. Serum iron is low from both processes. This overlap makes diagnosis challenging and often requires additional tests such as soluble transferrin receptor or reticulocyte hemoglobin content.
- **D)** Incorrect. Markedly elevated ferritin with high TIBC is an internally contradictory pattern. Inflammation raises ferritin and lowers TIBC, while iron deficiency lowers ferritin and raises TIBC. Both pathways being "maximally stimulated" would not produce this combination; they would instead create an intermediate, mixed picture.
